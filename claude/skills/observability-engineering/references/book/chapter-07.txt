OpenTelemetry promises a unified standard for telemetry. But, for the uninitiated,
it can sometimes feel a bit overwhelming in its complexity. There’s a dizzying array
of terminology, a broad application programming interface (API) and SDK surface
area, and a learning curve sometimes described as more of a “learning cliff.” Should
you use zero-code or library instrumentation? Which signals should you emit? Is
switching to OTel even worth the effort?
This chapter is not an in-depth guide for everything you need to know about OTel.
For that, we recommend reading Learning OpenTelemetry by Ted Young and Austin
Parker (O’Reilly, 2024). Here, we’ll focus on making OTel less intimidating and more
practical to approach. Think of this as your set of ropes, harnesses, belay devices,
helmets, and situational awareness training so that you won’t fall off of that learning
cliff.
In this chapter, we’ll cover fundamental design philosophies to help you understand
why and how OTel is built as the project you see today and how to gauge when it
is (or isn’t) a good fit. We’ll also guide you through understanding trace-first design
principles in complex architectures you’re likely to encounter. From there, we’ll
dive into trade-offs behind its various signal types and how to use them effectively.
Lastly, we’ll also show you how AI-assisted coding helps with OTel instrumentation,
especially when migrating from other existing frameworks.

What It Means to Use OpenTelemetry
In Chapter 4, we introduced the city-planning analogy, where instrumentation acts as
the sensors and meters that provide system insight. Now, imagine you’re responsible
for building and integrating those very sensors into your analytics pipeline.
You might find that some data is semantically linked (for example, traffic sensors
that count the number of vehicles at an intersection and the status of the traffic
lights themselves), yet it arrives in different formats, with different field names, and
with different ideas about units and meaning. You’d need to spend time transforming
signals into a normalized shape, documenting the changes, and then repeating the
cycle whenever a sensor changes, a vendor sunsets a product, or a “compatible
replacement” emits slightly different data.
If a sensor failed and the company that made it was out of business, or a new version
emitted different data, you’d need to rebuild your pipeline in order to maintain your
analytical integrity. Even if you manage to stitch it all together, you still end up with
a firehose of partially duplicated information, and you’d be on the hook for making it
coherent and usable.
OTel exists to reduce that burden. It provides an open standard for how telemetry is
created, transmitted, and interpreted. It also provides semantic conventions (shared
meaning for names and metadata) so you can swap components without rebuilding
your entire pipeline. In practice, that means less vendor lock-in, fewer painful migra‐
tions, and cleaner cost controls because you’re collecting what you mean to collect.
In our analogy, it would guarantee that you don’t have to worry about changing out
sensors or updating your analysis tools; the same data will be emitted and will work
in the same ways even with different components.
The project’s ambitions extend beyond providing a common format. The goal is for
telemetry to be native to the libraries, frameworks, runtimes, and infrastructure you
already use; rather than being a bolt-on afterthought. When a rich braid of unified
telemetry already exists, it enables a world where engineers can easily add business-
specific context as needed, where telemetry and observability are transferable skills,
and where observability is the default state of high-quality software.
In practice, that means your software system emits the right signals at the right level
of abstraction, without bespoke configurations or transformations.
What does it mean for your team to use OTel? Like all good software questions,
the answer is “it depends.” This chapter is written for developers and site reliability
engineers who are instrumenting applications and services (backend, frontend, full
stack) and who want guidance on patterns, boundaries, and practical techniques.

Effective Instrumentation With OpenTelemetry
You can start using OTel without writing any new code. There’s a bevy of zero-code
instrumentation libraries, agents, and other solutions that will generate metrics, logs,
and traces from the most popular libraries in every language. The hard part isn’t
generating telemetry; it’s generating useful telemetry.
Effective instrumentation is a bit like sculpting: you’re deciding what to keep and
what to throw away. In this section, we’ll focus on boundaries, span and attribute
naming, and how to choose the right telemetry signals to support the questions you
actually need to answer.

Trace-First Telemetry
The biggest difference between OTel and many older instrumentation approaches is
context. “Context” is an overloaded term—it can refer to the state of what’s happening
inside a system, the data structure used to encode and propagate unique identifiers
between functions and processes, or the specific values stored inside that structure.
We’ll demystify “context” by examining how OTel designs instrumentation. OTel is
described as “trace-first” because context is the glue that makes the rest of the model
work. If you emit traditional metrics and logs without context you can still get value,
but you miss the main benefit. In OTel, all telemetry signals can be associated back to
a specific request, transaction, or unit of work as long as an active context exists.
Being trace-first is important because context propagation is how that identity moves
through your system. There are two types:
Interprocess propagation
     The identity crosses process boundaries, often via HTTP headers using W3C
     TraceContext that include trace ID, parent span ID, plus optional baggage (arbi‐
     trary key-value pairs) set earlier in a transaction.
Intraprocess propagation
     The identity flows through methods, threads, coroutines, or components inside a
     process boundary.

HTTP is a common carrier for interprocess propagation, but it’s not the only one.
Context can travel through a variety of methods, including message queue envelopes,
environment variables, gRPC or other remote procedure call (RPC) framework head‐
ers, etc.—anywhere you can attach metadata reliably.
In languages like Java, or others with threads and strong concurrency primitives,
intraprocess propagation is often as simple as establishing a scope (e.g., wrapping a
method or block in a try, so you can easily capture failures) and then creating spans
within it. Any metrics or logs emitted inside that scope inherit the active context as
well.
Helpfully, low-level management of context like this is probably something you won’t
need to do, especially if you use automatic instrumentation.
At this point, a common thought often occurs: “If context is flowing, should I create a
span for every function?” In most systems, that’s the wrong instinct.
Another important part of OpenTelemetry’s design is that telemetry should be lay‐
ered; meaning that you should use the right signal for the right purpose. For instance,
spans should model meaningful units of work: discrete operations that matter to
performance and reliability.

There’s an art to creating a good trace. In practice, it’s all too common to err on the
side of too many or too few spans. Too many spans makes analysis expensive and
unhelpful (a trace with millions of spans is far too detailed and rarely actionable). Too
few spans often means collapsing minutes (or hours) of work into a single opaque
handler that still leaves you guessing at optimizations or effective ways to monitor
and alert.




                                                                   Effective Instrumentation With OpenTelemetry        |   117
You’ll also notice a pattern that seems adjacent to Conway’s law:1 traces tend to mirror
organizational boundaries (e.g., a group of developers or the microservices they
manage). Resist that urge. Hold preference for spans that map to true component and
service domain boundaries—even if that means crossing team lines—because that’s
what makes traces useful during incidents.
If your trace has a hundred two-millisecond spans, you probably have too many. Roll
them up: combine them into a single span, or represent the details as an attribute. For
fine-grained performance questions, profiling (see Chapter 20) is often a better tool
for merging function executions into useful data.


                        The Perils of Automatic Instrumentation
  Auto-instrumentation often errs on the side of producing too many unactionable
  spans, metrics, or logs in the pursuit of thoroughness across signal types. This isn’t
  malicious; it’s a reasonable default in a general-purpose library. You are responsible
  for making defaults sane within your environment.
  Most of the time, you should prefer not duplicating telemetry unless you really need
  to. A common pattern is to keep traces sampled. It’s expected that users will have
  different collection rules or sampling strategies across signals.
  It’s very useful to emit histograms for accurate and high-resolution counts of rate,
  error, and duration (RED) metrics from HTTP or gRPC endpoints in addition to
  trace data, especially for high-throughput services. Histograms can be stored cheaply,
  even if you have to rely on head sampling to reduce overall trace throughput.
  If you’re drowning in noise, turn it off: drop or sample out specific instrumentation,
  or replace it with custom spans and processors that summarize small work into
  attributes on a parent span.


Traces for Common Architectural Patterns
Most distributed tracing models (and OTel in particular) fit request/response archi‐
tectures well: a client sends a request, the service does work, it calls dependencies,
and the entire transaction can be visualized into the common “waterfall” trace
representation.
The further your system architecture drifts from the request/response pattern, the
more challenging it becomes to design your traces. Let’s walk through a few patterns.

Tracing streaming architectures
Second to the request/response pattern, one of the most popular and widely used
system architectures is stream processing, where data flows across queues, buses,
and topics. It’s used for everything from extract-transform-load (ETL) jobs such as
processing of financial records or inventory management, to event-driven business
workflows, to planet-scale aggregation for social media applications, and more.
Tracing stream-processing models is tricky for two reasons:

 1. The smallest “unit of work” you can instrument isn’t always the most useful unit
    of work to optimize.
 2. The things you’d like to alert or measure in these pipelines often have more to do
    with the trace shape itself rather than the spans that comprise the trace.

Specifically, developers often want to measure pipeline metadata like stage dwell
times (“How long are consumers waiting for new messages and what do those
messages have in common?”), or the amount or ratio of successful-to-failed stages
(common for developers implementing step functions or finite state machines atop of
queues).
Some architectures, like event sourcing, present additional challenges. For example,
when every logical action in a pipeline is a mutation or series of mutations against a
golden event, what exactly is considered the root span, and when does that occur?
OTel can still help, but you’ll usually need a blend of signals and a little design. Don’t
try to answer everything with spans alone. For example, let’s say you have a workflow
that looks like this:
    orders-api (produces OrderPlaced)
    → topic: orders.events
      ├ payment-svc (consumes OrderPlaced, produces PaymentAuthorized/Declined)
      ├ inventory-svc (consumes PaymentAuthorized, produces ItemReserved/Backorder)
      ├ shipping-svc (consumes ItemReserved, produces ShipmentCreated)
      └ reconcile-job (nightly batch over events store)
The first requirement is proper context propagation: serialize context into the mes‐
sage envelope when you publish, and then extract it when you consume. For Apache
Kafka and similar queue or message bus systems, message envelopes make this
straightforward.
The orders-api service emits a trace span like messaging.publish with attributes
such as system, destination topic, message ID, and relevant business details (transac‐
tion ID, customer ID, etc.). That span is marked as a “producer” span (as opposed to
“client”), and its context is serialized into the message envelope of the OrderPlaced
event and then written to the topic.



                                               Effective Instrumentation With OpenTelemetry   |   119
As consumers read those messages, they extract the context from the envelope and
create corresponding messaging.process spans. Those spans are linked to the parent
via “span links,”2 but not as direct children. When the consumers write new messages
back, they would repeat the process; a producer span for the new message type, and
so forth.
A useful approach is to store and hash a unique identifier for each transaction and
propagate that through the entire pipeline. That propagation ties all of these traces
together directly, rather than having to walk the tree in reverse. Span links can also
help you relate these events together across multiple traces.
Conceptually, this shifts from creating one trace with a single root and many children
into a timeline of traces that occur at different times. Because each logical transaction
carries a unique correlation ID, you can easily pull all relevant spans into a result set,
and then visualize them as needed.
That view can provide you with the relevant shape of your pipeline and the ability
to understand the performance of each stage, but it has trade-offs. While providing
excellent visibility into a single message’s journey, it can be tricky to generalize that
approach for investigations at the fleet level.
In that instance, OpenTelemetry’s metric exemplars can help. An exemplar is an
annotation on a particular metric point that associates it with a specific trace and
span. While your spans and traces provide deep insight into how each specific stage
or consumer is performing, metrics can provide top-level insight into the pipeline as
a whole—and they’re also where you should focus your frontline alerting.
Your pipeline metrics look more like counters emitted by each stage showing success
or failure (with attributes mirroring your span data, but at a lower cardinality—so
you don’t necessarily want to put the transaction ID on each measurement, but you
should add the consumer name). Emit a metric for successful and failed jobs, and
then calculate and alert on the ratio through failed_total, processed_total per
stage, type, region, etc. That lets you proactively alert on unusual Kafka behavior by
creating histograms that track the time from publish to process, and use exemplars to
attach your outlier datapoints to specific consumer traces.
If you’re working with step functions and are concerned about the number of stages
per transaction, that’s another good use case for histograms. Record the number of
stages at the terminal stage and, optionally, use baggage to pass down the number

Asynchronous work and jobs
For fan-out/fan-in or scatter-gather work patterns, the important decision is less
about span topology and more about how the request is presented to the user (or
caller). Let’s suppose you have a video-processing pipeline that accepts uploaded
content and then renders it into a consistent format for storage and presentation:
    POST /render → orchestrator
      ├ publish task.transcode
      ├ publish task.thumbnail
      └─ publish task.caption
    workers process → orchestrator joins when N succeeds or deadlines

The root span for these cases is the POST. You can choose to use parent–child or
producer–consumer relationships, depending on how you’re electing to monitor the
application and the expected results. Either way, the root span should contain links to
each child process, and have a final summarization span that rolls up the results.
Alerting often belongs on the entry point (POST) or on specific child tasks, depend‐
ing on service architecture or ownership (Conway’s law really does rule everything
around us). In this case, metrics may be less useful than pure spans because there’s no
shared infrastructure that you can optimize other than network links.
Next, let’s consider something similar but with a much longer time horizon, such as
nightly jobs. Suppose you have a nightly workflow that compiles inventory reports
from thousands of suppliers, all of whom have subtle differences in their data format‐
ting. Extracting and transforming that data can take hours (and in some cases, days)
before it’s loaded into a business intelligence database. A logical representation of the
work could look like the following:
    scheduler → [job.run nightly_etl] (ROOT)
                  ├ etl.extract
                  ├ etl.transform (hours)
                  ├ etl.load
                  └ report.write
In this scenario, you should avoid the temptation to turn the transform steps into
direct child spans. Long-running jobs introduce the “million children trace” hazard.
Instead, as recommended earlier, model each stage as its own trace and relate the
stages with links and correlation identifiers. One challenge with that approach is that



                                               Effective Instrumentation With OpenTelemetry   |   121
your parent span may not necessarily complete until all children are complete. There
are a few strategies to solve this problem, but they have trade-offs.
One option is to follow the disjointed ETL process elucidated earlier. You won’t have
a single root span for the job. Instead, you have a producer trace that’s linked to
your child traces. The downside is that it can be challenging to correlate all of your
child traces together. Alternatively, you can have a root span that you’re building up
in-memory (or in a file), adding heartbeat events or status events as stages complete,
which get closed and written out at finish.
The “Occam’s razor” solution to this problem is to periodically flush the root span
with updated timestamps as the child jobs progress. But that approach is not recom‐
mended, as the vast majority of tracing datastores don’t handle updates to existing
spans. In most cases, they are either rejected or cause undefined behavior.
In these situations, metrics or logs can also help by providing intermediate status
reports that you can monitor without breaking the span and trace data model. As
long as you’re preserving trace context through your child–consumer processes and
calls, you can associate metrics back to their underlying traces.
When deciding which trade-offs to favor, it can be useful to ask yourself and your
team what your exact concerns are, and then use the answer to guide your thinking.
Are you checking a box for requirements, or are you trying to understand and
optimize the individual performance of these jobs?
If your primary goal is a safety net rather than fine-grained performance optimiza‐
tion, consider a “wide event” approach: a single summarization span (or log event)
with hundreds or thousands of attributes that contain a significant amount of meta‐
data about the job that gets written at completion, with rich metadata about what
succeeded, what failed, and why. This is useful for tracking changes over time without
dealing with the overhead of creating a well-formed discrete trace.

Serverless
Most of the guidance thus far applies to serverless, but the constraints change the
trade-offs. There are also a few specific tips and considerations specifically when
running in AWS Lambda.
First, you should probably favor using custom instrumentation for your serverless
functions rather than relying on automatic instrumentation. While OTel instrumen‐
tation libraries for serverless exist, the resource constraints of serverless environ‐
ments mean it’s ideal to limit the spans you create in your functions. A single
well-designed span per invocation, rich with attributes capturing the details of a few
key events, often beats a sprawling hierarchy that burns cold-start time and memory.

use stateless Lambda tracing. In short, emit lightweight events from the function and
let a stateful OpenTelemetry Collector (with custom processors) build a map that

statefulness in libraries and context management in the serverless function runtime,
at the cost of more complex Collector-side logic you’ll need to implement. If perfor‐

type of extension layer necessary to accomplish this will be provided natively by more

use telemetry pipelines or transforms to route the data to the same place as your
other telemetry; use a query layer that allows for federated queries across multiple
datastores; or batch, join, normalize, and forward these events together. As OTel
continues to mature and gain wider adoption, we hope that these platforms will offer
native OTel solutions for their users.

Extended Berkeley Packet Filters
So far, this chapter has assumed that it’s possible for you to modify your code in
order to perform instrumentation. The reality in some organizations is that doing so
often requires a level of cooperation between observability engineers and software
development teams that is either slow, infeasible, or flat out impossible. In these cases,
extended Berkeley Packet Filter (eBPF) technology can help.




                                               Effective Instrumentation With OpenTelemetry   |   123
eBPF programs run in the Linux kernel itself,3 outside of user space, and enable
observability at a different level of abstraction than previously covered techniques.
eBPF is unique in that its programs are designed to be small, self-contained, low
overhead, and very safe—they cannot corrupt memory or crash. Because they run
outside of a process, they incur no additional runtime overhead and they can be
enabled or disabled without requiring a restart of the process, or recompilation.
OTel supports eBPF instrumentation through the OpenTelemetry eBPF Instrumenta‐
tion (OBI) package. OBI gathers metrics and traces from HTTP/HTTPS and gRPC
services written in a variety of languages and allows for propagating trace context
through services. It’s best understood as an accelerator and a gap filler that can
enhance existing instrumentation efforts, rather than something that replaces them
entirely.
For instance, OBI allows you to capture kernel-level detail about network operations
that would not be available to your applications. It allows you to propagate traces
through legacy services where you would otherwise lose visibility. A full discussion
of its capabilities, configuration, and limitations is beyond the scope of this book, but
we’ll touch on a few other key capabilities.
It carries some operational concerns, aside from the aforementioned limit on gener‐
ated telemetry. Potential conflicts may exist with other eBPF programs and you must
configure them carefully to avoid this. They may also need elevated permissions
depending on the deployment. If you aren’t running OBI as an administrative user
(a.k.a. root), then you’ll need to enable specific permissions for the process.
Currently, OBI and other eBPF-based instrumentation solutions offer real value,
and future work in this area promises to unlock more. Perhaps we’ll see channels
for extracting state from requests, allowing for custom attributes to be added to
span data or metrics. That could narrow the gap between “observing execution” and
“understanding intent.” For now, you should think of OBI and related tools as a way
to quickly generate tracing data for uninstrumented systems (e.g., to populate service
maps), extend traces through uninstrumented services, or generate instrumentation
as a part of debugging kernel-level behavior.
So far, we’ve covered options when instrumenting and strategies for designing traces
for common architectural patterns that go beyond simple request/response. Quality
traces are foundational when using OpenTelemetry because they connect everything
else. If you don’t have good traces, there’s a lot of value you won’t get from your
metrics or logs either.

Metrics, Spans, Logs, Events—Oh My?
Layered telemetry is the practice of using the right signal at the right level of detail for
the outcome you need. OTel makes this easier because shared context can correlate
signals back to the same request. We’ll examine OpenTelemetry’s design and seman‐
tics so you can choose the right mix of signals when observing your system.
As mentioned previously, OTel is trace-first, given its ability to correlate signals
through a shared distributed context layer. In practical terms, trace-first correlation
unlocks three benefits:

 1. Developers can layer signals that provide multiple “views” of the same work
    without regard for any downstream sampling or ingestion decisions.
 2. Operators can balance accuracy, visibility, and cost in observability pipelines for
    operational efficiency.
 3. Telemetry consumers can drill down from a high-level symptom to the telemetry
    signal that explains what’s happening in production, and how it relates to their
    goals.

OTel instrumentation should embrace a multisignal approach, but it’s more nuanced
than simply duplicating data. A useful three-question test for choosing signals is:

 1. What needs causality and full-request context for consumers? (traces)
 2. What needs inexpensive long-term storage and/or fast alerting? (metrics)
 3. What is rare versus common, and what are the audit requirements? (logs or
    events)

Sometimes you’ll layer two signals intentionally. For example, a common pattern
for HTTP servers is to emit both a span and a histogram metric for each handled
request. That gives consumers flexibility during investigations. If your HTTP service
has a particularly high throughput, you could head-sample your traces. Histograms
would provide last-ditch alerting for errors and duration outliers, and potentially
reveal deeper correlation into unsampled spans. Business-critical or sensitive func‐
tions—such as billing events or service level agreement (SLA) conformance data—
benefit from having isolated, event-only pipelines, even if your primary observability
signals for those transactions are span-based.
The technique is called layering (instead of duplication) because you’re providing
multiple views depending on the needs of consumers. A suitable analogy would be
using a microscope: the closer you zoom in, the more detail you see. The job of your


                                                Effective Instrumentation With OpenTelemetry   |   125
instrumentation is to provide the right level of detail at the right level of abstraction
to reveal the necessary view of system performance.
In practice, that means starting with traces at your service boundaries. Think of these
traces as the glue between all of your other signals. Next, you can layer on aggregated
metrics at that same boundary, but with fewer dimensions and attributes. The key is
in realizing those metrics don’t need to be separate instrumentations in your code.
You can use the OpenTelemetry Collector or other observability pipelines in order to
generate metrics directly from the span data (see Chapter 5).

                    Sampling and Span Metrics
                    It’s worth mentioning that generating accurate metrics from spans
                    is difficult (or impossible) if you apply head sampling! There is
                    work in the OpenTelemetry project to add support for probabilistic
                    head sampling, allowing for more accurate calculation of metrics
                    from head-sampled data, but it’s currently experimental.

Once you know what to emit, you need to name it. Names should be descriptive,
unambiguous, and easy to group by. Don’t put identifiers or variable route compo‐
nents in names. Instead, identifiers should be replaced with a description, and the
variable component moved to an attribute value. For example, GET /users/profile/
349827487124 becomes GET /users/profile/{user.id}.
In general, names for spans (or logs) should follow dot notation with snake case
separators (like_this) for multiword phrases. Consult the OpenTelemetry semantic
conventions for many examples. Similarly, metrics should have consistent names
across services. In OTel, you should prefer metadata (such as the type information)
on a metric to communicate what it is, rather than encoding it into a name. For
example, http.server.request.duration_ms should be http.server.request.dura
tion.

                    Schema-Driven Telemetry
                    An emerging practice is to define your telemetry conventions in
                    a schema file, similar to how semantic conventions are structured,
                    and use tools like OpenTelemetry Weaver to validate the emitted
                    telemetry at compile and runtime. Weaver allows you to generate
                    code as well, creating helpers for implementing the signals defined
                    in the schema.

Most teams adopt OTel in brownfield systems. This means that migrating your
instrumentation can present an opportunity to use schemas as an artifact and source
of truth, which builds shared agreement on what different attributes mean.

Sample your existing telemetry data and normalize it into a new, OTel-flavored,
harmonized format. Create a namespace for your organization, and use it to collect
org-wide concepts: service owners, business units, cost centers. Bake them into a
schema so teams stop reinventing field names. Check these schemas into source
control, and use them to aid your migration and to help your AI-assisted coding
efforts (for a more in-depth look, see Chapter 16).
Consistency and documentation is not optional. We’ve seen telemetry environments
with dozens of conflicting attribute keys for the same logical value, even within a
single signal type. That duplication and confusion is costly for everyone in the middle
of an incident: it wastes valuable time and significantly lengthens investigation. That
becomes worse when AI is introduced because models can’t reliably infer which
field is canonical, and they’ll often confidently build incorrect conclusions using the
wrong data.
Be thorough with your telemetry data. Include units as attributes on your signals, and
avoid mixing them on the same key. Normalize groupable keys like name and route,
or other entry points into your system. Practice defense in depth (DiD) for user data:
hash potentially sensitive values, use SDK- and Collector-level processors to filter or
use allowlist attribute keys and values, and leverage transform processors to filter out
common personally identifiable information (PII) like identity numbers. Preference
stable hashes for potentially sensitive data—like user identifiers—and ensure that you
can reverse them through an internal API, preferably something that can be scripted.
Now that we’ve covered what to emit and how to shape it, there’s still the practi‐
cal part: actually coding all of that instrumentation. Historically, that’s been time-
consuming human labor. Today, AI can help perform much of the grunt work—if you
use it deliberately.

Using AI Agents to Instrument Your Code
Before we start, we’ll again acknowledge that we understand the perils of committing
to writing about AI techniques in a textbook. As of this writing, several AI agents
such as Claude Code, Cursor, Gemini CLI, and dozens of others are widely in use. By
the time you read this book in print, it’s entirely possible they’ve become obsolete in
favor of some new technique.
However, we believe there’s general guidance that will continue to hold true when
coding OTel instrumentation into your applications, even in the era of AI agents. Let’s
examine where they shine and what they shouldn’t handle.

What Is an Agent, Anyway?
Agents, as defined by Anthropic engineer Hannah Moran in 2025, are “models using
tools in a loop”. Let’s unpack that statement so we can understand how AI agents can
help instrument our code.
When we talk about AI agents, we’re broadly referring to software harnesses that
(at a minimum) combine a generative LLM (e.g., OpenAI’s GPT-5, Claude Sonnet,
Gemini, etc.) and some subset of tools. In this context, a “tool” is a procedure call that
is exposed to the model through its API. Crucially, tools enable models to execute
deterministic function calls in software.
Consider an LLM in isolation. It predicts the next token of a prompt, based solely on
the data it’s been trained on. A model that’s tuned to function as a chatbot will do
something similar, but each turn (or, back-and-forth request and response) is added
to the conversation, and the model continues its prediction based on that entire
history.
A chat model alone can answer questions, but it can’t reliably inspect an entire
codebase, read the right docs at the right time, edit files safely, and validate the result.
Instrumentation work requires all of these capabilities.
Reductively, an agent is just a harness that gives the model those abilities—similar
to what we, as developers, need to do our jobs day-to-day. Agentic tools can be any‐
thing from shell access, to specialized web search tools (returning results in formats
optimized for LLMs), to integrations with software as a service (SaaS) datastores or
observability databases.
The end result is that agents can be effective partners for adding instrumentation,
especially during migrations from existing libraries or frameworks to OTel, where the
work is repetitive but still needs judgment.

Instrumentation with Agents
Historically, one of the biggest challenges in getting developers to add instrumenta‐
tion to their software is that it’s viewed as toil. And, honestly, it’s often a tedious and
formulaic process.
But the source of value isn’t typing span builder calls. The value is in everything we’ve
discussed so far: the instrumentation strategy you develop. The value is in what you
instrument, how you name it, and what questions it helps you answer.
Agents can help with both halves of that equation. In practice, two working modes
matter: planning and execution.




128   |   Chapter 7: Instrumenting Your Code with OpenTelemetry
In planning mode, you take your specifications and prompts, feed them to the agent,
add appropriate context (e.g., references to user stories, tickets, related artifacts,
software goals, OTel documentation, etc.), and ask the agent to work with you on
creating an instrumentation plan. No code yet—just a spec you can review to identify
span boundaries, names, and attributes.
Planning mode is especially valuable for new services and for migrations: it’s easier
to design once and have the agent implement as it goes, rather than retrofitting a
sprawling codebase later. In this phase, you’ll decide how to instrument, what to
instrument, and which attributes and signals are critical. If you’re using schema tools
(like Weaver), a great artifact from planning is a schema spec and a short set of rules
to add to your agent’s memory.
In execution mode, use caution if you’re working in a brownfield environment. You
should give the agent patterns to follow and rules on how to adapt. AI models will
imitate whatever patterns are most common in your repos, whether those patterns
are good or bad. Brownfield systems are rarely consistent. So, often, this means
your agent will need to implement small, specific, and targeted fixes that can be
generalized into scripts or instructions. Keep changes small enough that code review
actually works.
A useful pattern with agents is to have them automate the automation. Rather than
asking the agent to edit a number of files manually, have it identify patterns that
should change and write a script to make changes programmatically. For example,
have an agent write a script that identifies all custom metrics (using some heuristics),
and then tell it to analyze each result and determine what kind of change makes
the most sense (e.g., converting the metric to a span attribute, deleting it entirely, or
changing its type).
If you don’t already have them (and most teams don’t), consider “telemetry tests” that
capture diagnostic output before and after a telemetry migration. Perhaps use your
agent to first create those tests, and then use it to compare the semantics against
each other. You can also do that with auto-instrumentation: if you’re migrating from
a proprietary APM (application performance monitoring) agent to OTel, capture the
telemetry from both and ask your AI agent to diff them. This is exactly the kind of
repetitive comparison AI is good at.
Finally: share rules. Treat agent instructions like shared tooling. Check them into
version control, keep them simple, and make them consistent across the team. An
emerging pattern is the idea of “agent skills,” which are instructions for an agent that
can be shared among teams, individuals, and organizations.

Useful Strategies for Agents
AI models reward clear, concise, accurate instructions or documentation. They’re
bad at implicit nuance, sarcasm, or other forms of ambiguity. They’re also great at
sounding confident when wrong. Plan accordingly.
That said, some prompting folklore is outdated. You don’t need to bribe models with
rewards or threaten them with punishment.4 Extra emotional language often makes
outputs worse, not better. What matters for successful instrumentation is managing
context (or chat history) and giving crisp constraints.
Here are a few patterns we’ve seen work when asking agents to perform instrumenta‐
tion, or coding tasks in general:
Manage agent context.
   Agents will go offtrack as their context fills up, and they have a recency bias. Use
   features like subagents where an independent agent process performs repetitive
   work in isolated contexts, or keep an external task file in markdown that the
   agent periodically updates, especially for long-horizon tasks.
Work in small chunks.
   You’ll get better and more consistent results by focusing on a single file, or even a
   part of a file, at a time. Periodically commit your changes to source control, and
   don’t be afraid of throwing things away and rolling back if the agent goes off the
   rails.
Don’t be afraid of disposable code.
   You can use agents to build tiny, fit-for-purpose instrumentation tools. Throw
   them away when you’re done. You can even use an agent to build your own
   agent! This is especially useful for migrations as there’s probably a lot of docu‐
   mentation you have access to. Consolidate and compile it, distill it down into
   system prompts and reference files, then build your agent around that core.

Let AI help write the documentation.
    Use an agent to read through your existing libraries or code and have it create

Bring observability into the software development loop.
    Beyond migrations, having real-time observability data integrated into an AI

    allows your agent to make better choices based on real data rather than guesses.
As a real world example, one of the authors was implementing a feature that involved
fetching data and needed to know a good default for page size. The agent was able to
query telemetry data and determine the P50, P99, and P9999 size of result sets for the
feature in question, and how long those database queries took in production. Rather
than guessing or context switching to find out manually, the agent quickly calculated
an optimal number of items to display per page, using real-world data.
None of this replaces engineering judgment. But used thoughtfully, agents can reduce
the cost of doing instrumentation well, and make it easier to keep your telemetry
consistent as your codebase grows.

Conclusion
OpenTelemetry can feel overwhelming at first, but the core idea is straightforward:
model system behavior with shared context, emit signals with consistent semantics,
and layer telemetry so operators can move from high-level symptoms to concrete
causes.
In this chapter we covered trace-first design and context propagation, practical span
boundaries, trace strategies for streaming and asynchronous architectures, and spe‐
cial considerations for serverless and eBPF-based instrumentation. We also covered
how metrics, traces, logs, and events fit together—and how to choose the right signal
for the job.
Finally, we discussed how AI agents can reduce the toil of writing and migrating
instrumentation when you pair them with a real plan, consistent conventions, and
reviewable execution. While the features and capabilities of AI will surely change
