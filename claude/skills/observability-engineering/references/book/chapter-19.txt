               A Note from Charity, Liz, George, and Austin
We continue our look into observability use cases by exploring the world of mobile
and frontend applications. Backend applications and infrastructure are generally
controlled: you have a known OS, predictable resources, uniform networking, stable
time synchronization, etc. With frontend or mobile systems, you generally inherit
the environment: thousands of device models, OS, and browser versions; unreliable
cellular networks; resource throttling; captive portals; ad blockers; and concurrent
processes.
When you control deployments, configs, and sampling, and can correlate across
consistent infrastructure, it’s safe to say that observability happens within bounds:
the problem surface is mostly within your backyard. When dealing with frontend or
mobile applications, observability happens beyond the fence and in actively hostile
territory. You can’t control the runtimes, networks, or versions, and you have to be
extremely careful with taking any action that impacts device performance, available
bandwidth, user privacy, or cost.
As such, many of the observability practices discussed earlier in this book must be
adapted to fit in the frontend and mobile worlds. This chapter, written by Hanson Ho,
details considerations for observability in frontend and mobile. Matt Klein also con‐
tributes a section on considerations for telemetry management for mobile systems.
We begin with Hanson’s perspective.

Status Quo for Mobile and Frontend
For developers who work on mobile and web apps, the term “observability” is still
relatively new—if they know it at all. Those familiar with the term may mostly think
about it as an evolution of monitoring and ops practices for distributed systems. For
frontend or mobile engineers building websites and apps, it may seem synonymous
with the production telemetry they are used to—like stability issues or user actions
like taps and page navigation. The latter is commonly referred to as real user moni‐
toring (RUM), error tracking, or crash reporting when used by development teams to
determine what the user is doing in the app when problems occur.
On mobile and web, this data is often aggregated at the client and then sent to a back‐
end system to compute familiar metrics like daily crash rate and time to first paint.
These metrics summarize the state of the app in production and provide insight into
common problems users are experiencing. Unfortunately, the ability to slice and dice
this data is not great beyond some easily obtained dimensions like locale and app/OS
versions, making it difficult to find particular cohorts or ask interesting questions
when looking for deeper insights.
Production telemetry for mobile and web apps is primarily used in two ways: to
surface and quantify problems at runtime, and to provide additional datapoints
to help developers reproduce the problems internally. This is done by examining
specific problem reports along with metadata describing the execution context and
RUM events on the same device around the same time, in order to narrow down the
code path that could have led to the problem. Investigations tend to require access or
knowledge of the underlying code, as the data itself is generally not enough.
In classic monitoring approaches, the nature of the instrumentation (where problems
are identified on the device and reported) means that you generally have to know
the kinds of problems you are interested in finding beforehand. That means that this
kind of telemetry is not expected to be used to answer unanticipated questions. The
mobile and web app debugging workflow relies heavily on first reproducing the issue,
and then debugging the root cause locally. Luckily, unlike distributed systems, even
the trickiest of problems (if identified) can be reproduced on a single device. We are,
after all, dealing with a single app running on a single device.
Here’s an example of how this data is used:

 1. A few seemingly related crashes, identified by similar stack traces, happen across
    a variety of app screens. The top of the stack trace suggests a NullPointerExcep
    tion (or NPE) during view-tree traversal.
 2. The crashes are a minority of cases—most of those screens load correctly without
    error.

 3. RUM data can be used to find correlations with these crash events: looking
    through the RUM data of a few sessions that ended in this crash, there’s always a
    click on a specific button whose label contains non-ASCII characters.
 4. With that knowledge, you examine the source code for that button and find
    an assumption about the nature of the string used for the label. If it contains
    non-ASCII characters, it triggers a code path resulting in an NPE.
 5. By combining the recorded crash telemetry and the RUM event correlation via
    a session, you can pinpoint the precise conditions needed to reproduce the issue
    and identify the root cause.

While production telemetry can be used in this way to surface some app stability
issues, more subtle problems that are harder to determine like client-side perfor‐
mance degradation aren’t typically reported, unless it’s automatically collected by
first-party tooling. There are a number of reasons why this is the case, but none of
them are that mobile and web developers don’t care about performance; they just
approach the problem differently.
For the most part, performance changes on mobile and web are monitored through
the use of synthetic benchmark tests that run locally or in CI (e.g., macrobenchmarks
on Android), or through a synthetic testing suite. These tests are preferred to pro‐
duction data by devs because they overcome the biggest shortcoming of production
telemetry: the inability to control the execution environment. Synthetic performance
tests enable devs to lock down key performance determinants like hardware and
device state, which means runs are more repeatable and the iterations are more
comparable. With that setup, you can detect small but statistically significant changes
more easily, without requiring the large sample sizes and magnitudes of change
needed if production data were used. The downside of this is that your coverage is
only as good as the workflows that are benchmarked and tested. This makes this a
reasonable but ultimately imperfect solution, especially if your goal is to understand
how the user is experiencing your app.
Another kind of telemetry collected in production is what is known as “product
analytics.” Unlike telemetry used to identify and debug issues, this data is used by
product managers and other parts of the business to determine which features of the
app the users are actually using. Interestingly, the line between this type of telemetry
and RUM and stability events can be quite blurred, as they tend to model the same
app and user behaviors.
The difference between the two is often not what is being proxied, but how the recor‐
ded telemetry is actually used, as well as the types of tooling and vendors involved in
collecting this data. For instance, the same page view might be logged by a product
analytics vendor’s SDK so that product managers can create DAU (daily active users)




                                                      Status Quo for Mobile and Frontend   |   345
graphs for their feature, and also by a RUM vendor’s SDK so that developers can see
the events leading up to a crash.
But product analytics are usually not connected to the production telemetry used by
developers because they go into a totally different data set. This is why a common
and frustrating problem you might encounter on a mobile and frontend team is the
irate product manager, wondering why a key metric of theirs dropped since the last
version was shipped, with a team that can’t explain why because all of their own
dashboards are green.

Instrumentation Limitations
Mobile and web apps are used in contexts that are under constant change, so even the
most stable and performant ones are not free of problems that impact user experience
(UX). Many of these, like crashes, can be and are routinely detected and resolved
using production telemetry, aided by RUM data and contextual metadata to narrow
down the reproduction workflows. For important events and workflow performance
that are reported as telemetry and properly contextualized, engineers that know the
code should be able to quantify and solve any problems they reveal.
But one limitation of the production telemetry that’s collected on mobile and web
apps is that they can be really noisy due to the variety of factors that can impact run‐
time. To mitigate that, you need to model all the relevant factors that affect runtime
and record it as metadata along with your telemetry. This process can be difficult
given the wide array of relevant contextual information, but unless that is done,
problems that only impact certain users may not be easily discoverable. Without
knowledge of the precise dimensions that are material to a particular workflow, you
can’t distinguish between your user cohorts or tease out the signal from the noise.
In practice, only the static, easy-to-capture metadata on mobile and web are captured,
which often leaves the dataset lacking enough context to do all but the most basic of
filtering. If you want to group by app version or language, for example, that should
not be a problem. But if you want to sort users by something difficult to get and
model like network connection strength, or something ephemeral like UX, you’re
generally out of luck. Often, it’s key dimensions like this that are difficult to obtain
that provide the most useful insight in your analysis of app performance.
This leads to the obfuscation of major problems that greatly (but narrowly) impact a
small number of users. For example, an issue that impacts only 1% of your users may
not move the P50 very much—maybe not even P99, depending on the magnitude of
the change. The reason why this is happening may also not be clear, even if you have
all the right exemplars.
Problems like this on mobile and web can’t merely be deemed outliers because those
who are affected, no matter how small a percentage of your user base they represent,


346   |   Chapter 19: Observability for Mobile and Frontend
are totally and completely affected. That 99% of other users aren’t impacted matters
not at all to them. If your DAU is 100 million, and 1% of those users are experiencing
performance that is 2x worse, that’s a million people who are affected. This means
that even if you have the right instrumentation to surface a particular problem, you
may not be able to see it easily.
And therein lies another problem: in practice, many important events and workflow
performances aren’t even captured as telemetry. Typically, only obvious errors like
crashes or common workflows, like app startups that can be automatically captured,
are recorded as such. But we all know the myriad ways a mobile or web app can
perform poorly without crashing that leads to user frustration, like when some
important workflow is too slow for the user (by some unknowable definition). And if
that slowness isn’t from a workflow that can be automatically instrumented like app
startup or network requests, the chances of user frustration resulting from it being
reflected in your collected telemetry would be pretty low. In other words, there are
whole classes of problems that are effectively invisible to most app developers due to
a lack of telemetry.
While the instrumentation friction described isn’t unique to mobile and web apps—as
bespoke code needs bespoke instrumentation—the problem is a lot more glaring, as
most important workflows important to an app are also unique to it. This means they
couldn’t be easily and automatically instrumented like that of the relatively determin‐
istic execution of backend services. The increased level of friction, along with other
factors, make manual instrumentation in mobile and web apps relatively rare. So,
compared to distributed services, code from these apps are far less instrumented.
It follows that when the situation calls for it, manual instrumentation is still not
done as often as it should be because it’s just not something that mobile and web
developers are used to doing. It’s a vicious cycle. And in the rare case that they
are done, complex workflows spanning multiple screens and components—where
execution happens on multiple threads sequentially, unbeknownst to the instrumen‐
tation—consist of instrumentation that is inherently difficult to maintain. All this
in tandem results in a substantial universe of important events and workflows in a
mobile or web app that aren’t reflected in production telemetry.
What’s more, even with the right telemetry and metadata, assessing the impact of a
crash or performance regression on users can be nontrivial because the raw numbers
aren’t enough. Different users are impacted differently in a technical sense (e.g.,
varying degrees of change). So, if you’re looking at metrics with little segmentation—
as most mobile and web developers are used to—the impact may not even be perceiv‐
able. What does it mean to users when your P75 app startup time increased by 20%?
What if P50 increased 80%, but only for folks using Android 12 and below?
Also, different users react to the same level of performance regression differently.
Further, performance degradation matters more in some workflows than in others.


                                                           Instrumentation Limitations   |   347
Even if you can quantify the regression down to how users are affected individually,
how that impacts their overall UX or desire to use your app in the future can vary
as well. For some, a 20% slowdown is barely noticeable. For others, it can push
them over the edge in terms of losing patience with your app. For some, even major
slowdowns don’t affect usage because their workplace has mandated the use of your
app. For others, even a bit of perceived decrease in performance can trigger a switch
to a competitor’s app. So, while it’s great to know the actual magnitude of change, it’s
only part of the picture when assessing user impact.
Raw performance telemetry, even combined with good instinct, can only go so far
in terms of telling you how badly users are affected by app problems. To more objec‐
tively assess that, there needs to be, at minimum, a correlated change in some other
metrics for which you are more certain of the user impact, like DAU or revenue. By
bringing in data that the business cares about, it takes you into the realm of product
analytics, which are generally not related to telemetry used for observability. Why is
that? The reason may be more ridiculous than you think.
Suffice it to say, while the current usage of production telemetry brings some
value to mobile and web app developers, there are a lot more questions that they
cannot answer—important questions that can help identify and quantify app prob‐
lems impacting users and businesses negatively. With the lessons learned in driving
improved observability in distributed systems, we can make similar changes to how
we collect and use telemetry on these notoriously unstable platforms so that the
investment in the infrastructure can be further leveraged—solving problems that
even the operators of the most well-instrumented distributed systems can only dream
of.
But first, there’s that mountain of challenges that need to be overcome.

Overcoming Domain Challenges
Bringing the kind of observability used by backend engineers to mobile and web
presents some unique challenges that are inherent to the domain. Some of them are
technical—though the nontechnical, sociological ones are perhaps just as impactful in
creating barriers that stymie the use of production data by engineers in understand‐
ing the unknown-unknowns. Much of these have to do with the status quo, both in
observability (or o11y) and in the mobile and web development communities, which
combine to create problems that inhibit adoption of observability on mobile and
web—problems that are not typically seen in the backend.

Recognizing the Problem
The first domain challenge to overcome is the prevailing complacency present in the
mobile and web development communities. For the most part, mobile and web devs


348   | Chapter 19: Observability for Mobile and Frontend
are satisfied with the options they have for tracking down and solving performance
and reliability problems in production. This is because they often believe that the
current state of the art tooling would solve all their problems, if only their company
would pay for it.
But the ideal they strive for isn’t observability as the backend understands it, and
it has a much lower ceiling than what is possible. What they typically look for in
production tooling are better diagnostic capabilities and identification for known
problems, and they lean on CI tooling to detect performance regressions introduced
by code changes based on benchmarks.
In particular, it’s the prevailing thought in the community that performance regres‐
sions are best kept in check using synthetic performance tests. These tests run in
controlled environments as part of CI, so material change to performance can be
identified before or soon after a commit has been merged. The goal of never unknow‐
ingly shipping a performance regression is laudable, but the implicit limitation of it
is that you’re only finding regressions in your code when specific workflows happen
under specific device and runtime conditions, which may not necessarily mirror
real-world runtime conditions faced by users.
This strategy is generally pretty good at uncovering pervasive regressions in key
workflows. Identifying which workflows matter most should be straightforward, as
should prioritizing tests around them. It’s not totally free of complications, however:
the workflows you consider most important or most used may not be the most
vulnerable to performance regressions, nor necessarily the most critical to get right
—some workflows have an outsized impact on user satisfaction, if perhaps only
psychologically. This points to a broader challenge of needing to know what problems
you’re looking for in instrumentation, which is anathema to addressing problems that
are unknown-unknowns.
The next part of the equation is harder to resolve: how do you determine the specific
device and runtime conditions that you should always test against? The problem with
production is that there isn’t any one set of conditions that will get you a high degree
of coverage when you’re talking about an app that the general public can access. The
variance across multiple dimensions—whether it be device quality, browser and OS
versions, concurrent app execution, network availability, etc.—means that whatever
set conditions you have for your tests, you know the majority of your users wouldn’t
be under those precisely.
In fact, that much is recognized in these tests: they are repeatable by design, with
as few variations in the execution environment as possible, which is the opposite of
what you get in chaotic, heterogeneous production environments. These conditions
under which the tests are run are designed to maximize the ability to surface code
regressions, which comes at the expense of being realistic. But that’s really a feature,
not a bug.


                                                         Overcoming Domain Challenges   |   349
In other words, the ideal setup for determining mobile and web performance regres‐
sion hinges on having predefined workflows running under unrealistic conditions.
While it’s great for doing what it’s designed to do, it’s not a great way to understand
what’s happening in production, especially when the most problematic issues tend to
be the ones you don’t anticipate.
Mobile and web devs recognize this limitation, so they use production data to fill
in that gap. But the gap they perceive is not in identifying nonobvious problems,
but in quantifying the severity of obvious problems, and getting the right diagnostic
information to solve or reproduce them. This is especially important when a new
release gains adoption, as devs want to know if they’ve introduced any new issues that
their tests didn’t find—and if they did, they want to know how to reproduce and solve
them.
The last part is crucial: it’s understood that knowing the codebase is a requirement
to use production data to solve problems in mobile and web. But it’s not expected
that one can simply look at the data and know exactly where the problem lies. This
is why on larger teams, the key function of mobile on-calls is to be a master triager,
not necessarily the person fixing the issues. This assumption is so ingrained in the
community that devs don’t expect they can narrow down the root cause of most
complex issues based on production data alone, which means they don’t look for it in
their tooling.
This means that, explicitly or implicitly, the majority of mobile and web developers
feel good about the role that production data plays in their day-to-day work. Some
may wish that more app state and behavior are captured so that there’s more runtime
information to work with when issues surface, but they don’t expect any more out
of production data than what they’re getting: diagnostic info and the surfacing of
obvious issues. Worse yet, some believe that they are already getting all they can get
out of production telemetry! This complicity has stunted the growth of observability
on these platforms. It’s the curse of being “good enough.”

Instrumentation Difficulties
There are many ways in which poor performance and reliability can manifest them‐
selves in mobile apps—and do so without crashing. But while crash reporting is
well-supported in the mobile ecosystems, generic performance instrumentation is
not. This leaves a whole swath of problems that are not quantified by existing
telemetry coming from mobile apps, which means they are effectively invisible to
app developers.
Why is generic performance monitoring—such a seemingly important feature—sup‐
ported so poorly in both mobile and web ecosystems? Properly instrumenting an app
and ensuring that the telemetry reliably gets back to your datacenter is surprisingly
complex. And when you have it, the data—collected from heterogeneous sources


350   |   Chapter 19: Observability for Mobile and Frontend
under varying conditions—tends to be quite noisy, so it may not be able to detect
nonobvious issues unless you have a large sample. It’s yet again a vicious cycle: it’s
hard to do and not always useful, so people don’t do it; if people don’t do it, there
isn’t market demand for first-party or vendor solutions; this makes it harder for the
community to adopt en masse; and this then reduces demand.
What makes instrumentation and telemetry collection on mobile and web so com‐
plex? Let’s first talk about app architecture.

Application architecture
For distributed systems, the backbone of observability telemetry are distributed traces
augmented by related wide events, aggregated and properly contextualized. Notewor‐
thy incidents don’t generally arise from detecting a small number of outlier events;
they arise with the whole system’s performance degrading (or with leading indicators
suggesting that degradation can be expected soon). So, to know when your system’s
behavior gets to a point of noteworthiness, you model its performance—all of it.
Particularly, latency and execution duration should be monitored, because slower
generally means worse, so detecting slowdowns (along with failures) becomes a key
determinant of system health.
In fact, you can go a long way into high-level summarization of a distributed system’s
behavior and performance by using a distributed trace to capture execution duration,
code paths, and errors. You may need more data and context to know what’s going
on under the hood, but the answer of whether it’s doing what it’s expected to do
in a timely manner can be answered through the performance data. This is because
backend services have one job: to process requests and return the results. And a
distributed trace gives you the bones of that.
For mobile and web apps, that simply isn’t the case. There isn’t an automatically
instrumentable workflow that summarizes an app’s behavior—one whose trace can
determine whether the app is in a healthy state. Unlike backend services, usage of
these apps follows nonlinear paths, and success is not purely determined by execution
duration, but by whether the user gets what they were looking for out of using
the app. In other words, little can be known if you only had traces around process
execution. The fact that a user lingered on a screen for 10 minutes is not an indication
of whether the app is behaving well or poorly. So if you created a span to represent it,
its duration will not necessarily tell you much about how the app is performing.
Instead, the best way to model the usage of a mobile or web app, especially when the
users encounter unexpected behavior, is through recording telemetry for important
events (like crashes) or for workflows (like payment). But therein lies the problem: if
an event or workflow isn’t explicitly captured by instrumentation, it effectively doesn’t
exist for observability purposes, because there’s no way to passively or implicitly
model them. In practice, this means that events and workflows that aren’t easy to


                                                          Overcoming Domain Challenges   |   351
instrument or couldn’t be done automatically without direct intervention from app
developers are usually not recorded as telemetry. You may get data about crashes
and app startup, but not about unanticipated out-of-memory errors, or your bespoke
payment workflow failing due to a client-side validation error.
The main reason that manual instrumentation of workflows on mobile and web is
so difficult is because you have to identify not only the workflow, but the precise
area in the code that represents the beginning and end of that workflow—as well
as everything in between that can add additional context. All of these could reside
in different modules written and maintained by different teams. And threading
the needle through all of them is only the first step—ensuring that code changes
unbeknownst to the instrumentation writer don’t break the instrumentation is the
second, even more difficult step.
In other words, the “trace the execution” idiom that allows new telemetry to be
associated with the “current” trace on the executing thread doesn’t apply. A workflow
on mobile or web that can be thought of as a single trace can, and often is, executed
serially in disconnected chunks across multiple threads and screens, all connected
loosely by bespoke components within the app that don’t share any execution context.
Basically, the logical grouping cannot simply be divined by looking at the code, and
there isn’t an implicitly shared context that ties them today.
With a service inside of a distributed system, modeling its execution as a span and
then adding wide events to give more details is relatively straightforward: start a span
when a request is received, end it when it finishes, and ensure all events are associated
with the current span—which is often just whatever span was last started on the
running thread. The trace matches up nicely with the execution of the service. On
mobile and web, you have to know where you want to start the workflow and where
you want to end it. Unless it’s built into the app architecture, this otherwise requires
knowledge of the code and how all the components work together.
Given that a workflow can cross multiple components, it means that knowledge of the
instrumentation has to be embedded pretty tightly into the business logic of the code.
This can be tricky because different parts of the code enabling that workflow may not
contain a shared context. So the simple act of knowing which span to stop may be
challenging if you can’t do something like “stop whatever span is active on the current
thread.” This makes the complexity of instrumentation increase with the amount of
instrumentation that is present, which is a tricky proposition when you want good
coverage.

Life in a chaotic, remote sandbox
Overcoming instrumentation friction caused by app architecture may be nontrivial,
but it can be done. What is more difficult, if not impossible, to overcome is the
extraction of relevant information on mobile and web platforms, and delivering the


352   |   Chapter 19: Observability for Mobile and Frontend
telemetry to the server reliably. Mobile and web apps are essentially processes run in
sandboxed environments at the whim of the user and OS. App developers have little
control or visibility into the underlying system resources, or when execution starts
and stops. This limits the degree to which the app can provision enough memory and
CPU cycles to run the app properly, and also restricts the types of information that
the instrumentation has access to in order to explain performance.
Is a particular instance of a span an outlier caused by poor hardware, code regression,
or the fact that a YouTube video is playing concurrently? The available runtime
context can suggest plausible reasons (e.g., low-end device model), which can then
be corroborated with more data points (e.g., all workflows on this model are varying
degrees of slow). But the APIs afforded to apps to extract that context in production
are restricted to only what that app process has access to due to privacy reasons—
mobile devices and browsers, after all, can be seen as multitenant devices. As such,
they aren’t nearly exhaustive, particularly when it comes to what’s running on the
device concurrently with the execution of the app. So you are left with many potential
causes of performance degradation that are not represented in the data.
And even when your instrumentation is able to capture and contextualize the right
telemetry, that telemetry must first be sent over the internet to your servers before it
can be consumed. That it has to be done by processes living at the mercy of the user
and OS—over spotty network connections with limited bandwidth and high latency
(if a network connection exists at all)—means that recording the telemetry locally is
just the first step in the long journey to observability.
This point is especially pertinent because an app’s ability to record and send telemetry
is most unreliable when it’s running low on resources or operating under suboptimal
conditions, which is precisely when telemetry is the most useful. Making sure teleme‐
try is reliably delivered when everything is working fine is easy. But the challenges are
tenfold when your process isn’t being scheduled and the memory pressure is causing
so much garbage collection that it makes your app unresponsive, increasing the
likelihood that a user will manually terminate the app without your instrumentation
being able to save and send the telemetry it has recorded in memory.
The insidious implication is that unless you can quantify data loss, you wouldn’t
even know what data you’re missing, because lost telemetry will be skewed toward
situations when the app is having issues. So while the data you collect may suggest
that your app and users are doing fine, that may not be the case: telemetry from
problematic situations is lost at a much higher rate than from stable ones. This
could lull you into a false sense of security—a delusion that everything is fine simply
because errors and slowdowns aren’t being reported.

In some ways, mobile observability is the “final frontier” of observability practice.
While the importance of server-side observability is well-established at this point,
very few mobile teams have any observability practice other than crash monitoring.
This is unfortunate for two reasons.
First, in mature mobile applications, typically over 99.9% of user sessions are crash-
free. This means that the ability to understand subpar behavior (functionality bugs,
hangs, performance issues, rage taps, rage quits, and so on) is actually much higher
leverage when aiming to improve UX, yet most teams don’t have access to any tools
that can help.
Second, as an industry, we love to talk about our server-side success rate and all of
the tools that we have at our disposal to understand why we aren’t achieving the
requisite number of nines. Yet, as I like to say, a 99.99% success rate at the server-side
edge doesn’t mean much when malformed JSON is being sent via a 200 OK response,
causing the app’s golden flow to simply stop working. I’ve stopped counting the
number of times this has happened in my career; it’s certainly not theoretical.
So if mobile observability is arguably the most important observability—as it allows
us to understand what our customers are actually experiencing when using our
products—why isn’t it more established? The reason is that it’s extremely hard to do
well, and efforts to simply take the architectures and methodologies that have worked
well in the server world and apply them to mobile have failed time and time again.
So, what makes mobile observability difficult and different from the server world? A
few things:
Long release cycles
   Due to the epically long release cycles inherent in mobile, the simple act of
   adding a log or analytic event can take weeks for initial rollout, and months if
   not years to reach the entire population of app users. This means that all things
   being equal, mobile engineers would like to add as much logging and analytics as
   possible ahead of time in case the data is needed. However this is unfortunately at
   odds with the fact of the next challenge.
Users caring about data usage
    Users that are on metered data plans care deeply about how much data an
    app is using, and will notice if an app is using “too much.” Furthermore, even
    if users have unlimited data, slow networks and sporadic connectivity mean
    that observability data is sharing available bandwidth with critical application


354   |   Chapter 19: Observability for Mobile and Frontend
    traffic—i.e., sending too many logs can materially impact the performance of
    the main application. Note that nearly every large mobile app has measured that
    perceived application performance (time to first interaction, delay when moving
    screens and clicking buttons, etc.) impacts conversion and core business metrics,
    so this is not an academic concern.
Sporadic connectivity
    Unlike on server, sporadic or poor connectivity on mobile must be considered a
    normal event and not an outlier. This means that observability systems must plan
    ahead for sophisticated batching, local storage, and so on, or enough data will be
    lost to impact the reliability of the observation system.
Unplanned termination as the norm
   Because apps can be terminated at any time and may not be able to run in the
   background, mobile observability systems must plan for this case—similar to
   how sporadic connectivity might be dealt with.
Huge cardinality and cost
   Depending on the app, a relatively small server infrastructure may support hun‐
   dreds of thousands or even millions of monthly active mobile users. Storing
   individual telemetry for each active user is very costly, so aggregation and sum‐
   marization systems are typically needed for mobile observability.
Naive implementation of local storage for observability data, impacting user interface
(UI) performance
    While mobile apps can be multithreaded, in general a single “main” thread is
    used for drawing the UI and handling user input events. Care must be taken
    to not block the main thread unnecessarily or the app’s frame rate and general
    responsiveness may suffer. This is particularly important for local storage of
    observability data which needs to be durably stored due to several of the previ‐
    ously described issues. Thus, the observability implementation needs to carefully
    consider how logging data is processed and stored so as to not adversely impact
    app performance.
We have established that mobile observability is as important, if not more important,
than server observability. We have also established that mobile observability is very
challenging, and applying server observability methodologies directly is unlikely to
yield a satisfactory result, both in terms of cost and data quality. So, what then?
We need to step back, start from first principles, and design an observability system
tailor-made for mobile—one that provides excellent data quality, at a satisfactory
price point, and with high performance.




                             Applying Local Storage and Real-Time Control to Mobile Observability   |   355
Fundamentally, an observability system made for mobile needs to reconcile two
competing goals:

 1. It takes a really long time to add telemetry (let’s assume for now that all telemetry
    is wide events and that traces/metrics can be derived from those events), so
    developers should have a way to add as many logs as possible before shipping the
    app.
 2. We can’t ingest all telemetry at all times from hundreds of millions of mobile
    devices, for both cost and performance reasons.

It turns out there is a way to satisfy both of these requirements, though it requires
a fundamental shift in how we think about our observability practice. The way to
accomplish this is via coupling local storage of telemetry data with a real-time control
plane that can instruct every device on what telemetry to send for ingestion. Let’s
unpack these concepts in more detail:
Local storage
    Instead of sending every single log line over the network to a server-side
    log ingestion system, we can instead store all of the logs locally in a high-
    performance circular buffer of finite size. The circular buffer itself is persisted
    to disk. This solution solves multiple difficulties described previously:
          • Developers can log as much as they want because, by default, logs don’t leave
            the device, which mitigates concerns around high ingestion costs and app
            performance overhead for telemetry collection.
          • Disk persistence means the telemetry survives unplanned app terminations.
          • High performance means that the act of logging has minimal impact on app
            functionality, especially the main thread and UI performance.
Real-time control plane
    Instead of devices aimlessly pushing all telemetry in real time to a remote inges‐
    tion service, we use an intelligent bidirectional streaming connection between the
    ingestion service and the device. The server provides instructions to the client
    about what data to send under what conditions. And the client sends data in
    response to those instructions.
These two changes (local storage and a real-time control plane) may sound small, but
the implications are profound. Instead of sending all the data all the time, we now
have the ability to send a large amount of data when we need it, and none when we
don’t. This “fidelity dial” means that operators can decide how to get the best value
from their telemetry ingestion, and change it on the fly if our needs shift.




356   |    Chapter 19: Observability for Mobile and Frontend
Let’s look at a couple of examples of changes to workflows:
For monitoring purposes
    Synthetic metrics can be derived from logs (both counters and histograms) and
    sent directly from devices in a highly efficient and compressed form. The metrics
    that are sent can be changed immediately and without any app deployments,
    meaning that if monitoring needs change, the ingested data can be changed.
For debugging purposes
    The entire circular buffer can be ingested to produce a “session replay.” This can
    be done in response to highly specific conditions (i.e., a finite state machine sent
    from server to client tells the device what conditions to look for). For example,
    “Wait for the user to view the checkout screen. If the user leaves the checkout
    screen or 15 seconds have passed without a checkout, increment a counter and
    dump a full session with session replay.”
Using local storage, real-time control, and matching conditions based on finite state
machines, it now becomes possible to debug highly specific scenarios depending
on the need at hand. From looking at specific cohorts (all users in a geographic
area on a particular app version on a particular device model) to even debugging a
single specific user (very common in customer support scenarios), local storage and
real-time control make it easy to accomplish whatever targeting is needed. Further,
building the entire system on top of arbitrarily wide, structured events means that
user journeys, session replays, and so on can all be derived on demand.
Mobile observability certainly has unique challenges. Its importance requires unique
solutions, and our willingness to set aside long-established methodologies that have
largely worked well on server—cost control has not worked well on server, and
similar issues plague naive mobile solutions.
By replacing the “simple pipe”—which streams all data from device to server with
a smart system based on local storage and real-time control—we can reduce costs
and vastly increase productivity by allowing mobile developers to log to their hearts
content in every app release, while giving them access to this bounty of extra signal
when they actually need it.
Further, we can finally provide real observability to mobile engineering teams across
the industry that, in many ways, are decades behind their server peers in their
ability to dig into the unknown-unknowns, previously only having access to crash
monitoring. Crashes are important, but they are a minute piece of the overall puzzle.
The future is bright in the mobile observability world, and our end users will thank us
for providing more delightful and less buggy apps.

What to Observe and Why
Matt Klein’s section highlights a subtle but important differentiating factor of observ‐
ability in mobile and web: we are not observing one complex system, but many,
many simple ones. Aggregates create summaries not of a series of connected nodes
and services acting to serve the same set of incoming requests, but of millions and
millions of unrelated processes running on different devices, operated by different
users, each with different usage goals and expectations.
Mobile observability is akin to surveying a heterogeneous population; truths are
summaries that describe cohorts of users with shared characteristics, ephemeral or
concrete. Conclusions about the behavior of one application instance running on
one device controlled by one user can only tell you but so much about a different
instance running on a different device controlled by a different user. Insights have to
be qualified, locked to the particular context from which they were derived.
The variability of P50 and P99 can be vast on mobile and web simply because the
operating conditions are wildly different. It’s no fault of the application if devices
from 10 years ago are running alongside brand new ones. It follows that knowing
what the P50 experience is can only tell you so much about what P99 may be. That
fact alone should make folks who only benchmark with a single device worry about
how representative their data is of what their users experience.
To put it another way, knowing what the P50 duration is for a workflow in a dis‐
tributed system tells you what you can expect to happen when more requests come
in, in terms of performance, assuming a normal amount of variability. Knowing what
the P50 duration is for a workflow in a mobile app doesn’t tell you very much about
what you can expect at all, unless the execution context is very similar to that of the
instance that provided the P50 value. It’s perfectly reasonable if a cold app startup P50
is 1 second while the last measurement is 10 seconds, if the device generating that last
measurement was released nine years ago.
This is why the sets of tooling and use cases that help the discovery of problems and
then give insight into how those problems can be solved can be very distinct. The
former requires sifting through the data to identify small cohorts of similar users, to
find issues that impact them that aren’t obvious because they don’t impact everyone,
while the latter is about finding enough details about the experiences of the impacted
cohort so that you can understand why they are impacted. The former requires
breadth while the latter requires depth—perhaps to a degree that isn’t feasible to
capture in production.
What this means is while production telemetry can tell you quite a bit about the
state of the app and device, various factors make it so that the depth of data you can
capture at runtime pales in comparison to the kind information you have access to
when you can reproduce the issue locally.


358   |   Chapter 19: Observability for Mobile and Frontend
For instance, the Android platform gives you the ability to take profiling data of your
device while your application is running. This is a feature of the OS, and it provides
information not only about your app’s process, but also all the other processes that are
running on the device, as well as low-level system information like CPU scheduling,
memory, and hardware states. Android apps simply aren’t granted the permission to
access that kind of system data, and even if they were, the data volume would be too
large for it to be feasibly captured and transmitted at runtime in a way that doesn’t
affect UX.
This is why, for debugging purposes, Android devs have traditionally preferred local
tools that give all the diagnostic information you would want. And the fact that
issues are always reproducible if they are well-understood means that if observability
tooling can provide that understanding, devs would have the data they need to do
their job without asking more from it.
As such, discovering nonobvious app issues and pinpointing the precise conditions
under which issues are reproducible are the dual primary goals of mobile observabil‐
ity. Current tooling available to most mobile and web developers falls short of these
goals, but production telemetry combined with context is extraordinarily well-suited
to address them. Finding diagnostic information, while useful, is only a secondary
goal.

Opportunities for Improvement
While the challenges for creating observability in mobile and web applications are
unique and nontrivial, the opportunities that it presents can be described similarly:
unique and nontrivial. The reason for that comes down to having direct access to
a source of data that has previously been untapped by observability practitioners:
users. Not only can users provide telemetry through their actions that aren’t normally
accounted for, but that data also comes with rich context from which cause and effect
can be inferred at the workflow instance level. That direct linkage is invaluable for
finding needles in haystacks, when deducing from aggregates becomes difficult as
conditions narrow and sample sizes dwindle.
The unique and nontrivial opportunities to leverage data from users is what makes
all the toil to overcome the myriad challenges of mobile and frontend observability
worthwhile, to see all the work bear fruit. The juice, as they say, is worth the squeeze.

Making Observability User-Focused
Traditionally, instrumentation of distributed systems mostly produces telemetry that
models the execution of services: how long they took to execute, whether the execu‐
tions encountered any errors, and the context under which they run. Users of the
application, meanwhile, are an abstraction—the makers of requests—and their role


                                                          Opportunities for Improvement   |   359
can largely be summarized by the kinds of requests they make and the parameters on
those requests. So it’s not a large leap to say that they are pretty well represented in
existing observability systems, assuming request parameters are properly captured to
contextualize the associated telemetry.
Even when observability moved to the mobile and web spaces, we largely replicated
the same behavior when it comes to instrumentation: what is happening in the app
and how well is it performing? Even when the telemetry is the direct result of user
actions (e.g., RUM events), what we capture is still very matter-of-fact events that
we can observe: screen taps, page navigation, application startup duration, and so
on. But stopping there means ignoring a key advantage of making mobile and web
apps observable—our ability to directly observe in its entirety a single user and/or
session—and how we can leverage that connection to increase what we ultimately
want to understand: UX.
Trying to tie together telemetry for a given user or session in a distributed system is
possible and potentially very useful. However, knowing all the requests from a single
user or session can only tell you so much about how the user is experiencing the app,
because so much of what happened between requests is not known to the backend.
Why is there a two-minute gap between request A and request B? Is that reasonable?
There is only so much you can glean about why requests are made when you only
have data from your backend.
But what if you have access to all that is happening on the app itself? Knowing that
an attempt at checkout was made but ultimately not followed through with, as well as
what happened during the session after the initial attempt and what potential factors
could have caused it—that’s when you can start applying observability techniques
to understand not only application behavior, but also user behavior. Once you have
telemetry that models user actions related to other telemetry and context collected
from the app, that’s when your observability becomes truly user focused.
After all, isn’t the whole point of all this to ensure that users have good experiences
when they use your app? Experience is hard to understand directly when users are an
amorphous abstraction of request makers. But when you can get data that ties users
to app telemetry over a long period of time, you can paint a much clearer picture of
how their usage might vary depending on the UX.
A wise person once said that the nines don’t matter if your users aren’t happy. If you
have a mobile or web app and you don’t try to quantify user happiness using data
collected on the client side, are you even doing observability right?

Quantifying User Experience
Before going further, it’s important to be more concrete about what is meant
by “quantifying user happiness.” Too often, vendors make up easy-to-understand
“scores” that sum up the quality or stability of an app because evaluating said app
along multiple complex dimensions is, well, too complex for most stakeholders. Simi‐
larly, “happiness” sounds a bit like sentiment analysis, which is, at best, an imprecise
notion to quantify.
To proxy the quality of UX via telemetry in such a way that it’s stable and useful for
observability, neither generic algorithmic aggregates of metrics nor fuzzy sentiment
analysis would stand up to scrutiny. We need something that will consistently be
sensitive to change in UX, all else being equal. In other words, it has to be relatively
unbiased events from the app, or metrics that derive from it.
Stepping back a bit, the reason users use an app is to accomplish a goal: look at
pictures, buy an album, order some food. UX describes the journey a user has from
the moment they pick up their phone to the moment they accomplish what they
set out to do. A good UX—like if the app launches quickly, the UI is responsive,
and there are minimal delays—tends to allow a user to quickly do what they set out
to do with little frustration. Conversely, a bad UX—like if the app launches slowly,
is unresponsive to user actions, or has confusing UI—often causes users to give up
before they can accomplish their goal.
Mobile and web apps, due to their proximity to the user, can gather telemetry that
describes the user experience and intention and whether their goals are achieved.
While instrumentation can only record what’s happening in the app, there are events
that can be used as proxies for user intention and outcome achievement, which
means we can correlate them with other telemetry and context in order to under‐
stand the factors that lead to a higher likelihood of a user intention being met with a
positive outcome. The ability of telemetry to do this—and to describe and reflect user
intention and outcome, along with the runtime context—is what makes observability
“user focused.”
To extend this further, one can assume that if a user wants to do X, they are happier
if they were able to do X. While there are many reasons why a user who expresses an
intention doesn’t ultimately achieve a successful outcome, one can assume that bad
UX—including poor performance and bugs—is one of them. Therefore, this source
of users not achieving a positive outcome can be seen as something the app can
directly control. Something that completely removes all friction from the process (i.e.,
as soon as an intention is expressed, a positive outcome is achieved) will make failure
nearly zero. Conversely, something about UX that is so bad that it prevents users
from achieving a positive outcome, will make failure the only outcome.




                                                         Opportunities for Improvement   |   361
Therefore, user happiness can be proxied by the degree to which users achieve
a positive outcome—which in turn can be proxied by the likelihood that a user
who expresses an intention ultimately achieves one. So, something as simple as
“checkout completion rate”—which could be calculated by taking the number of
complete checkouts in an ecommerce app and dividing it by the number of checkout
attempts—can be seen as a metric that captures user happiness (i.e., did the user do
the thing they wanted to do?).

Adapting Existing Approaches
Now, you might be thinking: isn’t this just product analytics (i.e., the kind of data
that product managers use to determine which features are used)? In a way, that is
true. The actions modeled by user analytics products are similar to the user intention
and outcomes described here. The difference is that other than static context that
can be used for basic segmentation, product analytics data isn’t tied to telemetry that
describes the UX and app performance.
This is where user-focused observability provides utility: it ties together previously
siloed data used by both product managers and devs so that they can mutually
explain each other, allowing user behavior changes to be correlated with app perfor‐
mance data and quantifying the real impact of regressions in terms that the business
understands. By linking a particular instance of a successful or failed use journey with
all the context and telemetry that is collected in the past and concurrently, you have a
means to explain why the journey was or wasn’t successful.
Too often, product managers with access to product analytics will find changes to
conversion funnel data but, based solely on the data, cannot explain why. This is
because product analytics merely reports what happened and to whom, nothing
more. Mobile engineers are then tasked with finding out the reason, often knowing
little more than the timeframe in which the change was perceived. But because
mobile apps are relatively simple—at least compared to distributed systems—the root
causes of many issues can be isolated simply by looking at the changes to the app that
were made around that time.
While this workflow is generally good enough, there are many cases where it isn’t.
If the issue is nonobvious, multifactorial, affects only a subset of users, or is not
even caused by a change in the code, the amount of time that could be lost to
investigations could be significant—if the root causes can be determined at all. This
is where user-focused observability shines, and it’s not trying to isolate the root cause
using only the data, just that there is a user-impacting problem to investigate.
Being user focused flips the paradigm of observability from something that first
relies on detecting anomalies with the app and then determining user impact, to one
where you first detect user impact and then determine the underlying app anomalies
causing said impact. This switch eliminates the question of whether an app issue is


362   |   Chapter 19: Observability for Mobile and Frontend
impacting users given that the reason why it was found in the first place was due
to users being impacted. There will be no more debating whether a 20% increase
in P75 startup time merits an investigation. You will have data correlated with the
increase—potentially even more if you were running an experiment.

Applying User-Focused Observability
User-focused observability flips the traditional paradigm: instead of detecting techni‐
cal anomalies and guessing their impact, you first detect user impact and then isolate
the underlying technical anomalies. This switch ensures that every investigation is
centered on a material problem affecting UX or business key performance indicators
(KPIs).

Iterative Analysis Process
The goal is understanding how user behavior is impacted by app behavior through
the direct linking of user intentions and outcomes with app telemetry and metadata
at the workflow level, which can then be verified repeatedly at a statistically signifi‐
cant level in aggregate. The crux of this process relies on the ability to filter the
data by a variety of dimensions in order to tease out the relevant ones so that the
differences between cohorts is statistically significant.
The process plays out when a difference in user behavior is detected—when we iterate
to isolate pertinent dimensions that are correlated with the change, and identify other
correlations that could be investigated further to provide even more clues as to the
areas in the app where the root cause(s) may lie.
Given the noise inherent in the production data that is collected on mobile and
frontend apps whose usage may be cyclical, there will be false positives and dead
ends. But the idea is that any repeatable pattern of correlation suggests some root
cause, and it’s the observability practitioner’s job to determine whether it’s caused by
something under their control, which is often the case.
Let’s take a look at a fictional example to show how user-focused observability can
help track down app issues in production that are otherwise difficult to identify.

Example: Food Delivery App
FakeBurger is a food delivery app with tens of millions of daily active users globally.
An important metric monitored by product managers is “checkout rate,” defined as
how often a checkout workflow that starts is completed successfully. That is, when a
user shows sufficient intention to order food from a restaurant, they go through the
workflow to pay for it successfully. While this metric can fluctuate during the day due
to a number of factors, in the aggregate, there’s a range within which that metric is
expected to be.


                                                      Applying User-Focused Observability   |   363
The checkout rate is important to the business because drops may indicate an
increase in the friction experienced by users doing the main thing the FakeBurger
app is used for: ordering food. It can be viewed as a proxy of how happy users are
with the app, at least in the narrow sense that they were ultimately able to do what
they set out to. A change to this rate may not necessarily indicate that something
about the app has changed, but changes to the app can definitely affect this positively
or negatively. A decrease of the checkout rate could mean lost sales in the short term
and customer churn in the long term, so it’s an important one to keep an eye on.
In terms of the telemetry to calculate the checkout rate, we can model this in a
number of ways. The key data required are two events (e.g., start_checkout and
end_checkout) that are linked by some unique ID wherein the second event knows
it’s associated with the first, and the first event can be recorded without requiring
the second event to have happened. Depending on the backend, this could be a struc‐
tured log, two structured logs, a span, or whatever can keep track of this information.
While the telemetry tells you what happened in the app, contextual metadata that
distinguishes one checkout instance from another can tell you why it succeeded or
didn’t. For FakeBurger, they passively collect context like sessionId, userId, and
app version for all the telemetry they record. For workflows they are especially
interested in, such as checkout, they add unique and relevant metadata that help
further contextualize the telemetry, like payment type, discount code, previous app
screen, and so on.
It’s important to note that much of the contextual metadata isn’t directly represented
in the data sent to the server in API calls. In other words, app-only concepts like
the previous screen that the user was on are not represented in telemetry used for
backend observability. That this metadata describes factors that can cause the app
to behave differently means that they represent important information that can help
explain user behavior and app performance—data that would otherwise be lost if
telemetry weren’t collected from the mobile app. FakeBurger has learned the hard
way that looking solely at backend data is not enough to understand what the end
users experience in their app.
In addition, other RUM telemetry collected by the app during the session—such as
user navigation and screen taps—can also be considered contextual metadata with
which to differentiate checkout instances. It could help answer important questions
like “what did the user do during the checkout?” and to document app states that are
otherwise lost.
As mentioned before, FakeBurger uses the checkout rate as a proxy to user happiness
and satisfaction with the app. When it changes to a degree that is statistically signifi‐
cant—after controlling for the expected time and date-based fluctuations—they will
know it’s very likely that the status quo with the app has changed: whether it’s due to



364   |   Chapter 19: Observability for Mobile and Frontend
the UI being modified, a new app bug, a change in the user base, or even a change to
the instrumentation implementation.
Changes like this in mobile apps tend to coincide with a new app version, feature
rollout, or significant shift to the user base (e.g., entering a new market with users
who behave differently in aggregate than existing users). This is why after a new app
version is released and reaches a critical mass, FakeBurger mobile engineers look
at how key metrics like checkout rate have changed in production. They not only
look at these in aggregate for the whole population—they also split out data in other
ways, like comparing data from the new version to the rest of the population, as
well as comparing the magnitude of difference in some sample groups when previous
versions were rolled out.
The reason they split the data in multiple ways is so each view can corroborate
the others, as different comparisons carry different embedded biases. For instance,
splitting users by new and old versions during a specific time period will show a
difference. This is because early adopters of new FakeBurger versions tend to have
better phones, making slowness-related abandonments less likely and checkout rates
higher than those of late adopters, who abandon checkouts at a higher rate due to
performance-related issues.
As is apparent in this example, simply determining whether a change to the checkout
rate in production is caused by an app change is nontrivial. Doing that justice is
outside the scope of this chapter and book, but let’s assume they are able to determine
whether a change is caused. When they do, they can ask questions like the following:

  • Was there a cohort within the population for which the drop was outsized?
  • Did any other metrics change?
  • When you split out the sessions that contained a failed checkout and compared
    the data before and after the checkout rate drop, were there any differences in
    terms of the metadata or events that occurred prior to the failure?

The telemetry and metadata that FakeBurger records around the checkout workflow
will be able to answer these questions for them. Through this data—after it’s properly
processed and made available via dashboards—mobile engineers and SREs alike are
able to find app issues like the following, which would be difficult to surface were it
not for user-focused observability:

  • UI bugs not explicitly logged that add friction or prevent users from checking out
    (e.g., the “Next” button rendering below the fold)
  • A new discount code format that disabled the “Submit” button the first time the
    user enters the checkout flow—resolved only by abandoning and re-entering it
    a second time (potentially occurring without a new app version, as it might not
    have been caused by a client change)

  • Serious errors occurring infrequently enough not to move top-line app metrics
    in a statistically significant way, but which disproportionately impacted checkout
    rate by affecting heavy users
  • Multiple small glitches introduced over time, each too infrequent to move app
    metrics on their own, but collectively degrading the UX enough to impact con‐
    version rates

Through user-focused observability, FakeBurger is able to not only identify regres‐
sions but also further ask the data questions that they had not initially anticipated
asking. The reason is that they center on what matters first—user experience—and let
telemetry and metadata explain their changes. They will no longer only chase changes
to telemetry (not knowing which actually matters to their users and their business),
but instead focus on metrics that they know for sure move the needle. They let the
data suggest avenues of investigation or causes that they wouldn’t otherwise think of
in the chaotic environment of end user–facing mobile and web apps.

Mobile and Frontend Applications Deserve Observability
Observability on mobile and frontend applications needs to center users and their
experiences in order to maximize the utility provided by the unique perspective of
telemetry and metadata gathered that way. Instead of trying to isolate small problems
within the app in a sea of noisy and inconclusive data, it’s better to turn that around
and identify changes to user behavior and, through the data, provide reasons as to
why that might be the case.
The paradigm shift of looking first for user impact before app-level problems can be
jarring for developers who are used to looking at production data solely for more
information about app problems. Instead, user-focused observability goes a level
higher and identifies problems in the UX that are perhaps correlated with app-level
problems that are less easy to detect unless you know what to look for. It reduces the
size of the haystack in which you have to find the needle and, in doing so, ensures
every haystack you look through is causing material problems in UX and impacts
KPIs.
This is only possible through user-focused observability, because we have the ability
to model user intentions and outcomes alongside traditional app telemetry and meta‐
data—linking them directly rather than just inferring correlations in the aggregate.
Not only is that difficult to do on the backend, but the data used is missing a
pertinent source entirely: what is happening in the app that the end-user is actually
using.

and difficult to consistently obtain in a timely manner, it represents a treasure trove
of valuable data that you can’t otherwise obtain. The juice may be hard to squeeze,
but its sweetness hits like nothing else.
