                A Note from Charity, Liz, George, and Austin
We end our look into observability use cases by seeing how many of the concepts
we’ve discussed come together for engineering teams in the real world. The previous
chapters of this book have covered individual concepts or contexts to illustrate how
observability is practiced effectively in modern systems. But we think it’s best to show
how those concepts and practices are integrated to materially change real outcomes
for real teams at leading software companies.
In this chapter, Kesha Mykhailov shares the remarkable journey of how the Fin
team transformed their approach to observing and operating Fin.ai, an AI agent
they designed to handle customer service interactions. While the initial customer
experience was characterized by slowness and frustration, the efforts detailed in this
chapter elevated Fin to become a leading AI agent in the space. For Fin, the core of
this turnaround was finding ways to measure customer experience and connecting
every change, experiment, and optimization back to that one measurement.
This chapter is adapted from the original delivery of this story as a conference
presentation. We’ve tried to maintain as much of Kesha’s original voice as practical in
this adaptation.

When our company, formerly known as Intercom, embarked on building Fin, our
main goal was nothing short of creating a truly revolutionary customer service agent.
The main job of such an agent is to answer end-user questions on our customers’
behalf. In the age of AI, Fin’s customers are true pioneers: they are transforming their
support operations to offload repetitive and mundane conversations to Fin while
freeing up their support teams to work on more involved and complicated requests.
Once Fin answers a question while chatting with the end user, we consider that con‐
versation resolved. Hence, the percentage of conversations that Fin is able to resolve
when engaged is the most important efficiency metric—we call it the resolution rate.

Increasing Resolution Rate
Naturally, our customers are incentivized to increase Fin’s resolution rate by provid‐
ing high-quality content that Fin can use to provide accurate answers. On the other
hand, we push ourselves to increase Fin’s efficiency because we only charge our
customers for the conversations that are, in fact, resolved.
These “aligned incentives” are a secret sauce that makes Fin a top-tier customer
service agent. Since Fin launched in March 2023, its resolution rate has steadily
increased by about one percentage point per month, as shown in Figure 22-1. And
this is not some marketing data tweaked to make Fin appealing; this is an honest
signal averaged across all of the customers using Fin.




Figure 22-1. Fin’s average resolution rate shows a steady month-over-month increase in
its resolution rate.

Resolution rate is not an easy metric to improve. We had to evolve Fin from a single
LLM call into a complicated agentic system that follows customer guidance, invokes
actions, and completes tasks.
At any given time, we have dozens of A/B tests running in production that are adding
or refining those powerful capabilities. Every individual test delivers only a fraction
of a percentage point of improvement to the resolution rate by itself. It is only by
stacking them up together over time that this steady growth has been achieved. Such
evolution comes at a cost: new functionality increases the time Fin takes to process
a query. While we kept an eye on Fin’s latency, we deliberately prioritized capabilities
and efficiency over speed.

Speeding Up Without Losing Efficiency
In early 2025, we started getting complaints from both existing customers and poten‐
tial buyers that Fin was slow. Despite our strategy, we took customer complaints
seriously and started an engineering effort to figure out how to speed Fin up without
affecting its efficiency.
To understand what happened, we have to take a look at what Fin is from the
engineering perspective: Fin is built on top of the existing customer platform on
Intercom, and is deeply integrated with existing support workflows. As a result,
Fin is a complex distributed system with multiple network transactions involved in
processing a given interaction. In the example shown in Figure 22-2, a simple user
question like “how do I activate my account?” creates several background processing
requests that utilize LLMs at various speeds in order to return a correct resolution.




Figure 22-2. Fin is a complex distributed system with multiple transactions necessary to
process any given interaction.

On top of this, Fin is constantly evolving. Hundreds of engineers from 20 different
engineering teams ship changes to the code directly involved in processing Fin’s
interactions. At Fin, shipping is our heartbeat—and even on a typical Friday, our
heartbeat is measured at ~100 individual changes shipped per day. This rate of change
is where the real complexity comes from.
As customer complaints surfaced, engineers got to work. Optimization pull requests
were flying left and right, with many charts indicating significant improvements in
Fin’s individual components. But our customers and prospects wanted more.
It became clear that while we were focusing on the performance of the individual
components, we lacked a signal representing the time that end users were waiting
for Fin to reply. To build deep empathy with our customers and to experience that
slowness in exactly the same way they did, we decided to surface an end-to-end
signal: the time from when the user message is submitted to the time the first tokens
of Fin’s reply are rendered in Fin Messenger.

And Thus, “Time to First Token” Was Born
Measured on the frontend, this “time to first token” accurately represented the
customer’s experience. Time to first token is a measure used to reflect customer
experience: it measures the time between when a customer submits their question
and when the first character in the reply gets rendered. In other words, this is the
time elapsed between when the customer asks a question and perceives that an
answer is being returned.
From then on, every change we made to the system had to positively affect “time to
first token,” as shown in Figure 22-3.
Our engineers could answer the question of what (e.g., “what is the median time to
the first token?”), but they couldn’t understand the why behind it, as they couldn’t
map that high-level signal to the code they were working on. Our new tracing data
lacked required details for the engineers to dig for the root cause.
While we were trying to empathize with our customers, we lost touch with our own
engineers. But failing is part of the process, and we did learn something from this
experience: we understood exactly why our initial attempts to optimize Fin’s speed
were not successful.

Here, every transaction’s “start” is aligned with the “end” of the previous transaction,
with the answer tokens streamed back to the user at the end. In reality, it’s a lot more
complicated, and the process looks more like Figure 22-5.
The work being done by the agent to start responding to the user doesn’t necessarily
align with the actual underlying transactions happening in each service.
As we improved the speed of individual components, we didn’t know which were on
the critical code path. Were they directly blocking the progress of Fin’s interaction,
or were they part of the processing done after Fin’s interaction moved to the next
transaction?




Figure 22-5. In reality, parallel transactions don’t have to fully complete in order for the
first token to return. Some transactions may start in the middle of the previous one,
based on control flows within the critical path, and some transactions may not be in the
critical path at all.

To understand a system like this, a distributed trace has to include low-level telemetry
to identify where exactly control flow is passed over to a different transaction and be
able to identify things that lie on the critical path. We had to start from scratch and
build new distributed traces with low-level spans attached to the transaction spans, as
shown in Figure 22-6. With the new traces, not only were we able to empathize with
the customers by answering the question of what; we also gave our engineers a way to
answer why.
Armed with this new telemetry, we were able to quickly move forward and meaning‐
fully improve Fin’s latency.

Speeding Fin Up
Let’s take a look at one of the most impactful optimizations we’ve implemented: eager
requests.
Unsurprisingly, the most time-consuming part of Fin’s interactions is the LLM-based
agentic workflow making calls to multiple models. But even before interactions reach
that stage, there’s a bunch of work that has to be done, like storing end-user messages
in the database, progressing through customer-configured workflows, figuring out if
Fin has to be engaged in this given conversation, and so on.
What if we skipped that part and eagerly invoked LLM-based processing? There’s a
good chance “normal” request flows will alter the payload affecting the prompts. But
if it doesn’t, by the time we get to the AI-based part of the interaction, we might
already have an LLM-generated answer at hand, as shown in Figure 22-7.
As it turns out, in the majority of cases, the payload stays intact and we are able to
stream the response back to the end user quicker. This optimization alone helped us
to shave two seconds off the median time to first token.




                                                                      Speeding Fin Up   |   409
Figure 22-7. We created the concept of eager requests to prioritize time-consuming calls
to LLMs. Because they take the longest time to complete, prioritizing calls to LLMs in the
control flow allowed us to dramatically lower the time to first token.

But making Fin faster is only part of the job. The real challenge is to protect the speed
gains over time, especially given the rate of changes shipped on a daily basis. We
knew that things would degrade again and we needed a way to proactively manage
slowdowns.
Given the unpredictable availability of the models (provider outages, daily seasonal‐
ity, lack of capacity, etc.), a simple threshold-based alarm would be too noisy. We
decided to adopt an event-based SLO that tracks the percentage of interactions faster
than a newly established baseline, as shown in Figure 22-8.




Figure 22-8. Daily compliance of the time to first token SLO over seven days.

Focusing on our long-term trends of SLO compliance helped us stay focused on
meaningful degradations and not be distracted by intermittent short-lived spikes.

Finance Enters the Chat
At this point, the story could have come to a happy end. We made our customers
happy by improving Fin’s latency, and our engineers had a signal they could trust to
surface real impact. That customer-centric signal was connected to the low-level data
they could use to immediately investigate any degradation detected by the breach of
the SLO.
However, there was still one problem to be solved with our eager-request optimi‐
zation. When a “normal” request confirms a payload match, we stream the reply
prepared in advance by the eager-request flow. But what happens if Fin was never
engaged in the processing of a given end-user message in the first place?
We allow our customers to configure sophisticated multistep workflows where Fin
is one of many steps. But sometimes, customers want to route a given conversation
straight to a human based on certain conditions (e.g., sensitive topic around pay‐
ments). In this case, the normal flow halts and all the LLM requests emitted as part of
eager-request flow are wasted, as shown in Figure 22-9.




Figure 22-9. Users can configure workflows that bypass the use of Fin. In those cases,
eager requests may still be emitted as part of the control flow, but those tokens are
unused. This was a malfunction of the eager-request implementation.

As there was no Fin interaction in processing that end-user message, no latency
metrics were emitted. As a result, this eager-request malfunction was flying under the

radar. Engineering learned of this only after the finance team did a quarterly review
detecting a decrease in Fin’s gross margins. We were so focused on Fin’s latency that
we lost touch with our own business by incurring this extra operational cost.
Luckily, thanks to the great tracing we already had, we were able to identify and
fix the issue, quickly surfacing all cases where there was no need to trigger eager

using to quantify the extra LLM cost, as shown in Figure 22-10.




Figure 22-10. We were able to query our trace data to identify situations where eager

were able to quantify cost and use that to identify when that was happening.

We were happy to observe the LLM waste percentage going down, confirming our fix.
But we also wanted to stay on top of this signal.

The problem with the aforementioned query is that it takes minutes to run. More

Engineers need real-time feedback on their actions. That’s an important precondition
to being able to safely ship hundreds of changes per day. So, we added Fin’s cost per

Of course, we put an SLO on it to make sure we were notified of degradations to Fin’s
cost.

Empathy
Our customers were happy, as we were able to empathize with them and experience
Fin’s speed in exactly the same way they did. We also built empathy with the business
by surfacing a business-centric signal. Fin’s cost per interaction strongly correlates
with cost per resolution, which we ultimately charge our customers for. Crucially,
both customer- and business-centric signals are attached to highly detailed telemetry
that enables engineers to investigate and fix issues, making them happy too.
One might think our experience and learnings are specific to us, but in fact, it’s easy
to generalize this approach.

The Rasmussen safety model, shown in Figure 22-12, explains how sociotechnical
systems can drift toward failure (for more about sociotechnical systems, see Chap‐
ter 24).




Figure 22-12. The Rasmussen safety model triangle (or Rasmussen’s “drift to danger”
model) is a conceptual diagram that illustrates how systems move toward failure. It
represents the, usually invisible, boundary of safe operation and how constant organ‐
izational, financial, and efficiency pressures cause operators to “drift” toward, and
eventually cross, this boundary.

The model argues that every system operates within three boundaries:
Boundary of acceptable performance
   Cross that boundary, and the performance/availability of your system is affected,
   making your customers sad.
Boundary of economic failure
   Cross that boundary, and your business profitability is at risk.
Boundary of unacceptable workload
   Cross that boundary, and you risk putting too much pressure on your engineers.
For a regular operator, those boundaries are elusive and rarely understood. But they
do exist! Your customers will complain when things get too slow. Your finance team
knows exactly where the economical failure boundary is; they already promised that
gross margin to your investors. And finally, unless you equip your engineering team
well, they might crumble under pressure by not being able to increase performance
and reduce cost.

That’s where observability takes a central role. The art of observability is to surface
those safety boundaries to the operators and create a real-time feedback loop that
empowers them to take risks and know exactly where they are in the Rasmussen tri‐
angle. Practically speaking, the art of observability is to create a dataset that combines
a customer-centric signal with a business-centric one. Those signals must be updated
in real time and connected to highly detailed, low-level tracing telemetry that enables
operators and engineers to proactively monitor and fix issues as they arise.
This is the last mile of observability, and arguably, the hardest one. Many teams focus
too much on instrumentation, telemetry pipelines, and vendor management, and
overlook this. It’s easy to get bogged down with implementation details and miss the
forest for the trees. That’s where observability becomes just another cost center, where
the only conversation you can have with your finance team is about the cost of your
logging cluster.
Instead, if you walk the last mile, observability could be strongly associated with your
customers’ happiness and business success. The good news is that, to do this, the
only thing required is empathy. You have to deeply empathize with your customers,
business, and engineers. Understand their needs. Feel their pain the same way they
do.
As ironic as it sounds, in the age of AI, empathy is all you really need.

Conclusion
Kesha’s story of engineering at Fin embodies what the rest of the book has been
building toward: observability as a practical discipline that changes outcomes for
real teams in real systems. Rather than treating instrumentation, tracing, SLOs, and
iterative analysis as separate topics, Kesha’s story shows how they become a single
operating model that ties every change, experiment, and optimization back to a
customer-centric measurement. It also makes that measurement debuggable enough
for engineers to act on it quickly.
This story also demonstrates the “last mile” of observability: building empathy with
customers, engineers, and the business by surfacing the right signals, in real time,
with enough low-level context to explain why those signals moved. Organizations
learn how to ship fast without drifting into danger because they can see the bound‐
aries of acceptable performance, economic viability, and operator workload. With
that, they can course correct before pressure pushes them across the line. This story
is a concrete example of how modern engineering teams turn observability into a
learning system.
In the next part of the book, we’re going to look at how to create top-down organiza‐
tional alignment to make these types of outcomes possible.

                             Observability Governance




So far, this book has been written for engineers: the people instrumenting code,
running production services, and trying to understand what their software actually
does in the real world. Parts I–V are written from the practitioner’s perspective,
bottom up.
Part VI switches gears. We now write for technical leaders.
When we asked the software engineering community what was missing from observ‐
ability guidance, we heard the same stories over and over: millions of dollars spent
on tools nobody learned to use, CTOs who’d written observability off as irrelevant
in a post-AI world, and organizations where nobody could agree on basic questions—
what observability even is, what problems it’s supposed to solve, or who’s responsible
for it. That’s a leadership problem, not a tooling problem.
Technical leadership comes in many forms—staff, principal, and distinguished engi‐
neers, as well as directors, vice presidents (VPs), senior VPs, CTOs, and similar roles.
We don’t think this is solely the domain of executives. Technical contributors shape
these decisions too, and we hope that comes through in these chapters.
The biggest problem we see right now is a widening gap: organizations are shipping
code faster than ever, and simultaneously losing their grip on what that code actually
does. Part VI is about closing that gap—through better decisions, better feedback
loops, and leadership that takes both the technical and human sides of that problem
seriously.
Chapter 23 opens as a letter to CTOs. In the AI era, shipping code is no longer your
biggest constraint; now you are constrained by understanding, validating, and your
organizational learning speed. This chapter shows how much of that drag traces back
to unpaid sociotechnical debt, and how observability connects to paying it down.
Chapter 24 goes a layer deeper, using systems thinking to examine how change
actually moves through sociotechnical systems. Most organizations try to hire or buy
their way out of debt. It doesn’t work. This chapter shows what does work, and why
observability is a leverage point, not just a tool.
Chapter 25 helps you figure out what kind of feedback loops your organization
actually needs, and then connects those needs to the tooling landscape. It also cuts
through the AI noise to keep you focused on outcomes.
Chapter 26 walks you through building a business case for observability investment.
Is yours a cost center or a strategic investment? The answer changes what you buy,
how you manage it, and how you measure success.
Chapter 27 is a diagnostic. If your organization is feeling cost pressure on observa‐
bility tooling, that’s usually a symptom—you’re paying observability prices for moni‐
toring outcomes. This chapter helps you figure out what went wrong and how to
course-correct.
Chapter 28 is a guest-contributed chapter from Rick Clark, Global Head of Cloud
Advisory at UST. Rick gets practical about how organizational shifts actually happen,
and how to navigate the hurdles you’ll face trying to accelerate how fast your org
learns.
Chapter 29 examines the decision of build versus buy versus open source in detail—
the real trade-offs, not the vendor-pitch version.
Chapter 30 covers vendor partnerships. If you’re buying a solution, then building a
partnership matters almost as much as the technology. This chapter covers how to
design and run a proof of concept (POC) as well as how to build trust and credibility
with internal stakeholders, reciprocity, and influence with vendors.
Chapter 31 gives implementation guidance for the teams that actually drive observ‐
ability adoption across an organization: instrumentation patterns, org-wide rollout
considerations, and common environments like large enterprises and regulated,
highly secure industries.
Chapter 32 looks to the future. Now that the economics of code production have been
inverted, what will be the downstream consequences over the next few years, and
how can technical organizations prepare to meet them? We argue that it’s time for the
rigor to move from the code to the system.
