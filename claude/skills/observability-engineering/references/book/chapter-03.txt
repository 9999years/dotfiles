        The Origins of Observability in Software




In Chapter 1, we described observability as a property of system dependability and
the sense-making capability of complex systems. In Chapter 2, we talked about how
code crosses over from development to production, and how to safely test in prod.
This chapter is written by Charity Majors, in her voice and perspective.
It shares her retrospective on how observability entered the software industry, and
how it has changed over time. The popular understanding has diverged from the spe‐
cific, technical meaning it once had. This chapter is about recovering that meaning,
and reasserting the principles that matter most.
If you want to get straight to instrumentation and the practical bits, you have Chari‐
ty’s blessing to skip ahead.

An Introduction from Charity
Hi! My name is Charity, and I am the cofounder and CTO of Honeycomb. I come
from the infrastructure side of the house; I have been an operations engineer, site
reliability engineer (SRE), and database reliability engineer, working on systems like
Linden Lab, Parse, and Facebook, as both engineer and manager.
For 10 years now, I’ve had a front row seat to the industry’s attempts to make sense of
our complex, confusing, interdependent web of software, and I’ve contributed some
attempts of my own. It’s a bit ironic, because as an engineer I always hated monitoring
and dashboards, but I am no stranger to rage-driven development (it’s actually kind
of my thing), and I feel really lucky to be here.
Christine Yen—my Honeycomb cofounder—and I are often credited with inventing
or pioneering “observability” in software engineering. This is a distinction that has



                                                                                     39
always made me mildly uncomfortable, in part because it’s not entirely true,1 and in
part because the popular understanding is so different now.
But while Christine and I may not have been the first to use “observability” in the
context of software, we were the first to give it a technical definition, inspired by its
control theory meaning, and build a product to that specification.
When we first started exploring observability, all we knew was that we were trying to
do something different. We had tried all the leading monitoring and APM tools, but
they all suffered the same limitations: strict caps on cardinality to control costs, quer‐
ies that took minutes to run, and correlations that remained stubbornly manual and
guesswork. As engineers with experience in databases (me) and analytics (Christine),
we knew these limitations were not intrinsic to data, and we were confused why all
the vendors kept copying each other, iterating on a data model that worked so poorly.
We also knew we were building for software engineers, not infrastructure or oper‐
ations teams. Infrastructure is and was well served by the three pillars. Software
engineering was not, and still is not.
As generative AI has turned the cost model of software upside down, the question of
how software engineers can validate their code in production, swiftly and reliably, has
become more important than ever. Building may have gotten easier, but understand‐
ing the code we built has not gotten easier; if anything, it has gotten harder. The
solution, we are told, is agentic AI, but agents are only as good as their data. What AI
needs most—context, relationships, ontological grounding—are what the old model
sacrificed first.
What happened? Why would the entire tech industry standardize around a set of
“pillars” handed down from infrastructure? What can be done?

The Dominant Model of “Observability”
Is Just Monitoring, Rebranded
Thinking back 20 years ago, every tech company had an operations department,
and the ops folks ran everything: monitoring and logging, DNS & BIND, operating
systems and kernels, filesystems and file servers, LDAP (lightweight directory access
protocol) and Kerberos, datacenters and hardware, networking switches and routers,
and on and on it goes.

The first wave of cloud hit in 2006, when Google started hosting corporate email
and when Amazon Web Services (AWS) launched S3 and EC2. Over the next 15-ish
years, one by one, each of these operational tools and technologies we used to build
was replaced by a managed API, SaaS (software as a service), or PaaS (platform as a

accustomed to the idea that we could now buy operational excellence in a tool: we
didn’t need to invest in these skill sets anymore.
And then, in the 2020s, one of those operational skill sets—just one of them—appears
to have come furiously roaring back to life. Although we no longer talk about it as
“monitoring” or “logging.” These days, we call it “observability”—and it accounts for
20-30% of all infrastructure spending at many companies, a dizzying rise over the
past 15 years that shows no sign of slowing.
There’s a broad assumption in our industry that observability is just fresh branding
on top of monitoring. This is understandable, because all the monitoring tools
have just rebranded themselves as observability tools, and most teams are managing
observability as a cost center and an ops tool for infrastructure.
And therein lies the problem. Modern observability was never supposed to be an ops
tool for infrastructure; it was supposed to be a tool for developers to understand their
code in production. But that is not what it became.

We Lost the Fight to Define Observability
In 2016, when Christine and I started Honeycomb, we fought hard to give observabil‐
ity a technical definition. “Good observability means having the ability to understand
whatever is happening in your system, at any time, even if you’ve never encountered
this system state before,” we said. “To do that, you need tools that support high
cardinality, high dimensionality, an explorable interface, and the ability to visualize
events over time as a trace.”
We argued that observability was a tool for developers to follow their code into
production and understand it there, in rich, exploratory detail; and monitoring, or
logs and metrics, were for operations teams running infrastructure, and they were
complementary. You needed both.
We lost that fight, of course, in a landslide. The idea of observability started to get
traction in 2017, and got buzzy in 2018, and for a while it looked like it might stick.
Vendors had been telling their customers for years that handling high-cardinality
data was “impossible,” that it “couldn’t be done”; when we showed that wasn’t true,
engineers started getting excited. “Observability” tracks started popping up at main‐
stream conferences. Monitorama renamed itself to “Monitorama (+ Observability).”
O’Reilly asked us to write a book (this book!). But not everyone was on board.
Customers said they wanted observability, so vendors did what vendors do: they

rebranded. Pretty much everyone in the monitoring, logging, or APM categories
relaunched their existing products as “observability tools” between 2019 and 2021.
By the time Gartner added the “observability” category in 2022, the term had comple‐
ted its long slide into meaninglessness. The reasons engineers got so excited about
it in the early days—the reasons it was different—were long forgotten. Everyone
believes they have observability these days, because observability can mean anything.
On the one hand, it’s hard not to get a little depressed by this. The technical definition
we championed would have been really useful for all the people trying to understand
their options in a saturated market where every technical term is over-marketed.
On the other hand, the technological currents that pushed us to develop a new
paradigm for observability have only grown more intense. More and more teams are
having to learn the same hard lessons Christine and I learned about the ways your
relationship with production must be renegotiated after a step change increase in
system complexity.

Production Has Become Too Complex
for Us to Debug Via Intuition
Complexity has skyrocketed over the past 20 years. In 2010, the typical architecture
was some variation on the LAMP stack: Linux, Apache, MySQL, and PHP. Moni‐
toring and dashboards worked well in that world.
In 2026, there is no typical stack. Our systems are dynamic, ephemeral, and con‐
stantly in flux, a mesh of services, models, platforms, and APIs—and now (thanks to
AI) we have nondeterminism as a feature, and there are agents in the mix. You can’t
just hold a model of production in your head and reason about it anymore, or reliably
intuit the answers.
A good analogy for monitoring versus observability comes from finance. If you’re a
young person with a paycheck and a bank account, you don’t need any complicated
financial arrangements. Set up direct deposit, set up savings, autopay your rent
and utilities, and check your balance once in a while, and you’re golden. That’s
monitoring.
Now let’s say you’re a financial services provider, or a bank. You have to track every
single transaction going in or out of millions of accounts, pay and charge interest
at different rates, track assets and asset classes and depreciation, and manage tax
liability. You need to be able to drill down into any transaction between any set of
accounts at any time, on a moment’s notice, and trace the historical path of any
money that enters your system back until it exits the system. You need to set up
proactive fraud detection and compliance alerts. Oh, and if you get any of it wrong,
you could go to jail. That’s observability.

Does observability cost more than monitoring? Yes. Because it does more. Every

expensive to try to hobble along without it.

The Facebook Experience That Showed Us What’s Possible
Going back to the finance analogy: if you suddenly jump from a simple checking
account to running a large, complex financial institution with millions of transac‐
tions, how do you even know where to start? That’s not a common real-world
scenario, but it’s all too relatable in tech if you found yourself going from LAMP stack
to a massively distributed systems wasteland practically overnight.
Shortly before founding Honeycomb, Christine and I found ourselves living through
exactly that kind of painful shift. We’d been using tools built for simpler architectures
to tackle incredibly large and complex problems. Both of us, working separately on
Parse, had started shipping streams of data into a tool at Facebook called Scuba. And
that experience forever changed how we thought about software.
In our experience:

  • Novel problems that had been virtually impossible to find until you knew what to
    look for became possible to find in Scuba with no prior knowledge.
  • Investigations that had taken time and energy became fast, simple, and cheap.
  • Weirdest of all, the more junior engineers were starting to out-debug the experts
    who had built the system.

Entire categories of problems began to feel more like support tasks than engineering
problems. This was mind-blowing. After leaving Facebook, we couldn’t stop thinking
about how much we missed Scuba. We were convinced the world needed more tools
like it.
When engineers start companies, they typically fall into one of two groups: either
they are subject matter experts (SMEs) who know exactly what they want to build, or
they have an idea and need to find out if there’s a market for it. Christine and I were
neither. We didn’t even fully understand why Scuba was so transformational—we just
knew we missed it like crazy and couldn’t imagine going back to a world without it.
We had the Scuba whitepaper, so we started experimenting, unpacking our experi‐
ence, and sorting out what did and didn’t matter. Christine and I were not SMEs, and
most of the people we talked to had no idea what we were talking about (and even
less interest). We were repeatedly told by investors and leading experts that what we
were attempting was a “solved problem.”

Neither of us had any real background in this space. What we had was an experience
and a lot of conviction. So, we started reading and reasoning from first principles to
work out how to replicate the experience for others.

The Control Theory Definition Made It All Click
We knew what we were building was different—in workflow, storage, and signal
types—than anything else we had seen in the software domain. We couldn’t fit it into
any existing category of tools.
Oddly, it seemed to have a lot more in common with business analytics than with
anything used by software engineers or SREs. It wasn’t monitoring, it wasn’t logging,
it wasn’t tracing, it wasn’t APM—so what were we building? How should we talk
about it? What would make it click for people? That sent me off searching for new
technical terms. That’s when I read the control theory definition of observability:
     Observability is a measure of how well internal states of a system can be inferred from
     knowledge of its external outputs.

Suddenly, it clicked. Fireworks.
The control theory definition nailed what was different about what we were building.
With existing solutions, you had to know what you were looking for before you could
find it. You had to have knowledge of the internal state before you could infer what
was happening—a circular dependency. Observability broke that dependency.
If the goal is to understand any system state, at any given time, from the perspective
of any user or intersection of users, just by observing the system outputs…what
would that take?
Working backward, we derived six functional requirements (these may look familiar
to you from Chapter 1):

 1. High-dimensionality data: wide, wide structured events organized around a unit
    of work. All the context. Ideally, hundreds of attributes per event.
 2. High-cardinality data, preserving as much detail as you want. Yes to unique IDs!
 3. Store raw events and aggregate at read time, with flexible, inferred schemas and
    indexes. Pre-aggregation is a one-way street, and every key should be fast to
    query.
 4. Zoom in to individual requests, and zoom out to the whole system, or any level
    of granularity in between. Visualize events over time as a trace.




44   |   Chapter 3: The Origins of Observability in Software
 5. Exploratory, open-ended user interfaces you can interact with, instead of static
    dashboards.
 6. Queries must return within seconds, not minutes, so it is fast and interactive.

Traditional tools have always been good at known-unknowns. Once you know you
have a problem, you can generally find what you’re looking for. The tools work well
for infra monitoring, and well enough for finding and fixing bugs and dealing with
incidents.
What modern observability introduced was the ability to understand unknown-
unknowns. To evaluate the quality of your product, and understand the experience
each user is having. To follow your code into production and see how users interact
with it, to be surprised by what they come up with. Modern observability is about so
much more than just bugs and incidents. It’s about understanding.
This new approach was useful to software engineers for developing and validating
their code. That was the shift that took observability from a monitoring tool for
infrastructure to the sense-making capability of software systems—a distinction I
sometimes worry has gotten lost along the way.

The Modern Observability Landscape Is
Confusing and Continues to Expand
Then again, maybe not. After all, Honeycomb is no longer the only company with
a product that handles high-cardinality, high-dimensionality data with an explorable
experience. In fact, every observability startup founded since 2019 (that we know
of) has followed our recipe: wide, structured events, backed by a columnar data
store. When Honeycomb was starting out, there were no publicly available columnar
storage engines, so we had to write our own. Now there’s DuckDB, ClickHouse,
Snowflake, and more, and a new crop of observability vendors built on top of them.
Not that you’d know it just by looking at their websites: the three pillars, infrastruc‐
ture monitoring type tools use all the same words and phrases as the structured
events backed by columnar database–type tools. In 2024, I took another stab at
differentiation via technical terms. Regrettably, I used the most hackneyed technical
cliché of all: 1.0 versus 2.0:
Observability 1.0
   This is when you send each type of signal to a separate tool or storage system.
   Metrics go to a time-series database, logs go to a log aggregator, traces go to a
   tracing tool, profiling data goes to a profiling tool, and so on. Any correlations
   between pillars must be predefined and maintained by hand. You pay to store it
   many, many times.


                           The Modern Observability Landscape Is Confusing and Continues to Expand   |   45
Observability 2.0
   This is when you send your telemetry to one unified storage location. You send
   rich, structured data that preserves the relationships for you to interrogate later.
   You pay to store it once. In many tools, you can visualize the events over time as a
   trace, or slice and dice them like normal log events. No dead ends.
Simple, right? You can either have one source of truth or many sources, but you can’t
have both. It’s clarifying. Recent to the time of writing in early 2026, Hazel Weakly
proposed identifying data lakehouse architectures as “observability 3.0,” and there is,
inevitably, already an “observability 4.0” on the loose.
This escalating arms race in version numbers is, of course, the inevitable downside of
the numbering exercise, which is why I have mostly stopped using it. (It also made
traditional observability vendors angry. Some feel the “1.0” label implies they are less
advanced. They are not less advanced; they are simply built for a different use case.
But I get it.)

Costs Are Driving the Need to Change,
and AI Is Enabling That Change
My personal opinion is that the world is moving, slowly yet inexorably, toward
the unified model. To be clear: this is my personal opinion, not the vendor-neutral
authorial stance of the “we” voice elsewhere in this book.
Why? One big reason is cost. If you’re using 10–20 observability tools—and accord‐
ing to Gartner, that’s the norm—you’re paying 10–20x in costs for every request that
enters your system, at minimum. That cost multiplier is not sustainable.
Teams have been hemorrhaging their most valuable data—cardinality and context—
in an attempt to cap cost growth. It’s simply the wrong data model for the job.
When the distinction between observability and monitoring got lost, the losers
were developers and finance. Developers, because production remains as hostile
and opaque to their needs as ever. Finance, because when you’re trying to meet
developers’ needs using infrastructure tools, you end up paying to store the same data
over and over and over again, in slightly different formats. And then you end up
paying over and over and over again to store data that bridges the gap between those
formats, store different data types in different ways, and so on.
The other big reason is AI, which attacks the status quo on multiple fronts at once:

     • AI is a technical enabler that can close the gaps that have made production feel
       uncomfortable to developers.
     • Modern observability has been hamstrung by the ecosystem built around three
       pillars, but the AI ecosystem is springing afresh.


46    |   Chapter 3: The Origins of Observability in Software
  • AI is a competitive forcing function for execs to invest in reliability and
    guardrails.
  • AI agents are gluttons for context, and AI is only as good as the data it’s trained
    on.
  • Nondeterminism is a feature. Developers who want to understand agents will
    have to instrument them and observe in production.
  • Our ability to write code is no longer the bottleneck. Now, the bottleneck is our
    ability to understand what we have written and make decisions about what to do
    next.

So, what do we do with all of this?

Distilling the Lessons That Matter
From a computer science perspective, none of this is new. The business side of
our organizations went through this exact transformation 20-odd years ago when
columnar storage engines were first introduced. Suddenly, analysts went from having
to predefine metrics and dashboards to being able to slice and dice and zoom in and
out, exploring data, finding new trends on the fly. They were hooked.
Sales and marketing have always paid top dollar for great data tools because it means
the difference between profits and losses. For engineering, that argument was never
quite as clear and straightforward. And of course, engineering has long relied on
tools that were selected, bought, rolled out, and maintained by infrastructure teams.
Today, most of the tools that handle software telemetry get bucketed under “observ‐
ability.” Some of them enable engineers to understand the quality of their product
from the perspective of any or each of their users, as we have described. Most do
not. The yawning gap between the cost of observability and the value returned is a
function of this mismatch.
The cornerstone of every high-performing engineering org is fast feedback loops, and
you get those from observability—the sense-making function of production systems.
That function has a compounding ripple effect. The more fast feedback loops that
engineers have, the deeper and more meaningful the questions they ask. When they
consistently find good answers quickly and easily, your systems and teams shift
toward operating more sensibly and transparently. Your ability to ship swiftly, with
confidence, picks up real speed. (We discuss this flywheel extensively in Chapters 24
through 25.)
That concept is not well understood by most engineering leaders right now—execs,
managers, staff, and engineers alike. Probably because very few teams are actually
operating anywhere near their theoretical peak potential.



                                                        Distilling the Lessons That Matter   |   47
But some are. This is not theoretical. And I’m actually uncharacteristically optimistic
that more leaders are beginning to understand the role that observability plays in
unlocking that potential. Leaders are waking up to the fact that their AI investments
pay off only after they first invest in reliability and observability.
At an individual level, observability is a force-amplifying power. It makes you better
at everything else you do and connects you with the impact of your work on other
people. When you understand exactly how your code behaves, what it’s really doing,
and how that translates into each user’s experience. That is what gives meaning to the
work of software engineering.
Every company needs somebody who understands both observability and systems
thinking and can make good choices—or else every migration, feature, deploy, and
decision will be longer, harder, and more painful than necessary. Attention is the
scarcest resource at every tech company right now, and the bottleneck has shifted
from writing code to understanding the code we write. We don’t need everyone
to understand observability deeply from first principles. But every company needs
someone, or some team, who does.
If that person is you: good luck. My hope is that this book can help lead you to
success.

Conclusion
Charity’s history of observability can be read in many ways. The takeaway we most
hope you leave with is an understanding of the user experience that sparked the
breakthrough—the moment that made “observability” ubiquitous.
The six functional requirements captured by that experience illustrate what separates
a newer school of thought from more traditional debugging approaches. We think
this is the most misunderstood lesson in observability—and that misunderstanding is
largely responsible for the disillusionment that sets in when the costs don’t seem to
match the value.
The rest of the book unpacks this in detail: technical implementations, use cases, and
guidance for different roles and team types. If you haven’t already, the book’s preface
can help you figure out which sections matter most for you.
