   Diagnosing Your Observability Investment




If you’ve felt growing pressure to “cut observability costs,” you’re not alone. Observa‐
bility spend has climbed steadily for years. According to Gartner,1 the cost of observa‐
bility has been rising at least 40% per year for the past 15 years straight. Observability
is now the second largest software line item in R&D organizations—second only to
cloud—and most orgs spend somewhere between 10–25% of their infrastructure bill
on observability tools.
In the last chapter, we talked about how to make the business case for observability.
It’s rarely pleasant when leadership or finance starts asking for justifications. But cost
pressure can be useful when it forces us to ask hard questions, like is our investment
working?
That question often gets misread as “can we justify this line item?” It shouldn’t.
The actual question is, “did we buy the right capabilities, and are we getting the
right returns?” Many organizations are experiencing a disorienting mismatch—for
example, paying for observability as if it were a strategic capability while operating it
like a cost center and using it like monitoring. If you aren’t getting the returns that
you feel you should based on what you’re paying, you might be right.
In this chapter, we’ll show you how to diagnose your observability expenditures.
If you decide to invest in better observability, it may cost you. Or it may not:
outrageously high bills are the most common consequence of poor tool fit, so you
might actually pay far less to get better results. Either way, you should be able to point
to real outcomes: tighter feedback loops, richer understanding, faster learning.

Before you start slashing costs, start with a proper diagnosis: is your observability
delivering on the capabilities you need it to?

Don’t Pay Observability Prices for Monitoring Outcomes
In the last chapter, we identified two different feedback loops to focus on, depending
on your organization’s biggest bottleneck. These loops require different investments.
That doesn’t necessarily mean they require different tools or products,2 but they do
need to be considered and evaluated as different problems:
Operational loops
   Protect system stability: detect, respond, recover, prevent recurrence.
Developer learning loops
   Accelerate learning and validation of code shipped: deploy, observe, learn, adjust.
These loops are complementary, and most organizations need both. But they repre‐
sent different priorities and require different capabilities. A common failure mode is
conflating them: buying tools and building practices that mainly support operational
workflows while expecting the learning gains that only come from developer learning
loops. In Chapter 23, we called this “observability prices for monitoring outcomes.”
If your focus is operational outcomes, the right governance model is a cost center.
Cost centers optimize for reduced spend, predictable overhead, and coverage. These
budgets get squeezed when money is tight. Unfortunately, if operational stability is
your biggest priority, those cuts can be genuinely harmful.

The Firefighting Trap
There’s a trap that teams fall into when they overprioritize operational loops. Teams
that pride themselves on their firefighting and incident management often can’t
explain why fires keep starting. Incident response expertise is valuable, but opera‐
tional feedback loops are the longest, lossiest, and most expensive kind in terms of
downtime, customer impact, and lost revenue (see Chapter 25).
Alerts and escalations cannot be the only way you learn about your systems. Opera‐
tions should be your goalie, the last line of defense when everything else fails. If your
goalie is making 50 saves a game, your defense is not working.3

An Investment Posture Mismatch
With developer learning loops, the right governance model is strategic investment.
Strategic investments optimize for capability growth, learning speed, and competitive
advantage. They’re evaluated by what they produce, and they’re much easier to
defend when money is tight.

               A useful diagnostic question for strategic investments: does it pro‐
               duce organizational capabilities that affect business metrics?




It’s worth reiterating that neither the cost center nor the strategic investment gover‐
nance models are wrong. But you can’t get faster development cycles or learning
speed outcomes with cost center governance. This mismatch is what creates the
frustration that leadership and finance are expressing when they pressure you. We’ll
spend the rest of this chapter examining what to ask in order to diagnose whether
your observability investment is working.

Activities Versus Learning
When engineering teams make strategic investments, it’s common to take on a set of
new activities meant to change outcomes. But there’s a meaningful difference between
activities and learning. Activities are the specific tasks, methods, and resources your
teams use day to day. Learning is the cognitive change, the skill acquisition, and the
actual knowledge gained from those experiences. Activities are the means; learning is
the outcome.

When feedback loops are long, laggy, and lossy, you lose the mechanisms that enable
learning. Many “productivity” investments turn into activities and reliability theater
instead of genuine learning:

  • Chaos engineering without attribution becomes disruption.
  • Feature flags without validation become complexity.
  • Progressive delivery without detection becomes a slower deployment pipeline.

In each case, observability closes the loop by providing attribution, validation, and
detection. That closing is what turns activities into learning. You don’t capture
the value of your productivity investments until your organization actually learns
something.

                     Useful diagnostic questions here: For each practice you’ve invested
                     in, can you point to the feedback mechanism that turns it into
                     learning? What percentage of the time is it being used?




How to Know If Your Investment Is Working (or Not)
The dual mandate from the last chapter—internal measures for productivity, external
measures for core differentiators—is what you used to make the business case for
observability. How it gets implemented will vary by organization. If you don’t have
much experience translating technical outcomes into business needs, that can be a
real challenge. However, understanding if your investments are paying off can be
relatively simple.
For operational loops, your investment is working if:

  • Time to identify known causes is decreasing.
  • Alert quality is improving (fewer false positives, more actionable ones).
  • Incident escalation rates are dropping.
  • Recovery is getting faster.

For developer learning loops, your investment is working if:

  • Engineers can answer novel questions about production.
  • Deployment frequency is going up while batch sizes are coming down.
  • More engineers can debug independently, not just the same few people.

  • Time from hypothesis to validation is shrinking.
  • The number of issues found by engineers is going up, while the number of issues
    that customers find and escalate via support is decreasing.

If your observability spend has gone up but none of these indicators have improved,
or the set of indicators you were targeting have not improved, then the investment
isn’t working.
High spend with few returns over a long period is usually what triggers questions
from finance. If spend is being questioned and you can’t point to capabilities or
concrete improvements, the uncomfortable answer might be: the investment didn’t
deliver.
When an observability investment doesn’t deliver, it usually isn’t a communication
problem. It’s evidence that something needs to change—tooling, instrumentation,
organizational approach, or all three.

Advice for Observability Investments
Beyond the diagnosis of your current observability investment, the following advice
provides a set of targeted, practical steps you can take to proactively manage it as an
investment instead of a cost center:
Identify the point of diminishing returns on each investment.
    Yes, track and manage the cost of your telemetry. But also ask, “Where can
    we make targeted investments in observability that will unlock disproportionate
    business value?” This means understanding your specific differentiators, compet‐
    itive position, and strategic priorities. For every targeted investment you make,
    figure out where the point of diminishing returns sets in and cap it there.
Quantify your gaps in visibility.
   If you have experienced downtime or outages as a direct consequence of your
   weak observability or inability to connect cause and effect, consider preparing
   an impact statement. An impact statement is a comprehensive assessment that
   goes beyond immediate revenue loss to quantify the full financial, operational,
   and reputational damage caused by a system interruption. These are designed
   to expose “hidden” costs, such as employee downtime, SLA penalties, customer
   churn, and so on.
Implement telemetry pipelines with tiered service.
    This is the only cost-effective way to scale a heterogeneous environment, where
    not all services are equally important. Consider defining at least two tiers of
    service, “low” and “high” (most companies end up adding a third which is
    somewhere in the middle):


                                                       Advice for Observability Investments   |   483
          1. Services in your critical path might be defined as ones that are revenue
             generating, customer facing, or under active development. They get wide,
             rich, distributed traces or canonical log data at a high tail-based sample
             rate—that’s the “high” level of observability.
          2. Services that are stable, decoupled, and not customer-facing might just get
             end-to-end health checks and a synthetic or two—that’s the “low” level.
             Control the verbosity and telemetry level with feature flags, so you don’t have
             to redeploy your instrumentation just to change which signals are emitted.
Invest in attribution.
    We previously referred to this as “observability for your observability.” Cost is a
    vital component of architecture, and so are tokens. One of the most wonderful
    aspects of the modern, unified telemetry model is that you can pack hundreds
    and hundreds of attributes into every single trace or event. Tag every request,
    session, or transaction with the cost of running it, which line of code it came
    from, which normalized queries it executed, and so on.
There are many, many creative uses for attributes and attributions, but one of our
favorites is assigning a budget to each team and letting them track and manage their
own observability budgets. As we said in Chapter 24, good observability enables
self-awareness in humans and self-governance in teams. This is a good example of
how that works.

Conclusion
If you’re hearing “we need to cut observability spend,” that’s a signal that’s less about
cost and more about outcomes. Cost pressure is often the moment that organiza‐
tions are forced to ask the question they should have been asking all along: is the
investment working? Remember, you don’t have to justify a line item: you need to
demonstrate that capabilities are materializing for your organization. Are those dol‐
lars converting into tighter feedback loops, faster understanding, and faster learning?
Cost anxiety most often arises from a mismatch in expectations—paying for observ‐
ability as if it were a strategic capability, while operating it like a cost center and
using it like monitoring. You must be clear on your organizational priorities. Are
you building operational loops that protect stability, or developer learning loops that
accelerate learning and validate change in production?
