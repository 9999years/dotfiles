 Getting Started with Observability Analysis




In Part II of this book, you learned about the fundamentals of instrumenting your
code to emit telemetry. But that’s only half of the equation. Collecting the right data
is a fundamental requirement, but the true measure of observability is what you can
learn about your systems from that data.
This chapter looks at how gathering the telemetry data you learned about radically
changes how you’re able to understand the behavior of your code. Working in high-
observability systems, the analysis techniques you’ll use differ drastically from those
you typically use in more traditional, low-observability systems.
We’ll start by examining the analysis techniques used in low-observability systems.
Those approaches heavily rely on prior system familiarity and experience for inves‐
tigators to successfully find answers. In contrast, with high-observability systems,
investigators start with a structured process—the core analysis loop—that leads them
to the correct source of issues. That difference results in both leveling the playing
field when identifying issues and quickly accelerating the fixing of them.
The core analysis loop can be accomplished manually, but it’s best when automated.
In looking at how to automate analysis, we’ll consider the roles that both humans and
computers play in creating effective debugging workflows. We’ll provide guidance
when using AI assistants to automate telemetry data analysis. By demonstrating these
analysis techniques, you’ll understand why systems with a high degree of observabil‐
ity let you identify issues that are impossible to detect with low-observability tooling.

Debugging from Known Conditions
Prior to modern observability tooling, system and application debugging meant that
you started from what you already knew. What does this particular threshold-based
alert tell me I should be investigating? What does “normal” look like on this dash‐
board I’m seeing, and how does that compare to what is currently displayed?
Think back to when you first joined a new team and knew little about the system you
were investigating. It’s likely you first needed to accumulate a fair amount of system
knowledge and understanding of what “normal” meant before you could jump into
an incident and lead the investigation.
When you’re new, it can seem downright magical when the most senior members of
an engineering team approach their troubleshooting workflows. Somehow, they just
know which questions are the right ones to ask. They instinctively know the right
places to look. But that intuition isn’t magic; it’s more like accumulated scar tissue.
With repeated exposure to previous system failures, they’ve memorized the various
failure modes, the weird edge cases, and the dashboards or graphs that usually point
at the right places to look.
Engineering team managers often understand that phenomenon quite well, because
they’re usually the ones responsible for somehow bottling that “magic.” They urge
their senior engineers to write detailed runbooks in an attempt to identify and solve
every possible “root cause” they might encounter out in the wild. A scramble occurs
to document the escalating collection of dashboards the team has accumulated and
what each of them means.
Some documentation is essential: ownership, escalation, dependencies, and a few
solid entry-point queries. But the time spent creating those runbooks and document‐
ing dashboards is largely wasted in vain, because modern systems rarely fail in pre‐
cisely the same way twice. And when they do, it’s increasingly common to configure
an automated remediation that can correct that failure until someone can investigate
it properly.
Anyone who has ever written or used a runbook can tell you a story about just how
woefully inadequate they are. Perhaps they work to temporarily address technical
debt: there’s one recurring issue, and the runbook tells other engineers how to
mitigate the problem until the upcoming sprint when it can finally be resolved.
More often—especially with distributed systems—a long, thin tail of problems that
almost never happen are responsible for cascading failures in production. Or, five
seemingly impossible conditions will align just right to create a large-scale service
failure in ways that might happen only once every few years.

                            On the Topic of Runbooks
 The assertion that the time spent creating runbooks is largely wasted may seem a bit
 harsh at first. To be clear, some documentation you’ll find in runbooks is essential.
 For example, for every service, you should capture basic information, including
 which team owns and maintains the service, how to reach the on-call engineer,
 escalation points, other services that this service depends on (and vice versa), and
 links to good queries or dashboards that serve as entry points to understanding its
 performance.
 However, maintaining a living document that attempts to contain all possible system
 errors and resolutions is futile and dangerous. That type of documentation can
 quickly go stale, and wrong documentation is perhaps more dangerous than no docu‐
 mentation. In fast-changing systems, instrumentation itself is often the best possible
 documentation, because it combines intention (what are the dimensions that an
 engineer named and decided to collect?) with the real-time, up-to-date information
 of live status in production.


Yet engineers typically embrace these dynamics as “just the way that troubleshooting
is done.” After all, that’s how debugging has worked for decades. But is that the best
we can do?
Must we first intimately understand all parts of the system—whether through direct
exposure and experience, documentation, or a runbook? Then we look at our dash‐
boards and then we…intuit the answer? Or maybe we’re supposed to just make a
guess at the root cause, and then start looking through our dashboards for evidence
to confirm our guess?
Even after instrumenting your applications to emit observability data, you might still
be debugging from known conditions. For example, you could take that stream of
arbitrarily wide events and pipe it to tail -f and grep it for known strings, just
as troubleshooting is done today with unstructured logs. Or you could take query
results and stream them to a series of infinite dashboards, as troubleshooting is done
today with metrics. You see a spike on one dashboard, and then you start flipping
through dozens of other dashboards, visually pattern-matching for other similar
shapes.
Collecting better telemetry data isn’t what enables you to debug unknown conditions.
How you analyze that data is what makes all the difference.
At Honeycomb, it took us quite some time to figure this out, even after we’d built our
own observability tooling. Our natural inclination as engineers was to jump straight
to what we know about our systems. In Honeycomb’s early days, before we learned
to stop debugging from known conditions, what better observability helped us do

was just jump to the right high-cardinality questions to ask about the hundreds of
dimensions captured in our events.
If we’d just shipped a new frontend feature and were worried about performance,
we’d ask, “How much did it change our CSS and JavaScript asset sizes?” Having just
written that instrumentation, we would know to figure that out by calculating maxi‐
mum css_asset_size and js_asset_size, and then breaking down performance by
build_id. If we were worried about a new customer who was starting to use our
services, we’d ask, “Are their queries fast?” Then we would just know to filter by
team_id and calculate the P95 response time.
But that’s still debugging from known conditions. What happens when you don’t
know what’s wrong or where to start looking? What if you don’t have the faintest
idea of what could be happening? The key shift isn’t just jumping further along in the
process; it’s how you investigate when you don’t yet know the story.
When you don’t yet know the story, then instead you must debug from first
principles.

Debugging from First Principles
Debugging from first principles is the practice of treating your understanding as pro‐
visional until you can verify it with evidence. A first principle is a basic assumption
about a system that was not deduced from another assumption. In philosophy, a first
principle is defined as the first basis from which a thing is known. Debugging from
first principles means using a methodology to understand a system scientifically.
Proper science requires that you do not assume anything. You must start by question‐
ing what has been proven and what you are absolutely sure is true. Then, based on
those principles: you form hypotheses, test them against your telemetry, and repeat
until the explanation fits the observed behavior.
One of the most practical capabilities unlocked by modern observability is that
the investigative process becomes both methodical and teachable. While intuitively
jumping straight to the correct answer is wonderful, it becomes increasingly imprac‐
tical as complexity skyrockets (and it definitely doesn’t scale).
Instead of requiring everyone to become a system wizard for your team to achieve
velocity, you can rely on a repeatable loop that guides investigators toward the condi‐
tions that actually explain the problem. This capability becomes even more essential
in modern systems, where the sources of issues are often multicausal: what happens
when you’re wondering what went wrong, and the answer is “13 different things”?
That methodical and teachable loop is the foundation of analysis in high-
observability systems.

Using the Core Analysis Loop
The core analysis loop is hypothesis-driven debugging: propose, test, refine. Form
hypotheses, explore the data to confirm or deny them, and iterate based on what you
learn.
That approach is not only more scientific than relying on intuition and pattern-
matching, but it also democratizes the act of debugging. Using the core analysis loop
is more rigorous than intuition-based troubleshooting, as it tends to reward curiosity
and careful observation over pure system familiarity.
Success when debugging no longer skews mostly toward the most experienced engi‐
neers. Instead, anyone who is most curious or most diligent about observing their
code in production can become the best investigator. That means even someone with
very little knowledge of the system should be able to jump in and debug any given
issue.
Let’s look at how that occurs.
First, debugging from first principles typically begins when something signals that
system conditions aren’t normal; a customer report, a shift in performance, or an
alert. (For effective alerting, see Chapter 11.)
Regardless of the notification source, at this point you know that something is wrong
(e.g., slow), but you don’t know exactly what. Begin the process: query system teleme‐
try to form an initial hypothesis, validate or invalidate that hypothesis with data, and
iterate until you systematically arrive at an answer. Figure 8-1 shows the four basic
steps of the core analysis loop.




Figure 8-1. The core analysis loop.



                                                         Debugging from First Principles   |   139
The core analysis loop works like this:

 1. Start with the overall view of what prompted your investigation: what did the
    customer, anomaly, or alert tell you?
 2. Verify that what you know so far is true: is a notable change in performance
    happening somewhere in this system? Data visualizations can help you identify
    changes of behavior as a change in a curve somewhere in the graph.
 3. Within that change of the curve, search for common dimensions that exist there
    but that aren’t present in the baseline performance area (outside of the curve).
    Approaches to accomplish that might include:
      a. Examining sample rows from the area that shows the change: are there any
         outliers in the columns that might give you a clue?
      b. Slicing those rows across various dimensions looking for patterns: do any of
         those views highlight distinct behavior across one or more dimensions? Try an
         experimental group by on commonly useful fields, like status_code.
      c. Filtering for particular dimensions or values within those rows to better
         expose potential outliers.
 4. Do you now know enough about what might be occurring? If so, you’re done!
    If not, filter your view to better isolate this area of performance as your next
    starting point. Then return to step 3.

This loop lets you move forward without needing a preexisting mental model of
every subsystem. You can use it as a brute-force method to cycle through all available
dimensions until you identify which ones explain or correlate with the outlier within
the graph (but remember not to confuse correlation with causality). This method
holds true, regardless of how familiar you already are (or aren’t) with the system in
question.
Of course, that brute-force method could take an inordinate amount of time and
make such an approach impractical to leave in the hands of human operators alone.
An observability tool should automate as much of that brute-force analysis for you as
possible.

Automating the Brute-Force Portion of the Core Analysis Loop
The core analysis loop is a method to objectively find a signal that matters within a
sea of otherwise-normal system noise. The brute-force work in that loop is churning
through all of the noisy data to identify a signal that’s different. Computers are great
at that.
Rather than manually searching across rows and columns to coax out patterns, auto‐
mation can retrieve the values of all dimensions, both inside the isolated area (the
anomaly) and outside the area (the system baseline), diff them, and rank which fields
and values are most strongly associated with the anomalous behavior. Automating
that loop very quickly surfaces likely causal factors and gives an investigator the next
best place to look.
For example, you might isolate a spike in request latency and, via an automated core
analysis loop, get back a sorted list of dimensions ranked by how often they appear
within that area. You might see the following:

  • request.endpoint with value batch is in 100% of requests in the isolated area,
    but in only 20% of the baseline area.
  • handler_route with value /1/markers/ is in 100% of requests in the isolated
    area, but only 10% of the baseline area.
  • request.header.user_agent is populated in 97% of requests in the isolated area,
    but 100% of the baseline area.

At a glance, that highlights what’s different about the particular requests you care
about—whether that be one deviation or dozens. As a concrete example, we’ll look at
the Honeycomb BubbleUp feature to show an automated core analysis loop.
With Honeycomb, you start by visualizing a heatmap to isolate a particular area
of performance you care about. Honeycomb automates the core analysis loop with
BubbleUp: you point and click at the spike or anomalous shape in the graph that
concerns you, and draw a box around it. Then, BubbleUp computes the values of all
dimensions both inside the box (the anomaly you care about and want to explain)
and outside the box (the baseline), and it compares the two and sorts the resulting
view by percentage of differences, as shown in Figure 8-2.

This information has been tremendously helpful. We now know the conditions that
appear to be triggering slow performance: a particular type of instance in one particu‐
lar AZ is much more prone to very slow performance than other infrastructure we
care about. In that situation, the glaring difference pointed to what turned out to be
an underlying network issue with our cloud provider’s entire AZ.
Not all issues are as immediately obvious as this underlying infrastructure issue.
Often, you may need to look at other surfaced clues to triage code-related issues.
The core analysis loop remains the same, and you may need to slice and dice across
dimensions until one clear signal emerges, similar to the preceding example.
In this case, the example is drawn from an actual incident. We contacted our cloud
provider and were also able to independently verify the unreported availability issue
when our customers also reported similar issues in the same zone. If this had instead
been a code-related issue, we might decide to reach out to those users, or figure out
the path they followed through the user interface (UI) to see those errors, and fix the
interface or the underlying system.
This is why arbitrarily wide, structured events matter: you need enough context
to slice, dice, compare, and explain. Metrics typically cannot provide the necessary
dimensionality. Logs can sometimes be correlated into event-like structures—pre‐
suming you have correctly appended all the request IDs, trace IDs, and other headers,
and then done a great deal of postprocessing to reconstruct them into events. But that
still requires the ability to aggregate those logs at read time and perform complex,
custom querying against the aggregation.


                                                           Debugging from First Principles   |   143
In other words, even the manual version of the core analysis loop is unattainable
without the basic building block of arbitrarily wide, structured events. And if it can’t
be done manually within your observability tooling to begin with, then it can’t be
automated.
The core analysis loop is a useful exercise to practice manually, to better understand
exactly how the interesting causal dimensions get surfaced. However, automation
is much more practical. Automation doesn’t replace investigation. It removes the
repetitive, time-consuming comparison work so humans can focus on interpretation
and decision-making.
Because debugging from first principles does not require prior familiarity with the
system, automation of the core analysis loop can be done simply and methodically,
without the need to seed “intelligence” about the application being debugged.
That begs a question around how much intelligence should be applied to this number
crunching. Is artificial intelligence and machine learning the inevitable solution to all
our debugging problems?

Automating Analysis with Generative AI
In the first edition of this book, we wrote about the misleading promise of AIOps
(artificial intelligence for operations)—a term coined by Gartner in 2017 to describe a
functional intersection with observability that used machine learning to reduce alert
noise and improve anomaly detection. At the time, we wrote that AI could only help
if clearly discernible patterns existed and if it could be trained to use ever-changing
baselines to model its predictions. Back then, that was a training pattern that had yet
to emerge in the AI world.
We summed up the challenge of using AI as a tension between humans and
machines, suggesting that we let computers do what they do best (churn through
massive datasets to identify interesting patterns) and fuse that with what humans do
best (add cognitive context to the interesting patterns that sift signals from noise). But
now, in the era of generative AI, computers might arguably be able to do both. How
does that impact today’s considerations for automating analysis of observability data?
The pace of forward progress in the generative AI space is breathtaking, which
presents a challenge for this book: can we write guidance that might not be obviated
by the time you read this paragraph? With that in mind, instead we’ll focus on
process by discussing modes of analysis and how they intersect with the humans ulti‐
mately accountable for decisions and system reliability. We’ll also look at how you can
best prepare yourself for integrating agentic AI into your observability engineering
practice.

The telos of observability is ultimately understanding and, through that understand‐
ing, to improve. Agentic AI systems rely solely on humans for the sense-making
part of an investigatory loop—the “what” in “what are you trying to understand”
mentioned earlier. Thus, you should begin your consideration of agentic tools by
understanding where the AI sits in respect to the human: are the tools a copilot, a
commander, or a caretaker? Let’s explore these three personas so that you can identify
them.

Agentic AI Personas
The “copilot” sits alongside the human as they work to analyze an issue, investigate a
performance regression, or generate further hypotheses. The copilot may have access
to tools in order to independently execute tasks, or may use only what is provided
by the human operator. Ultimately, the operator is using the agent as an extension
of their knowledge and expertise. In return, the human must evaluate the output of
the agent and steer it toward the correct place to look for answers and hypotheses. A
practical example of this model is using ChatGPT or Claude Desktop to analyze stack
traces, pictures of distributed trace data, and code in order to generate new queries or
reason through function execution.
The “commander” is a step toward autonomy. In this mode, the human generates
tasks and goals for an agent, which then goes to implement or run them with less
oversight. A commander has access to tools, which enable the agent to write and
run code, access remote data sources, surf the web, or almost anything else. Goals
can be more fuzzily defined. For example, “Why is my service slow?” is a valid
question. The agent will use its abilities to both interpret the question and determine
an answer. Further, it can also offer solutions. An example of this type of agent is
Cursor or Claude Code. Humans may choose to provide direct step-by-step oversight
with these agents, or allow them to run in the background working on tasks, but they
provide a level of separation between the work being performed and the operator.
Commensurately, that separation also offers a weaker transference of understanding
to the operator.
Lastly, the “caretaker” becomes not only autonomous, but also unmoored from direct
human supervision. Rather than responding to queries or tasks, caretakers are passive
observers with strictly defined goals. For example, these agents may be initialized
with access to runbooks or other knowledge artifacts and placed in the path of alerts
or deployments with a set of rules: “After a deploy, check each endpoint with a new
feature flag, look up the ticket for that flag to understand why it exists, and see if
the goal was attained” or “When an alert fires, go collect all relevant information
and provide three hypotheses about what’s happening, and then post them in the
appropriate incident channel.”

These agents are not science fiction: state-of-the-art generative AI models and
machine learning technology puts them within reach, and interesting work is being
done in this area.1 Crucially, caretaker agents require a significant amount of knowl‐
edge transfer from humans to the agent as they are created or configured. The agent
must be taught about the idiosyncrasies of your system.

Using Agentic AI for Observability in Practice
Considering these types of agents points us to a fundamental question: where does
the context live? Is it in the mind of the human, or has it been properly transferred
from human to agent? These are somewhat nuanced situations that will change as the
technology evolves. However, let’s consider what is true, regardless, in each of these
cases.
Telemetry data quality is paramount. While a human may be able to cope with minor
differences in attribute patterns or field naming across different services, those same
subtle changes can have much larger implications to the quality of AI analysis. Does
the primary actor have enough context to cope? One thing to note in these personas
is that as the autonomy of AI increases, so does the amount of potential error—and
the degree of error compounds.
If you’re working with a copilot, you can just start a new session when the AI
loses the plot. A commander may take erroneous actions, but you’ll have some
opportunities for more immediate oversight. A caretaker that collates incident data
independently has many more opportunities for small interpretation errors to cas‐
cade into further service impacts, wasted time, loss of trust in tools, and even data
loss or corruption.
Humans still matter—a lot. Irrespective of mode, goal, or tool, humans still own the
outcomes. You will be responsible for the context the agent has, the goals that it seeks
to achieve, and the outcomes of its behaviors.
As you experiment with agentic AI for observability, remember to embrace failure
and uncertainty. A common critique of AI agents is that they’re wrong. We would add
some color to that critique. Often, it is less of a problem that the agent is wrong, and
it’s more that the agent is optimized for confidence.
A casual observer of this space may be under the impression that “hallucinations” are
a major issue in AI agents. There’s nuance to that take, and state-of-the-art models
have better mitigated hallucinations. Far more likely is that the agent makes a rational

That means it may take an agent more attempts to come to the same conclusion
about an issue than it takes for a human; the agent must freshly reconstruct the sys‐
tem state each time. It might run multiple “incorrect” queries on its way to building
a correct one. Here, your role as an operator is to ensure that the agent has the best
information available so that it may steer itself in the right direction.
Let’s illustrate that concept in practice. An AI agent observed a telemetry field named
slow_request that was always true when requests were over 300 milliseconds, and
otherwise false. There was no other metadata available to this agent about what this
field meant, and it could not determine any other immediately obvious cause.
The agent’s conclusion is that the requests were slow because of the slow_request
attribute; it posits that perhaps this attribute indicates a load test, or that it is possibly
used as a feature flag. In reality, this attribute simply indicates that the request took
over 300 milliseconds. After adding a description explaining why this field exists, and
why it would be true, the agent then omits it from its analysis.
The more autonomous that agentic AI becomes, the more critical it becomes for a
need to provide better context about the environment. AI agents have specialized
needs, but those needs are very human-like. AI needs accurate, succinct, and timely
documentation about the systems it’s observing in order to reach better conclu‐
sions. Generative AI agents need high-quality telemetry data—not just normalized
attributes and names, but semantic consistency, human-readable descriptions, and an
ability to quickly search and enumerate the telemetry space. They need the freedom
to investigate and iterate rapidly on hypotheses, but they also need guardrails and
checks against potentially destructive actions.
In short, many of the things that you can do to improve system analysis with agentic
AI also improves the ability of humans to perform the core analysis loop. (For a
deeper dive into this topic, see Chapter 10.)

Conclusion
Achieving a high degree of observability requires more than collecting the right
telemetry. It requires analysis practices that work under any degree of uncertainty.
The core analysis loop provides a repeatable, first-principles method for investigation
that works well regardless of the complexity of your system. It works incredibly well
for effective fault localization, but it can become very time-consuming for humans as
data volume grows.
Automation makes it practical to apply the core analysis loop in even the most com‐
plex systems. Whether automated programmatically or by pairing with AI agents, the

workflow can be greatly accelerated. But that only further emphasizes the premium
for collecting high-quality telemetry quality and capturing an abundance of system

Now that we’ve covered the basics for effectively analyzing observability data, we’ll

one proactively looking for anomalies in the system. Other times, those anomalies in
the system are the ones that come looking for you.
