The buying process for observability is broken. The world has changed so fast, and
process and alignment and structure have not caught up.
And observability is so inextricably interwoven with your company’s pace, feedback
loops, ability to see clearly and make good decisions, that if your observability
evaluation and procurement process is broken, your company is broken, too. These
decisions have ripple effects that linger on for years.
This chapter is not intended to be an intensive guide to the software buying process
from start to finish (which would be impossible anyway), but it does offer an intro‐
duction to the art and science of vendor engineering. We cover topics like leveraging
trust and credibility to extend your influence across organizational boundaries; driv‐
ing sociotechnical transformations from within; and building enduring, positive-sum
partnerships across industry and company lines.
It’s good for the company, it’s good for the industry, and it’s good for you. These
are some executive-level skills—one of the rare ways to earn them as an individual
contributor (IC)—and they are durable in a post-AI era.
Most software transformations fail. Each one is ridiculously unlikely to succeed.
Those that do succeed rely not only on technical excellence and top-down mandates,
but on trust and credibility.
In this chapter, we will give you some tools to get started. We’ll talk about who your
stakeholders are internally, and how you can build trust and credibility with them.
And we’ll talk about who your stakeholders are on the other side of the transaction,
and how you can build reciprocity with them.
We will also give you some guidance on the trickiest parts of the process and some
common pitfalls: how to communicate up to C-levels, how to design a meaningful

This may not sound like glamorous work, but it’s engineering: it’s hard, and it’s

Does Buying Software Even Count as Engineering?
There’s an unspoken hierarchy of tasks among software engineers, influencing who
we look up to as well as our leveling docs and promotion packets. At the top:
new code, new features, new products. Lower down are less glamorous tasks, like
refactoring, tests, and infrastructure. “Buying software” isn’t on the list—is it really
even engineering?
You bet it is. Or it can be, and it should be.
In fact, we will argue that vendor engineering is one of the most elite engineering
            1
disciplines, with an effort-to-impact ratio that is second to none.
The decisions you make about which observability vendors to partner with will affect
the productivity of every engineer and the experience of every customer. If you’re
hungry for impact, it’s hard to beat that.

The Difference Between Vendor Engineering and Buying Software
As you can see from Charity’s story, true vendor engineering is about leveraging
influence to move the industry forward, which is a significant step beyond simply
“buying software.” This evolution from mere purchasing to strategic vendor partner‐
ships helps explain a fundamental truth of our time: modern tech companies are
dramatically more productive than they were 20 years ago. There are a lot of reasons
for this, but one of the biggest is that software companies have gotten better at
leveraging each other’s work. This lets each of us specialize and make better products,
instead of every company building everything from scratch.
The biggest technical innovations are well-known: cloud computing! APIs! SaaS!
Sure. But there’s a whole messy middle of decisions we don’t talk about as much: how
we decide which companies to bet on, how to partner with them, and how to drive
outcomes that benefit everyone involved.
Easy to say. Hard to do.
Vendor engineering is about wielding influence without authority across company
and industry lines. Buying software just takes money, while vendor engineering
takes technical depth and breadth, strategic thinking, and a solid grasp of business
priorities as well as the levers to drive organizational change.

are skills that most engineers only get to practice or train in on the management

These are durable skills in an era where AI has begun to generate more lines of code

The best vendor engineers end up shaping entire industries, holding vendors to

What to Know Before You Start
It can be challenging to write about the software purchasing and procurement pro‐
cess, because it seems like every company has a different process, for every different
tool, every different time. That’s not entirely fair (larger companies in particular tend
to be better at imposing some rigor), but there’s no getting away from it: there’s a lot
of hyper-local variance.
This can be tough on vendors, but it’s great for you. You should be able to find an
entry point without much difficulty.

Find Local Guidance
Start with the docs. Your company probably has some existing policy documents to
structure and guide the software purchasing process.
But docs are never enough. Find the local experts, people who have done this before
and are willing to share their expertise.
If you’re a staff engineer who wants to grow into this kind of work, offer yourself up
as an apprentice to someone like this. Offer to run their errands and do legwork in
exchange for the chance to watch them up close and learn. This arrangement benefits
everyone.

Understand Your Role
If you’ve signed up to drive the evaluation and purchasing process, your job is to keep
the machinery moving. There’s a certain amount of project management involved,
which is hard to outsource. That means it’s yours.
Your purchase will likely flow through security review, legal review, and technical
architecture review. Each has different timelines, different concerns, and different
needs for context. Your job becomes connecting specialists on your side to the ven‐
dor, sharing context about why this purchase matters, helping reviewers prioritize,
and providing realistic timeframes about how long each stage will take.
The broad lesson: assume nobody else cares enough to keep the purchase moving.
You’re the sheepdog. Stay on top of every stage, provide regular updates to your
stakeholders, escalate when needed, and accept that this may consume significant
time.
Let’s take a look at each of those stakeholders, and what their department typically
needs to know or care about when it comes to a software purchase.

Mapping Your Stakeholders
Software purchases at scale involve far more stakeholders than seems reasonable,
each with legitimate but often conflicting concerns. Stakeholder management is one
of the reasons that vendor management has such a high difficulty score. You aren’t
just pushing packets around; your job is to understand.
For each stakeholder, ask yourself what are their needs, what are their red lines, where
are they coming from? The more you understand their point of view, the more you
can partner with them creatively to navigate obstacles. This is what makes vendor
engineering an engineering problem, not just project management.

Engineering
Most developers aren’t deeply opinionated about observability vendors. Few feel
strongly attached to specific tooling. This cuts both ways—less political resistance,
but also less organic enthusiasm unless you can demonstrate concrete improvements
to their daily work.
The key to winning engineering support is showing the tool solving their specific
problems. This means getting involved in their codebases, understanding their pain
points, and demonstrating genuine improvements to the things that hurt them today.
It’s hard for anyone who isn’t a software engineer to get other software engineers
excited about observability, which is one of the most compelling reasons for an



538   |   Chapter 30: The Art and Science of Vendor Partnerships
engineer to drive this process. Show, don’t tell. Find some champions; you’ll need
them. They exist. Follow the pain.

Management
Engineering leadership wants to know how a software purchase fits into the broader
technical direction, what opportunity costs you’re taking on, and how you’ll drive
adoption across the organization.
They typically control the organizational levers you will need to access in the migra‐
tion stage. Feed them written progress updates to keep the drumbeat going.
They need to know what you expect their teams to contribute, how this intersects
with their roadmap, and how much of their existing work they’ll have to deprioritize.
Ideally, you’ll have had a chance to rack up some major tech wins for them, so they
will be looking for excuses to prioritize this work instead of dragging their feet (we
will talk about building champions in a moment).

Finance
Finance stakeholders care about cost management, budget predictability, tooling
consolidation, ROI, time to value, and contract terms.
With finance stakeholders, you need to translate your purchase or budget requests
into concrete business outcomes. How does your proposal reduce customer-
impacting incidents, accelerate development velocity, or enable new capabilities that
drive revenue?2

IT/Ops and Security
Internal IT and security teams evaluate system alignment, cybersecurity implications,
compliance requirements, authentication integration, deployment complexity, and
ongoing support burden. In regulated industries, these concerns intensify.
For observability specifically, security teams often get heavily involved because of the
nature of data flowing through the system. Customer data, business logic, system
internals—all of it becomes visible in your observability platform. Security teams
need assurance about data handling, retention, access controls, and compliance with
regulations like General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) or industry-specific
requirements. (For more on handling highly secure instrumentation data and dealing
with compliance, see Chapter 31.)

Procurement
At large organizations, you’ll work with procurement specialists—and experiences
vary wildly. Some procurement departments are efficient partners who understand
their role is facilitating good purchasing decisions. Others seem designed primarily
to prevent purchases from happening, drowning you in process while providing little
actual value.
It doesn’t matter. Treat procurement like your friend. Find out what’s holding them
back, what their biggest pain points are, and see if you can help them out. See if
you can help deliver a win for them that looks great on their performance review.
Definitely do not exclude them or route around them just because they make your life
troublesome (even if that is what they do).

Communicating Up the Ladder
When decisions require C-level approval, you probably won’t be in the room. Struc‐
ture your case and put it in writing so your boss or executive sponsor can effectively
advocate up the chain. Frame everything in business outcomes rather than technical
details,4 as discussed in Chapters 27 and 28.
No matter how much your boss insists that artifacts don’t need polish, if there’s
any chance they’ll get escalated up the ladder, make them presentable. Executives
trust polished slides and documents far more than engineer-quality artifacts, however
technically sound.
Surprises kill deals. You don’t want anyone in that room to be surprised by anything
they learn there. Go overboard in making sure bad news gets surfaced early on and
dealt with, objections handled satisfactorily, stakeholders appeased, obstacles noted,
and contingencies planned for—no surprises.

Trust and Credibility with Internal Stakeholders
Mapping your stakeholders reveals the “who” and “what” of a purchase, but building
trust and credibility provides the “how” for actual execution. While these two terms
are often used interchangeably, they represent different currencies in an organization.
Understanding the distinction is the difference between being a strategic partner and
being just another petitioner.

Credibility Versus Trust
Credibility is built when you assert something is true, and it turns out to be true.
This is where your technical expertise translates into an asset. When you assert that
something will or will not work, do people believe what you say? If you say one thing
and someone else says another, who gets believed? It will speed things up if you are
credible, so be conservative in your claims, and be cautious about speaking for others.
Trust is built when you say that you have someone’s best interests at heart, and
your actions prove you do. This is rarely a technical consideration. When you need
someone to trust you, extend yourself first. Do something to demonstrate that you
understand their needs and concerns, and you’re willing to earn it.
Ideally, the change you want to drive is one that will genuinely make your stakehol‐
ders’ lives better. Find those points of overlap and prove them out. If they invest the
time to get creative in helping you solve your problems, will they also be indirectly
solving their own problems? That’s what we mean by “alignment.” If you can’t show
alignment with a stakeholder, the job gets harder. Then you are just a petitioner—one
of many.
Trust is grease in the machinery; it opens doors, attracts allies, and soothes the
organizational immune response. It builds up over time, increases with use, and can
be lent around like capital. One of the biggest reasons to work on improving your
credibility and trust is so you can lend yours to causes that you care about.

Building Trust with Stakeholders
How to build trust with stakeholders is not mysterious: figure out what makes them
win and help them achieve that. What OKRs (objectives and key results) do they
have, and which ones are they most worried about? Are there any chronic sources of
pain or threats that you can make go away?



                                             Trust and Credibility with Internal Stakeholders   |   541
Do some of the grunt work. Get your hands dirty. Facilitate key interactions that
could be helpful. If you already have influence in the org, put your name on any “this
will turn out well and I’m sticking my neck out to make sure it happens” type of lists
on their behalf.
With these stakeholders, you’re the supplicant. You represent more work, more risk,
and more exposure, and you are just one in a long line of questionable supplicants
they have to deal with. It’s on you to earn their trust.

Breaking Trust with Stakeholders
One of the most reliable ways to build trust is to tell the truth, especially when it
doesn’t portray you in the greatest light or if the news is bad. Surprises are a direct
breach of trust; they leave execs (and other stakeholders) feeling unmoored and
suspicious.
To navigate the setbacks effectively:

  • Never sit on bad news or big, scary numbers. When you learn something that is
    likely to upset someone, tell them right away.
  • Being forthcoming is twice as important if you bear any personal responsibility
    for the situation at hand.
  • Eliminate surprises. Again, surprises are a breach of trust. Bad news, big mis‐
    takes, and disappointment are par for the course; that’s life. But unexpected
    shocks at the C-level will likely tank a deal.

With internal stakeholders, remember that you are the supplicant: you need some‐
thing from them, and you will work hard to get it, but you are all on the same
team with the same overarching goal. The dynamic with your vendor relationships is
exactly the opposite.

Trust and Reciprocity with External Stakeholders
While internal stakeholders share your company’s overarching goals, the dynamic
with external vendors is reversed: they want to make a sale, and you want what’s best
for your company. Most vendor relationships are transactional, but high-functioning
partnerships operate like two teams within the same organization—a state only
reached through transparency and reciprocity.

Building Vendor Relationships
With your coworkers, you should assume you all want the same thing—for your
company to succeed. When there is conflict, it’s a disagreement in good faith about
how to achieve this.


542   |   Chapter 30: The Art and Science of Vendor Partnerships
You cannot assume the same of your vendors. They want something from you, and
they will work hard to get it. With your vendors, you should assume you want
different things. They want to sell you something. You want the best outcome for
your company. These things may converge, or they may not.
Remind yourself that vendors will be helpful and useful for exactly as long as you
appear to keep making progress toward their sale, and no further. If a better outcome
for you lies elsewhere, you are on your own to find it—and if they know you’re
looking, they may try to actively mislead you and thwart you. (Yes, this includes
lying.)
This is normal. This is capitalism. This is the working basis of most friendly, produc‐
tive contractual partnerships. It is not how all vendors operate, but it is the right
baseline assumption.

The Vendor Trust Coefficient
Over time, as you engage with a vendor and a sales team, you will build up a mental
model of how they operate and whether they care about what’s in your best interest—
almost like a trust coefficient for each vendor.
Will they answer you honestly, even when it reflects poorly on them? Do they actively
lie to you, or only mislead by omission? How much time do they devote to spreading
FUD (fear, uncertainty, and doubt) about other vendors, or trying to use you to set
“traps” for the competition (feeding you questions designed to elicit an answer that
favors them)?
In short: how shady do they act?
You will want and need their help. Vendors are experts in their field, after all, and
they’ve run a ton of POCs. There’s no reason not to lean on them. But:

  • Are they working to help you assess your critical needs, and to design, instrument
    and run a POC to exercise those needs, including the rough edges and hard
    cases? It’s not shady to help you figure out how to map your problems to their
    solution, or to show the value they can add.
  • Or, do they helpfully offer to do the work for you in ways that elide their
    product’s weaknesses or approximate your hardest problems? Do they reassure
    you that test datasets are just as good, performance is never a problem, or sway
    you away from use cases that might show their product in a bad light? That
    means they are actively working against your best interests.

Just because a vendor is a little shady, doesn’t mean you can’t work together profitably.
It just means you have to keep a closer eye on them. Double-check any information
they give you, especially about their competitors. (Alternatively, just assume that any


                                             Trust and Reciprocity with External Stakeholders   |   543
time they mention a competitor, they are lying and a little scared.) Make sure the
work is done to your specification, not theirs. For every lie you catch them in, assume
they’ve told a hundred more.
After all, you have a professional responsibility to act in your company’s best interest,
and that does not change—no matter how lovely your sales contacts are.

The Reciprocity Principle
We believe that the best vendor–customer partnerships are built on the rule of reci‐
procity. You should want to be a customer that your vendor will fight to retain and
make happy; your vendor should want to provide a service that is valuable enough for
you to fight to keep and want to pay for.
If both of you feel like high-fiving after you negotiate a new contract, that is a
promising start to a great partnership.
Not everyone agrees, and that’s fine. We’ve seen engineers gloating when they think it
costs their vendor more to run their workload than they are being paid to run it—the
vendor is serving them at a loss, in other words. It’s always fun to get something
for free, but in a B2B relationship, it’s usually more valuable to you if your vendor
actually wants your business. (And if the product is good, presumably you want to
keep using it, which means they need to stay in business.)
The reverse can happen, too. Some vendors have a pricing strategy that is designed
to “leave no money on the floor.” This means finding the exact point at which the
price is so painful it will force the customer to leave, and charging right up to that
point. This might not actually sour the relationship—as the people who pay the bills
are usually different from the people who use the product—but it can lead to internal
tensions between payers and users over whether the service is worth it.
A certain slice of enterprise tech is all but impervious to price; what they do care
about is trust, reciprocity, and the ability to influence the vendor’s roadmap (or
strategically plan around it).
When your company is really well-aligned with a company you’re buying from—and
there’s a high degree of respect, transparency, and reciprocity on both sides—it can
come to feel like your successes are their successes, and their successes are yours.
That’s the dream. It doesn’t usually happen. But when it does, it’s incredible.
That kind of trust can only be built over time. But it’s not where you start out—and,
fair warning, it’s not where you usually end up, either.

How to Influence Another Company’s Roadmap
The ability to influence a vendor’s roadmap is one of the more advanced forms
of vendor engineering—one that transforms you from a passive consumer into a


544   | Chapter 30: The Art and Science of Vendor Partnerships
strategic partner. This comes in many forms: product roadmap updates, feature
previews, alpha or beta access, executive dinners after events where long-term plans
get discussed off the record. Access like this gives you confidence that you and your
vendor are strategically aligned for the foreseeable future.
Influence on another company’s roadmap can be earned or cultivated in a number of
ways. Briefly (this topic alone could fill a book!), these include:

  • Being an important customer, valued for your logo, your contract size, or both
  • Strategic alignment—your companies are going in the same direction, there may
    even be opportunities for cross-selling or co-marketing
  • Sweat equity—are you willing to commit some of your own resources to test or
    develop together?
  • Technical credibility and personal relationships, both formal and informal
  • The levers that drive organizational commitments, such as SLAs, OKRs, and
    KPIs

In “A Story About Engineering Influence” on page 534, about how Mark Callaghan
helped to get the pluggable storage engine work on the MongoDB roadmap, you can
see most of these levers at work.
After the Facebook acquisition, MongoDB really wanted to win Facebook as a cus‐
tomer, so they started paying a lot more attention to Parse. Mark was able to leverage
his technical credibility, as one of the engineers who built the pluggable storage
engine for MySQL, to argue that it would be a game changer for MongoDB to do
the same. He also introduced them to key figures in the storage engine community,
and used his influence within Facebook to get the MongoDB storage engine support
on the RocksDB roadmap. He showed up at their conferences, spoke when invited,
joined the MongoDB Community Champions (formerly Masters) community, and
built relationships in the company and community.
This is not a one-way street, by the way. Vendors also need the ability to influence
their customers’ roadmaps at times, starting with the migration. They need to map
out the organization and figure out who can actually sign the deal, and what the
migration process should look like. This is a normal part of de-risking any deal. A
migration that doesn’t appear on a customer’s roadmap or on the OKRs of any of
their engineering teams will probably never happen.
And never underestimate the power of personal relationships. A reliable back channel
can be the only way to cut through layers of formality and confusion and find out
what the hell is going on.
While technical credibility and personal relationships are valuable, those informal
back channels are fragile. For influence to endure, you need to move from the

handshake of strategic alignment to the contract of operational reality by codifying
your expectations in a formal agreement.

SLAs Encode the Vendor–Customer Relationship
SLAs are not just paperwork; they are the encoding of your relationship and the
agreements you have made. SLAs are to the vendor–customer relationship what SLOs
are to the engineering–sales relationship: the assurances that mutual trust can be
built on.

                     An SLA is a formal, legally binding contract between a service pro‐
                     vider and a customer that defines the expected quality, availability,
                     and responsibilities of services provided. It sets clear performance
                     metrics (e.g., uptime) and remedies or penalties if these standards
                     are not met.

Don’t wait until the very end to think about them, don’t forget to get stakeholder
buy-in, and definitely don’t leave the vendors and procurement teams to hash it out
together without your full understanding and participation.
SLAs are some of your most effective levers for driving behavior. Well-crafted SLAs
can help you influence future roadmap and planning decisions in a company where
you have no authority or direct influence, even after you and your negotiating
counterparts have all moved on.
You might assume that the more you trust a vendor, the less important your SLAs
are, but that would be incorrect. Good fences make good neighbors, good boundaries
make good friendships, and good SLAs make good vendor partnerships. They pre‐
vent misunderstandings, align incentives, encourage long-term thinking, and even
protect the vendor from potential lawsuits (always a relationship buzzkill). They
protect you both, so take them seriously.
The topic of buying software and forging partnerships is long and complicated, and
we don’t pretend to take a comprehensive approach. But there are some things you
should seek to clarify from the start, during vendor evaluation—confusion on any
of these points can easily derail the entire process or lead to a substandard outcome
later on.

Early-Stage Considerations
Every org-scale sociotechnical transformation that succeeds has achieved two things
by the time it finishes: a mandate for change from the top, and a bottoms-up ground‐
swell of support. Execs ultimately have to believe it belongs on the roadmap, ahead of

other important work, and engineers need to see the value and want it for themselves,
before you can tap into their creative energy for problem-solving.
In the early stages of a vendor evaluation, you might not have either. This is normal.
Your first step should be to get clear on scope, success criteria, and executive sponsor.
What kind of support and mission parameters do you already have, and which are
you supposed to build?
Here are three very different (fictional) scenarios that help illustrate the point. The
first is an insurgency, the second is a consolidation directive, and the third is an
open-ended exploration:
Fort Pillars Financial (the insurgency)
    This Fortune 100 financial services organization has run the same three pillars
    observability vendor for 17 years at $40 million annually. Their 8,000+ engineers
    have no direct access to production telemetry: all requests flow through central‐
    ized operations. Mean time to resolution (MTTR) averages six to eight hours,
    and is primarily spent correlating signals across siloed systems—by hand.
    Your vice president has commissioned you as her agent of insurgency: find a
    modern alternative, recruit two or three product teams for a POC, and generate
    compelling ROI evidence. She needs quantifiable improvements in MTTR, devel‐
    oper productivity, and cost to force a strategic reassessment at the C-level. Her
    words: “Make their eyes pop.”
Tool Sprawl Industries (the consolidation mandate)
    This technology conglomerate pursued “developer empowerment” and a compet‐
    itive acquisition strategy that resulted in over 40 observability tools across the
    organization. A recent P0 outage took 14 hours to resolve because the relevant
    signals were scattered across five different systems. Annual observability spend
    exceeds $60 million with massive redundancies—and also, at the same time,
    massive gaps.
    You’re a principal engineer with 16 years at this company. The CEO, CTO, and
    CFO have all signed off on a consolidation directive: you are being asked to
    assess all use cases and tools, recommend one modern platform to replace them,
    and develop a migration template. The budget has been preapproved with an
    expectation of 40–50% cost reduction. You have 90 days.
Agent Smith Systems (exploring on the cutting edge)
   This pre-IPO software company adopted OTel early and has consistently demon‐
   strated technical craft and rigor. AI-assisted development has increased the rate
   of new code deployed 5–8x over the last 18 months. But their observability
   infrastructure can’t keep pace: the vendor’s Model Context Protocol (MCP) inte‐
   gration times out, hallucinates metrics, and can’t handle AI-generated code paths
   (or anything else with high cardinality).

      They are starting to operationalize agentic workflow, but getting anxious about
      going any faster without observability that can validate as fast as they ship. They
      haven’t really thought about whether the goal is to replace their existing tools or
      simply augment them. A big migration wouldn’t frighten them. But right now
      they are just trying to prove that something, anything, can work at the speed of
      AI at production scale.
All three of these scenarios are quite common, but each calls for a radically different
approach when it comes to engaging with internal engineering teams, designing a
POC, and aligning around success criteria.
Before the first line of code can be instrumented, you must also clear the final
two hurdles: proactively managing internal friction that may arise, and making the
foundational OTel decision to protect your organization from vendor lock-in. Let’s
look at each consideration.

Define the Scope of Work
How you frame the problem and scope the work is often the determining factor in
whether or not the work succeeds.
Aim too small and, even if you succeed, you’ll have missed a rare opportunity to solve
the bigger problem; at best, you or someone else will just have to go back and do it all
over again. Aim too big, and you will inevitably founder on the rocks of sunk costs,
scope creep, and territorial pushback.
The Goldilocks size will be set by how much air cover you have (from how high up),
how much evidence you have already assembled to prove your case, and the populist
ratio of anger to inertia among engineering teams.

Identify Your Executive Sponsor
Find out who can sign the contract. This sounds basic but trips up many purchases,
as the nominal decision-maker often lacks signature authority. (Also find out who has
budget authority. This may or may not be the same person.)
If there are multiple layers of management between you and the person who can sign
the check, make sure that every hop of the chain stays informed and engaged.
Try to learn as much as you can about how the decision-maker is likely to make their
decision. If you have a clear mental model of what that person cares about, objections
they have thrown in the past, their top priorities and goals, complaints they typically
register, and so forth, you can proactively address those things. Again, surprises are
never good.
This can be challenging, because people are not always very consistent or self-aware,
but you have to try. Any exec who wants to say no is going to say no, and will


548   |   Chapter 30: The Art and Science of Vendor Partnerships
find a way to retroactively justify their decision. Don’t assume that silence means
agreement. Farm for dissent, early and often.

Define Success Criteria Before You Start
The biggest POC failure mode is never defining what success looks like. Too many
POCs get run by ICs or managers who lack budget authority and decision-making
power. By the time the results trickle back to an exec who can actually approve the
purchase, context has been lost and the evaluation criteria feel arbitrary.
Success criteria should be specific and measurable. They should translate the problem
you are trying to solve into functional requirements, architectural characteristics,
and budgetary guidelines. Make your case in terms of the business, as discussed in
Chapter 26.
Most critically, someone close to the budget needs to understand these criteria and
agree they’re the right ones. Figure this out now, not after three months of hard work.

What to Do When Your Coworkers Perceive You as a Threat
Everything we said about how “you and your coworkers are on the same team” is
true. However, this does not prevent you from having incompatible opinions about
what is in the company’s best interest. When people feel threatened, they are rarely at
their best. Two common triggers you may accidentally set off are threats to their tools
or threats to their turf:
Threats to their tools
    Sometimes a tool is more than a tool—it becomes part of someone’s identity. If
    someone (or a team) has been using the same tool for a very long time, they
    may take pride in their expertise. They may be active in that tool’s community,
    be widely known as an expert and sought out for their opinion, or write highly
    regarded essays and talks about it. They may genuinely believe it’s the best tool
    for the job, and they might even be right. But they are usually ill-equipped to
    evaluate alternatives with an open mind.
Threats to their turf
    This one can be tough to avoid with observability, because observability is every‐
    where. You probably already have some tools, and those tools have owners who
    take pride in their work. In Chapter 28, we provided some advice on how to
    acknowledge the heroes who got us here, and how to try and bring them along
    with you in a way that delivers a win for them too.
When ego and expertise are on the line, it’s easy to lose sight of the common good.
When people feel threatened, they are very likely to reach for any levers they have
to sabotage your work, go around your back, escalate above your head, or get you
shut down. They will notify their favored vendor of the threat (i.e., you) and work

whatever angles they can to eliminate that threat, up to and including CEO-to-CEO
contact, if possible. Don’t be naive about this.
The best advice we can give you is to seek counsel from local experts, execs, and
people who have done this before, and never stop trying to extend a hand and build
positive sum outcomes for everyone. Yes, even the backstabbers.
There’s one more decision you should make before you start, but it’s an easy one.

The OpenTelemetry Decision
During vendor evaluation, if you haven’t already adopted OTel at the instrumentation
layer, now is the time to start. If you have to go through and reinstrument everything
anyway, just to evaluate a new vendor, let this be the last time you need to do that.
If a vendor discourages you from using OTel, they are trying to lock you in. If a vendor
locks you in, they are reducing market pressure to improve their own product.5 You
have been warned.
The most important technical contribution to any software buying process comes
at the POC stage. Converting your business case into a meaningful POC—one
that exercises the hardest questions your engineering teams are facing—requires a
deep understanding of your team, your technical problems, and the needs of your
business.

Design and Run a Meaningful Proof of Concept
A POC for observability software should be a load test of your hardest problems and
representative use cases in production. Your job is to build a POC and set of criteria
that are sturdy enough to demonstrate value and support a swift decision.
If more than one vendor is being evaluated, part of your job is to develop a rubric for
comparing them. What does each vendor do best, and how much does that matter to
you? Maybe vendor A is exceptional at a particular use case, but weak at two or three
others. If their strong suit happens to overlap with all of your revenue-generating
requests, it might still be the best option, but if their strong suit is only 20% of what
you care about, you can easily rule them out.
Another place that is both art and science is in deciding whether to instrument your
code to see each vendor at their best, or apply the same apples-to-apples instrumenta‐
tion across all the vendors you trial. Apples-to-apples may take less work from your
engineering teams,6 but resist the urge to shortcut. Instrumentation is not a bolt-on;

What Does a Good Proof of Concept Look Like?
POCs, like the buying process itself, are a bit like snowflakes; hard to generalize
about, hyperlocal, and each one a little different. Your organization probably has
some existing prior art in the form of templates or vendor checklists.
However, as people who work for observability companies, we will take the liberty
of being a bit more prescriptive. In our experience, successful POCs for observability
tools usually include the following elements:
Description of your pain
    Include a short description of the challenges that are causing you to look at
    alternative solutions. Include any precipitating events, if such exist; this might
    mean a pattern of lengthy incidents that violated customer SLAs, or any other
    modernization initiatives in flight.
Your definition of success
    This usually looks like a list of primary outcomes you are trying to achieve.
    For example: understand each customer’s experience, shorten feedback loops
    for developers, add observability to unknown-unknowns without incurring addi‐
    tional cost, and so forth.
Required capabilities
   Make a list of the kinds of questions you rely on, and the questions you wish
   you could ask more easily. Try to avoid including solutions in your requirements
   (e.g., “a query language that supports joins”), but if you’re struggling to list which
   capabilities you wish you had, you can just write down any workflows that are
   currently lossy, laggy, frustrating, or time-consuming.
Scope of the POC process.
    Write down what the evaluation will focus on—which teams, workflows, hard
    problems or edge cases, datasets—as well as what is out of scope for the POC.

Vendors will usually offer to write the requirements for you, to “save you time.” True,

make sure that what is written down is actually reflective of your real needs and
your hardest problems, and not composed expressly to make this vendor the obvious

If you can, try to get your colleagues to agree up front that if the tool passes all the
POC tests, you will proceed based on the data—and not relitigate endlessly based on
the feelings or vibes of too many stakeholders. This may be unenforceable (execs can
always change their mind), but it sets the right expectations and makes people take
their part more seriously.

Structure Your Technical Evaluation
Each of your technical stakeholders is going to have their own concerns, must-haves,
and wishlist items. Your job is to validate that the list remains reasonable: compre‐
hensive enough to include the jobs to be done, but not tied up with preserving
backward parity at the expense of net new capabilities.
Identify which engineering teams are likely to be users of yours (users of the tool you
buy), and which engineering teams are gatekeepers. They have different needs.
If you know you have internal competition or friction over turf with other teams, use
the POC to keep everyone honest. Be alert for “capabilities” that are actually poison
pills from other vendors, designed expressly to make other tools fail. Define success
in terms of the business use cases, not specific features or implementation details.
One of the biggest things to test for in a POC is how well the new solution plays with
the old solution. Both solutions will need to run in parallel for a while, so you may
need to deploy a lot of instrumentation that’s turned off until you can fully migrate
off the old vendor. Can you set up dual writes? OTel can help by letting you wire up
multiple exporters.

Validate Pricing at Scale (If Cost Is a Concern)
Most companies (but not all!) are sensitive to cost. If that’s you, one of the most
important aspects of your POC will be validating the pricing model and getting some
ballpark estimates.
This is surprisingly difficult to do without sending all your data through the system.
Most teams compromise by sending a representative sample—perhaps a single clu‐
ster’s worth of data—and extrapolating to full production scale.

The price on the vendor’s website is meaningless—nobody pays that. Always see if
you can find a customer with an architecture or product similar to yours who is
willing to speak freely about how much they pay. Don’t forget to ask how many
ongoing engineering cycles they devote to managing costs (this can be substantial, as
in several engineer-salaries worth).
Vendors can help with introductions and research. If you trust your vendor, this can
save you a lot of time and energy. If your vendor is on the shady side, do the sourcing
yourself to avoid shenanigans.

Build Champions and Advertise Your Wins
Not every POC is a winner; not every migration is worth doing. Engineers run POCs
all the time that they don’t particularly care about, and that’s fine. But if you have
developed a strong conviction that this one is a winner, and adopting it is in the best
interests of your company, you sit in a unique position of influence. By developing
champions, solving hard problems, and enthusiastically broadcasting your shared
triumphs, you can substantially improve the odds of success.
The best POC processes solve real problems for real teams in production. This is by
far the best way to build champions—not just out of developers, but also out of their
management layers. Don’t start with the easy problems; go straight for the toughest,
gnarliest knots in your org. Who is understaffed, and can’t get out of firefighting
mode? Which team has been getting woken up night after night after night, and
still can’t identify the issue? Which teams are losing good people to burnout and
exhaustion? Who has a principal engineer who’s been pulled into every SEV0 or SEV1
for the past year?
Follow the pain. Help solve their problems. Turn relieved engineers into enthusiastic
champions and advocates for the cause.
Don’t let those wins get lost or buried! Start a brag list: write down every time
an engineer uses instrumentation and tooling to identify inefficiencies, save the com‐
pany money, find a bug before a customer does, accelerate time to delivery, decrease
MTTR, or pinpoint lurking issues that have been plaguing other people for months.
Help your coworkers do things worth bragging about, then praise their achievements
in public. Make sure their manager sees it, and their manager’s manager, all the way
up the ladder. The bigger the win, the more you celebrate. Make other engineers want
to see what all the fuss is about. You want them lining up and begging to get in on it.

You are responsible for keeping the purchase moving through your organizational
machinery. If something is held up on your side, say something. Don’t just go dark.
Sometimes the vendor can be useful in helping unblock you, but only if they know.

The deal isn’t done until the contract is signed on both sides; be wary of premature
complacency. But once the signatures are done—congrats! This is huge!
After the deal is signed, it’s time to switch gears and start planning the process of
migrating and cutting over to your new vendor.

Migration and Follow-Through
Ha, you thought you were done? What happens after the contract is signed will
determine whether your investment actually pays off or not.

Handing Off to Another Team
Shortly after the contract is signed, the people staffing the deal will often (but not
always) change over. The vendor side may do a handoff from solutions architects
to customer architects, or presales to post sales. On the customer side, this is often
where platform engineering or your observability team steps in to start planning a
migration path for affected teams.
A bit of turnover is normal and OK, but a lot of turnover on either side can lead to a
stalled project and dropped packets.
You may be handing the baton off, but don’t drop and dash off. Stick around to make
introductions and help draft the migration path, along with timelines and milestones.
Share whatever useful bits you’ve learned from the POC. Set up a regular checkpoint
with whoever you are handing off to. Someone needs to continue to ride herd on
your side; if it stops being you, make sure the handoff is explicit and known to both
sides.

Most Migrations Have Three Steps
Successful migrations often follow a three-stage structure:

 1. De-risk the migration by proving it works with a nontrivial but manageable
    example.
 2. Execute the bulk migration—get everything moved (accept some messiness).
 3. Iterate service by service to clean up and optimize.

Migrations at enterprise scale have historically taken months, if not years: no wonder
people avoid doing them at all costs. Modern LLM tools have brought these timelines
down substantially, usually on the order of weeks—occasionally days. But this will
depend to some extent on how practiced you are with migrations. If you’ve been



554   |   Chapter 30: The Art and Science of Vendor Partnerships
using the same tool for the past 15 years, it’s going to be a heavy lift; there’s no way
around it. On the other hand, this is a muscle you need to build.
Plug milestones into your organizational operating system (KPIs, OKRs, whatever),
or the work will just get bumped.
Don’t try to use an export of the old tool configuration as input to the new tool. It will
be full of cruft. Start with clarity around business requirements, then build the new
config in the new tool from scratch, using new tooling approaches and workflows.
If you are still connected to the project, your critical contribution is this: present
the messy reality as linear progress to stakeholders. Stakeholders want clear narrative
arcs. Give them one, even though the actual work involves lots of back-and-forth
iteration and problem-solving. Part of your skill in driving migrations is turning
messy loops into linear progression that stakeholders can understand and support.

You Aren’t Done Until You Decommission
Adding a tool is easy compared to decommissioning one. The last 10% of a decom‐
missioning project is the hardest and slowest, as only the difficult, scary parts remain.
Ensure you publish a timeline for deprecation and deletion of the old tool, and try
to stick to it. Only when the old tool is gone will you reap the full cost benefits of all
your hard work.
You have likely moved on to other projects by the time the migration reaches this
point (which could be months, or even years later). If so, consider circling back and
helping with the dirty work for a few weeks. It could give everyone the boost they
need to get past the finish line. (And then: throw a party!7)

Plan for the Next Transition
One last counterintuitive detail: start planning immediately for the eventual replace‐
ment of the tool you just bought. Write down a recap of the buying process while
it’s fresh in your mind. What was harder than expected? What went well? What do
you wish you had known at the outset? How effective was your POC? Are there any
lingering concerns in your mind about the vendor you chose and their ability to
execute? Was there a close runner-up?
Requalify tools and vendors on a regular cadence. Examine cost versus value. When
cost approaches or exceeds value, reassess your requirements, evaluate the current
market, and consider alternatives. If you find something compelling, set up demos or

Conclusion
When you’re a rookie engineer, maybe you dream of writing the next operating sys‐
tem or distributed consensus algorithm—some kind of pure, hard computer science.
Few of us dream of growing up to send reminder emails to procurement teams and
post-sales training teams.
Yet partnerships and vendor engineering are often the most effective ways of solving
your company’s hardest sociotechnical problems at scale. In a world where AI gener‐
ates more code than people do, your value still lies in solving business problems with
technology.
Companies that try to build too much themselves will fail to compete with those that
are nimble enough to leverage the best of the industry. Decisions about who you
partner with compound over time; a good partnership, like good architecture, can
move mountains.
By treating the buying process with the same rigor as a production system—design‐
ing robust POCs, building trust with stakeholders, encoding protections into SLAs—
you ensure that your infrastructure remains an asset rather than a liability. Buying
software isn’t the end of the journey; it’s the beginning of a partnership.
