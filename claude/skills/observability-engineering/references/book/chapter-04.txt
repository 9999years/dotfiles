Now that we’re on the same page about overarching theories and the origins of
observability as a practice, we’ll get into concrete details about technical fundamen‐
tals and implementations. We will begin by examining the fundamentals of instru‐
mentation.
There’s an old business adage that roughly says, “you can’t improve what you don’t
measure.” That idea rings true for the systems you build with a high degree of observ‐
ability. Because there are so many different functional aspects of modern application
systems, in practice, we must collect an increasingly vast amount of data in order to
fully understand them.
As you’ll see in this section of the book, generating and collecting telemetry data
is the foundational cornerstone of an observable system. Given a sufficient volume
of telemetry data to work from, it’s possible to understand any given state of your
system—no matter how novel or bizarre. However, before we start talking about data
analysis, we first need to dig into what a “sufficient volume of telemetry data” means.
Let’s dive into what it takes to properly instrument your systems.
This chapter provides an introduction to instrumentation, and it offers considera‐
tions when generating telemetry and regarding where that telemetry is sent. As a soft‐
ware engineer responsible for ensuring application reliability, these considerations
must be essential to your workflows.
We also briefly introduce concepts that all software engineers must know
when approaching observability: the OpenTelemetry project (an open source and
vendor-neutral instrumentation framework), deployment strategies, and practical
considerations like customization, costs, and sampling. This chapter focuses on
introductory-level information, but we will build much further upon these concepts
throughout the course of this book.

Instrumentation Basics
What is instrumentation in software engineering? It’s the practice of adding code to
your applications so that they emit data about what’s happening as your software
runs. You use that data to understand its behavior, measure performance, or diagnose
runtime errors.
To use an analogy, modern observable systems are akin to flying home at night,
looking out the window, and gazing down on bustling city lights. Thousands of
lights will flicker on and off, traffic will flow across intricate pathways, and complex
patterns of activity can be beautiful to observe but difficult to understand without the
right vantage point.
Just as city planners need traffic sensors, population monitors, and utility meters to
understand how their metropolis functions, software engineers need instrumentation
to understand how their applications behave in production. One of the authors of this
book came up with that comparison after a few days of intensive planning work for
this book. Clearly, that work took its toll, but we think the analogy stands.
You can think of instrumentation as installing sensors and meters throughout your
application’s sprawling metropolis to report on everything from how long database
queries take, to which features users are actually using, to what happens when errors
occur. That telemetry data becomes the foundation for observability, or your ability
to understand and explain any state your system can get into, no matter how novel
or complex. The more comprehensive the instrumentation, the more granular your
understanding becomes.
Without proper instrumentation, debugging production issues becomes an exercise
in detective work with insufficient evidence. You might know that users are experi‐
encing slowness, but you won’t know which specific operations are slow, for which
users, or under what conditions. If you don’t start with proper instrumentation,
you’ll find yourself adding log statements after problems occur, waiting for issues to
reproduce, and making educated guesses full of logical leaps in an attempt to arrive at
elusive root causes with even more elusive contributing factors.
With good instrumentation, you can approach problems systematically, following the
data to understand exactly what happened and why. This part of the book will teach
you how to implement good instrumentation.

The Vocabulary of Telemetry: Logs, Metrics, and Traces
Before diving into instrumentation strategies, it’s helpful to understand the types of
telemetry data your applications can emit. Take this with a grain of salt for now
(we will explain more about telemetry datatypes in the next chapter), but most



52   |   Chapter 4: Getting Started with Instrumentation
readers who are new to modern observable systems will think of these as what is also
sometimes referred to as “the three pillars” of observability: logs, metrics, and traces.
While this book doesn’t promote the idea of “pillars,” we want to emphasize the
importance of these datatypes for telemetry for observability. Each type serves a
particular purpose, and it’s important to know which type of data to reach for when
instrumenting an application.
Historically, these datatypes have served different purposes and answered different
questions about your system’s behavior. For the purposes of this chapter, we’ll unpack
the historical uses and connotations of these datatypes, and how to collect them
(before breaking them down further).
Logs represent discrete events that happened in your application at specific points in
time. The content of your logs might capture when a user logged in, when a payment
was processed, or when an error occurred. Traditional logs were often unstructured
text designed for human reading, but modern logging practices emphasize structured
logs with consistent key-value pairs that machines can easily parse and analyze. A
well-structured log entry might look like:
    {
    “timestamp: “2024”,
    “user_id”: “630630”,
    “action: checkout_completed”,
    [...]
    }
Metrics represent numerical measurements of your system over a period of time.1
They answer questions like “How many requests per second is my service handling?”
or “What’s the 95th percentile response time for my API?” Metrics are typically cap‐
tured as numeric values that aggregate or summarize data. Instead of storing infor‐
mation about every individual request, you might store a counter that increments
with each request, or a histogram that tracks the distribution of response times. That
aggregation makes metrics efficient to store and query, but it also means you lose the
ability to drill down into any context behind the sum.
Distributed traces (also known more simply as “traces”) represent the journey of a
single transaction as it flows through a system. When a user places an order on your
site, that request might trigger calls to several dependent services to update inventory,
calculate shipping, process payment, and email a receipt. A trace captures all of these
operations as a connected sequence, showing you the timeline of what happened and
where time was spent. Each step in this sequence is called a span, and spans can
contain their own detailed information about what occurred during that operation.

As noted in Chapter 1, these datatypes have been historically managed and stored
separately, even though they complement each other. Each telemetry type has a use
case for which it is best suited. Logs give you detailed context about individual events.
Metrics give you aggregate views of system health and performance trends. Traces
give you end-to-end visibility into complex operations that span multiple services.
In the next chapter, we’ll look at ways to optimize for and unify these use cases
to achieve a high degree of observability. For now, let’s focus on modern instru‐
mentation approaches that make it easy to emit rich telemetry that serves multiple

OpenTelemetry: The Universal Language for Observability
Historically, observability vendors required the use of their own proprietary agents
and libraries to collect telemetry from your applications. If you wanted to try a
different vendor or send data to multiple backends, you’d need to rethink your entire
instrumentation strategy and, often, face a scenario where you’d need to rip and
replace existing agents and code. That created a frustrating lock-in scenario, where
your initial choice of observability tool dictated how you could instrument your code.
OpenTelemetry (or OTel) emerged as the solution to that problem. It’s an open source
project that provides vendor-neutral APIs, libraries, and tools for collecting telemetry
from your applications. Think of OTel as a universal translator that lets your applica‐
tions speak a common language that any observability backend can understand.
The power of OTel comes via its separation of concerns. Your application code
uses OpenTelemetry APIs to emit telemetry, but the actual destination for that data
is configurable. You can send the same instrumentation data to multiple backends
simultaneously, making it easy to evaluate different observability tools or maintain
redundancy. You can even change your backend completely without touching your
application code by just updating the destination configuration. As a result, library
authors can also safely call the API knowing it will default to performing no opera‐
tions and thus have no overhead unless configured.
Out of the box, OTel supports automatic instrumentation for popular frameworks
and libraries. That means you can get basic telemetry flowing with minimal code
changes. For a typical web application, you might only need to add a few lines of
configuration in order to automatically capture HTTP requests, database queries, and
calls to external services. Automatic instrumentation gives you immediate visibility
into common actions to understand your application’s behavior without extensive
manual instrumentation.
But the real strength of OTel becomes apparent when you start adding custom instru‐
mentation specific to your business logic. While automatic instrumentation captures
the technical plumbing of your application, custom instrumentation captures the

domain-specific operations that matter to your business. For example, custom instru‐
mentation might be used to capture user registration flows, order processing pipe‐
lines, or how content recommendation algorithms perform. The simple reality of
business applications is that they have many unique aspects that automatic instru‐
mentation simply cannot and will not understand.

Automatic Instrumentation Versus
Custom Instrumentation
Inevitably, most engineers will ask whether they should use automatic or custom
instrumentation. The answer is to use both. The distinction between automatic and
custom instrumentation represents different approaches to capturing telemetry, each
with its own strengths and appropriate use cases.
Automatic instrumentation works by intercepting common operations in your appli‐
cation framework and libraries. When you make an HTTP request, query a database,
or call an external service, automatic instrumentation can capture details about
these operations and how they relate to each other without any explicit code on
your part. Modern automatic instrumentation is remarkably sophisticated, capable
of understanding complex frameworks and extracting meaningful telemetry with
minimal configuration.
The primary advantage of automatic instrumentation is in its breadth of coverage
with minimal effort. You can instrument dozens of common operations across your
entire application by adding just a few lines of configuration. That minimal effort will
give you immediate visibility into the basic technical health of your application with
data like response times, error rates, dependencies, and resource usage.
However, automatic instrumentation can only capture what it understands about
technical operations across the lowest common denominator for all applications.
It can’t understand your business logic, domain-specific workflows, or application-
specific error conditions. For example, automatic instrumentation might tell you that
a database query took 200 milliseconds, but it won’t tell you that this query was part
of a user registration flow that failed because the email address was already in use.
Custom instrumentation fills that gap by capturing telemetry that is specific to your
application’s business logic. That’s what enables you to understand the state of things
like user actions, business processes, and domain-specific operations. For example,
custom instrumentation might capture when users complete specific workflows,
when background jobs process certain types of data, or when business rules trigger
specific decisions.
The most effective observability strategies combine both automatic and custom
instrumentation. Automatic instrumentation provides comprehensive coverage of



                                      Automatic Instrumentation Versus Custom Instrumentation   |   55
the technical plumbing and the skeleton of how everything fits together. Custom
instrumentation attaches insight into business operations and user experience, adding
muscles to that skeleton.
Teams may initially be shocked when they turn on automatic instrumentation in
production—the level of detail can be overwhelming and often counterproductive.
Customizing your instrumentation isn’t just about adding more; it’s also about know‐
ing what to take away. Developing an intuition for what’s valuable in different envi‐
ronments can pay dividends. For example, recording every database call and Redis
query may be useful locally, but in production those small spans can add up to a real
performance impact.
As your system matures, you’ll likely find that custom instrumentation becomes
increasingly valuable for understanding the aspects of your application that matter
most to your business. In Chapter 7, we’ll cover this entire topic in more detail,
along with specific guidance on instrumenting for both common and less-common
application architectures.

Ownership of Instrumentation
One of the most important decisions your team will make in the course of an
observability initiative is who owns the instrumentation strategy and how it evolves
over time. In traditional monitoring, instrumentation was often the responsibility of
a dedicated operations team that added monitoring after applications were deployed.
That reactive approach often led to gaps in coverage and instrumentation that didn’t
align well with the actual structure and business logic of applications.
Modern observability requires a shift in ownership. The engineers who write the
application code are best positioned to understand what instrumentation would be
most valuable. They know which operations are critical to the business, which code
paths are most complex or error-prone, and what context would be especially helpful
during debugging. When developers own instrumentation, it becomes part of the
design process rather than an afterthought.
However, that shift in ownership doesn’t mean every software engineer needs to
become an expert in observability tooling. Instead, successful organizations establish
patterns and conventions that make it easy for developers to add good instrumenta‐
tion without deep expertise. Those conventions might include techniques like provid‐
ing standard libraries to more easily propagate common instrumentation patterns,
code review guidelines that ensure new features include appropriate telemetry, or
documentation that explains when and how to add custom instrumentation (for a
more in-depth look, see Part VI on observability governance).
Your instrumentation strategy should evolve alongside your application. As your
system grows more complex, as you learn from production incidents, and as your

understanding of user behavior deepens, so too will your instrumentation needs
evolve. In other words, instrumentation isn’t a one-time setup task. As a software
engineer, you should build the evolution of instrumentation into your development
process to ensure that your observability capabilities keep pace with your system’s
complexity.
In time, you will establish instrumentation as a primary deliverable in your develop‐
ment process, just as you have with tests (see Chapter 9, on the parallels between
test-driven development and observability-driven development). When planning
new features, your team should explicitly discuss what telemetry would help you
best understand how those features behave in production. During code reviews, your
team should evaluate whether the proposed changes include a sufficient level of
instrumentation. When conducting post-incident reviews, your team should identify
gaps in telemetry that made the investigation more difficult than necessary.
If it hasn’t already, instrumentation will become an integral part of your ongoing
software development workflows.

Building a Custom Instrumentation Strategy
Developing an effective custom instrumentation strategy requires thinking systemat‐
ically about what aspects of your application behavior would be most valuable to
understand. Rather than instrumenting everything, focus on the areas where visibility
will have the most impact on your ability to operate and improve your system.
Start by identifying the critical user journeys in your application (see Chapter 11).
Critical user journeys are the sequences of operations that users must complete
successfully in order for your business to function. Of course, the specific details
will vary across industries and use cases. But, as an example, let’s suppose you have
an ecommerce application. Business-critical user journeys for you might include
things like user registration, product search, cart management, and checkout. Or,
let’s say you have a content-management platform. Then, critical user journeys might
include content creation, publishing, and consumption workflows. Instrumenting
these journeys gives you visibility into the health of your core business operations.
Consider the error conditions that are specific to your domain. While automatic
instrumentation might capture HTTP 500 errors, it won’t understand business-
specific failures like “insufficient inventory,” “invalid coupon code,” or “user not in the
correct experiment group for this operation.” Custom instrumentation can capture
those domain-specific error conditions along with the context needed to identify,
understand, and resolve them. Be sure to distinguish temporary versus permanent
failures, as well as failures that are or are not actually actionable by an IT team.

It can take practice when you’re first thinking about what to capture. To get started,
try thinking about the questions you frequently need to answer during incident
response or when analyzing system performance:

     • Which users are being affected?
     • What are the key stages of my pipelines?
     • What feature flags are in use?

Remember that instrumentation is not just about capturing what happens. Instru‐
mentation exists to capture any additional context that also helps you understand why
it happened. Include relevant business context like user segments, feature flags that
were active, or the source of a request (for more in-depth examples, see Chapter 6).
That type of contextual information often makes the difference between quickly
resolving an issue and spending hours trying to reproduce and understand it.
As your custom instrumentation evolves, you’ll want to establish patterns and
conventions that make it easy for all developers to add consistent custom instru‐
mentation. That might include standard attributes that should be included in all
business-operation traces, naming conventions, or templates for instrumenting com‐
mon patterns like background job processing or API endpoints (for more tips, see
Chapter 31).
Remember that your custom instrumentation needs will evolve as you become more
immersed in these concepts and as your understanding of your system deepens. After
major incidents, you’ll begin to find opportunities for additional instrumentation
that could have helped you detect or resolve similar issues more quickly. When
you find yourself repeatedly digging through logs or manually correlating data to
understand system behavior, you’ll likely identify new custom instrumentation that
could simplify that analysis.
The goal is to build instrumentation that not only helps you understand what’s
happening in your system, but also empowers you to ask and answer better questions
that you haven’t even thought of yet. Recall that systems with a high degree of
observability give you the ability to understand and explain any state your system can
get into, no matter how novel or complex. Great instrumentation captures a high level
of context and provides the foundation for great observability.
That said, as you build custom instrumentation, it’s tempting to try to capture every
possible piece of information. While some observability tools are built to work with
that strategy, for others that choice may not be practical. Time-series databases have
explicit design constraints that lead to poor outcomes when the cardinality of a
metric becomes too high. Full-text search databases require indexing and precompu‐
ted schemas. Too much instrumentation can create noise that makes it harder to

Cost Considerations, Volume
Management, and Processing
Inevitably, you’ll end up needing to manage the volumes of telemetry data that
properly instrumented applications produce. The fact of the matter is, most traffic
is boring and commonplace, and so is the data for that traffic. But some traffic
is interesting, or essential to understanding what’s happening. Determining how to
manage data along these lines isn’t a simple calculus, and there are many levers
that you have access to for performing this management and balancing trade-offs.
We won’t cover all of those options in-depth here, but we will introduce the basic
concepts and considerations. In later chapters, these topics are discussed further.

Processing and Pipelines
It’s common to process telemetry data before storing it, regardless of the type of
instrumentation you use. For example, you may need to drop or transform data to
reduce its volume or perform extraction or conversion of data into other formats. You
might also need to do things like perform data normalization or filter out personally
identifiable information before sending your telemetry to a backend for storage (see
Chapter 16).
When you’re writing instrumentation into your code, you should be agnostic to
backend capabilities for the sake of portability, and try to defer processing decisions
until later in the telemetry-collection process. After all, you don’t know exactly what
telemetry data you might need in your investigations. If any data captured becomes
problematic, it’s much easier to remove specific details that turn out to be unneeded
rather than to be left wanting for attributes that were never collected to begin with.
Additionally, you should consider the scaling implications of your instrumentation
strategy, design, and implementation. For example, not all users of an instrumenta‐
tion library will require global replication or remote procedure calls with submillisec‐
ond response times. Some will, but not all. Good instrumentation design takes that
variability into account. In cases like these, you can use techniques like telemetry
layering (see Chapter 7).

Cost Considerations and Volume
You can capture all the telemetry you want, but what does it cost? Just about every‐
thing in your instrumentation strategy can be quantified in terms of cost.




                                       Cost Considerations, Volume Management, and Processing   |   59
Some costs are clear, such as the amount of storage, memory, or computation
required to both store and query the data. These costs are typically incurred as a
bill from an external vendor if you bought an observability solution. Or, they could
be incurred as internal costs measured in both infrastructure and labor required to
create and maintain custom-built observability solutions (see Chapter 29).
Many costs are more hidden. For example, a poor instrumentation strategy can lead
to confusing data or disjointed datasets. If data is scattered across 10 or 15 different
tools, you’re likely paying hidden costs like cognitive overload and friction when inci‐
dent responders are unable to quickly triage and correctly diagnose issues after alerts
are triggered. Those costs often manifest visibly as delayed incident-resolution times,
but the factors behind that increase are often invisible to executive stakeholders. In
addition, cost-management practices have an overhead on developer productivity
and incident resolution—trying to understand why spans are sampled out, or creat‐
ing convoluted workarounds to reduce metric cardinality.
We’ll discuss these costs further as we examine choices between buying and building
observability solutions. For now, keep in mind that there’s no such thing as a free
lunch. Teams that are deciding to build versus buy may account for obvious costs
like labor and infrastructure, but are often surprised by less readily apparent costs.
For example, self-hosted telemetry datastores often still incur costs for things like
network egress, which can quickly escalate while transferring telemetry data between
availability zones, regions, clusters, datacenters, and so forth.
Most often, the overhead required to collect and transmit a trickle of telemetry
data over the network for backend systems is quite small and manageable. However,
when working at scale, distributed or cloud native workloads can turn that into a
flood of data, especially if instrumentation is misconfigured. Various solutions exist
for managing telemetry data at scale, such as utilizing telemetry pipelines to buffer
high-resolution data locally, while shipping aggregated data across the network (see
previous callouts for these concepts).
However, there is one specific element of cost control and volume management that
deserves special attention in this introductory chapter: telemetry sampling.

Essential Concepts for Sampling
Rather than recording 100% of all generated telemetry, sampling is the practice of
selectively deciding what data to keep (the collected sample) and discarding the rest.
In general, we believe it is a best practice to sample all telemetry data, regardless of
the scale at which you operate: sampling lets you capture truly valuable data while
discarding irrelevant noise.
Conceptually, there are only two ways of sampling data: you can sample either before
or after it gets emitted.


60   | Chapter 4: Getting Started with Instrumentation
Before
Sampling before data gets emitted happens in various ways. For example, there’s one
very common method for pre-emitted telemetry sampling that most developers don’t
often appreciate: metrics. Metrics are a form of sampling. They are numerical values
that are calculated by choosing measurements and dimensions, and then aggregating
all measurements that occurred over a specified time interval. The individual events
that occurred during that interval are considered noise and get discarded. Only the
pre-aggregated summary value remains.
A more-often realized form is something like probability-based head sampling, which
is a simple technique that boils down to “only sample a percentage of my traces or
logs.” Head sampling works very well for services operating at a truly high volume,
but the simple probability-based approach presents significant drawbacks for services
not operating at enormous scale.
Let’s say your service has uniform traffic patterns and you only care about aggregate
statistics. If that’s the case, then this approach favors a reduction in overall telemetry
data volume at the cost of potentially missing outliers or long-tail issues. In other
words, in a scenario where we are only keeping a 1% sample of telemetry data, it’s
entirely likely that an error that only occurs 0.1% of the time would be completely
missed. Again, for services operating at enormous scale, 0.1% of traffic is still a
massive amount of traffic, but for anything less, you risk losing valuable error data by
head sampling.
Sampling data before it gets emitted (at the source) most often favors resource effi‐
ciency over data fidelity and completeness. It is also oblivious to concerns upstream
or downstream of its decision-making. For example, there is no way to know if
a downstream service call results in an error at the time that a head-sampling
decision must be made. If what you’re after is a high degree of observability, the head-
sampling trade-off is almost entirely skewed toward saving on cost at the expense of
debuggability.

After
The second form of sampling happens after telemetry data is emitted. Unlike head
sampling, this form of sampling should be used to optimize for telemetry data fidelity
and completeness. A wide variety of techniques can be used for this purpose.
For example, with distributed traces, you can use tail-based sampling. Tail-based
sampling is a technique where all spans for a trace are collected and buffered before
a sampling decision is made. That way, you can apply processing logic and rules to
examine the entirety of the transactional record to determine whether or not this
specific record is worth keeping.




                                       Cost Considerations, Volume Management, and Processing   |   61
In other words, with tail-based sampling, you could examine each trace or log line
entirely before making a decision to keep or discard. Does this trace look just like the
thousands of other 200 OK status requests you’ve recorded over the last hour? Or did
it capture something more significant that indicates it is worth keeping?
Techniques for effective telemetry sampling are sophisticated and nuanced because
they ultimately must take into account specific rules or considerations for your
business. For this introductory content, we won’t unpack how to sample effectively
any further. But we do think it’s imperative to point out that not all the telemetry
you generate is necessarily worth keeping. Remember, all telemetry has some level of
cost. Practiced effectively, sampling is one of the most effective levers you can use to
manage observability costs (for in-depth techniques, see Chapter 15).

Conclusion
You can’t improve what you can’t measure. Instrumentation is the foundational cor‐
nerstone of your observability experience. Every software engineer must learn to
integrate instrumentation into every facet of the development lifecycle. You will rely
on automatic instrumentation to quickly reveal the basic vital signs of operational
health in your application. You’ll rely on custom instrumentation to understand the
health of your business functions.
You’ll need to think about instrumentation holistically and form a cohesive strategy
for its ownership and evolution. More than likely, you’ll add that instrumentation to
your code via OpenTelemetry. As a software engineer, you may not be involved in all
of the intricacies for managing observability costs, but you should have at least some
fundamental understanding of the techniques used to do so and their implications.
This chapter introduces concepts that all engineers should know, and we’ll continue
to unpack those further in the remainder of this book.
Next, we’ll explore another telemetry-related concept that is fundamental to learning
about systems with a high degree of observability. It’s only possible to move from a
three pillars model to a unified model of observability by removing the erroneous
distinctions between the telemetry types you know as logs, metrics, and traces.
