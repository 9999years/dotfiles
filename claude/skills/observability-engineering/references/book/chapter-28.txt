You’ve invested in observability. Dashboards are everywhere. All three pillars,
checked: metrics, logs, traces. You’ve paid your vendors and staffed your teams. But
when you look at your organization, the reality doesn’t match the promise.
War rooms still happen. Correlation is still manual. Engineers ship code and have no

people get pulled in because nobody else can figure out the tools. Customers discover
bugs before you do. Mysteries persist so long that they become folklore.
You spent millions. You hired and trained all the people. Your dashboards proudly
display green. Why?
Here is the uncomfortable truth: you have legacy observability masquerading as
modern observability. You bought “observability” tools. You renamed teams from
“monitoring” to “observability.” But the outcomes have not changed. You still cannot

trace a single user’s experience, debug problems you have never seen before, or
answer a novel question from leadership.
The label does not change the capability.
This chapter is about recognizing that gap and building a path to close it. It demands
a strategic dismantling of the status quo. In the sections that follow, I’ll define the
tests to separate genuine observability from legacy monitoring that’s masquerading as
modern tooling. I’ll then map out the human landscape, exploring how to neutralize

for change. Finally, I’ll provide a tactical roadmap for building a dedicated team to
establish a permanent organizational capability that can withstand the complexity of
the AI-augmented future.

Recognizing Legacy Masquerading
as Modern Observability
Before organizational change can happen, you need to see clearly where you actually
stand. Not where the vendor says you stand. Not where your dashboards say you
stand. Where the evidence says you stand. You need a clear diagnosis: do you have
legacy monitoring masquerading as modern observability, or do you have the real
thing? Not feelings or suspicions. Evidence.
In this section, I’ll take you through five tests that can help reveal where you actually
stand. Not feelings or suspicions. Evidence.

The Ownership Test
Ask any engineer, “After you merge code, how do you know whether it is working in
production?” The responses reveal one of two realities:
Legacy observability
    Code goes into a black hole. You have no idea when it deployed. You have no
    visibility into whether it is working. If it was terrible, someone might get paged
    hours or days later. A customer might discover the bug months from now. The
    news may eventually make its way back to you. Or not.
Modern observability
   Code goes live within minutes. You get a notification with a link showing your
   build being canaried out. You watch key metrics in real time. If something
   regresses, you see it immediately. If everything looks good, you watch your
   change roll out with confidence.




488   | Chapter 28: The Organizational Shift
The real question this test asks: how much energy, prior knowledge, and creativity
do you demand of every engineer just to understand the code they shipped? In
organizations with legacy observability, understanding your own changes requires
heroic effort. In organizations with strong observability, it is effortless.
If the feedback loop does not exist, it does not matter how much software you build or
buy. You are pouring water into a leaky bucket.

The Two-or-Three People Test
When an engineer needs to answer a question about production, do they pull up their
tools, or do they turn and ask one of the two or three people who know how to use
the tools? The responses reveal one of two realities:
Legacy observability
    Only a handful of senior engineers can effectively investigate production prob‐
    lems. Everyone else is helpless. The tools are so complex, limited, or poorly
    designed that only specialists with years of accumulated knowledge can use them.
    When those specialists leave, the knowledge leaves with them.
Modern observability
   Sixty to eighty percent of engineers can independently investigate issues. The
   tools make investigation accessible. Knowledge is encoded in the system rather
   than in people’s heads.
In large enterprises, this test reveals something worse than a skills gap. It reveals a
structural vulnerability. Those two or three people are single points of failure across
hundreds of engineers and thousands of services. They get pulled into every incident.
They become bottlenecks for the entire organization. Then, they burn out.
If your observability requires a firefighter, you do not have observability. You have a
dependency on humans who will eventually leave.

The Mystery Test
Ask engineers directly: “What’s something weird about production that we just
accept?” The responses reveal one of two realities:
Legacy observability
    Engineers accept shallow mysteries that persist for months. “We have a load spike
    around midnight and do not know where it comes from.” “That job just fails
    sometimes.” “Latency is higher on Tuesdays for some reason.” After spending
    two days investigating without resolution, they learned to stop investigating odd
    patterns. The mysteries become folklore. Nobody bothers asking why anymore.




                                     Recognizing Legacy Masquerading as Modern Observability   |   489
Modern observability
   Mysteries become specific and investigable. Engineers will say, “We have a load
   spike,” which becomes “The batch analytics job triggers 40,000 database queries
   because the query plan changed.” Mysteries get solved, not accepted.
You can surface this question in a team meeting, during an incident retrospective, or
in one-on-ones. You could also search your chat history for phrases like “just does
that sometimes” or “we never figured out.” Look at recent postmortems and count
how many of them list the root cause as unknown or indeterminate. Check your
runbooks for procedures that amount to “restart and hope.”
The mystery test is particularly revealing in enterprises with long histories. Accumu‐
lated folklore is a symptom of accumulated failure. Every mystery your organization
has stopped investigating is evidence that your observability could not answer a
question that mattered.
Mysteries that persist are not mysteries. They are the shape of your blindness.

The Arbitrary Question Test
Give an engineer a question they have never been asked before. Something that
requires slicing data by dimensions you did not preconfigure. For example, “Show me
all failed checkout attempts from mobile users in California using version 2.3.1 of the
app during lunch hour over the past week.” The responses reveal one of two realities:
Legacy observability
    They need to check if those dimensions were indexed. Probably not. They wait
    for new indexes to build. Hours or days. They resort to manual log analysis.
    Painfully slow. They give up and say, “We don’t track that combination.”
Modern observability
   They answer in under 60 seconds. The data exists. The query runs. The answer
   appears. No one had to anticipate this question for it to be answerable.
This test separates monitoring from observability. Monitoring answers questions you
predicted. Observability answers questions you did not. In a world where novel
problems are the norm, the ability to ask novel questions is not optional.
If you can only investigate what you predicted, you cannot debug the unexpected.

The Deployment Confidence Test
Ask engineering leadership: “How much fear is there around deployments?” The
responses reveal one of two realities:




490   |   Chapter 28: The Organizational Shift
Legacy observability
    Deployments happen weekly or less. They require war rooms. Engineers are
    genuinely afraid of breaking things. “Read-only Fridays” ban deployments before
    weekends. Junior engineers are not allowed to deploy. Change Advisory Boards
    exist to slow things down because slowing down is the only safety mechanism
    anyone trusts.
Modern observability
   Deployments happen multiple times daily. They are automated and routine.
   Engineers deploy confidently because they can see the impact of changes. Prob‐
   lems are detected quickly. Rollbacks are fast. Fear is replaced by evidence.
Fear is a signal. This test tells you that engineers do not trust their ability to see
what their changes will do. They are not afraid of deploying. They are afraid of not
knowing.
If your engineers are afraid to ship, they are telling you they are operating in the dark.
Listen to them.

After the Tests
The results of these five tests are not a scorecard. When an engineer cannot tell you
whether their code is working, when the same three people get pulled into every
incident, when midnight load spikes become folklore, you’re seeing evidence. You’re
seeing failures that can be named.
Closing those gaps is not about buying more tools or hiring more people. It is about
changing how your organization thinks about production, who owns what, and what
“good” actually looks like. That is organizational change.
You will face resistance. Vendors will fight to keep their contracts. Existing teams will
fight to keep their identities. Leadership will fight to avoid admitting that millions
were spent on the wrong things. The test results are your answer to all of it. Evidence
that cannot be dismissed as opinion. Language for a fight that is already underway.

Understanding the Resistance
The pushback you will face is not born of obstinacy. People resist because they have
real incentives to protect, deep professional identities at stake, and legitimate fears
about what change means for them. Any significant shift in how an organization
operates triggers an automatic response designed to protect the status quo.
The resistance has two dimensions. First, there is the organization itself (a system
that reflexively protects its current state regardless of who is pushing for change), and
second, there are the specific groups whose incentives make them natural sources of
friction. Understanding both is the first step to navigating either.


                                                            Understanding the Resistance   |   491
The Organizational Immune Response
Committees will form to “evaluate options” and slow-walk decisions until the initia‐
tive loses momentum. Pilot programs will be quietly starved of resources, and then
declared failures. Success criteria will be redefined so the current state qualifies.
Someone will produce a report showing that everything is fine, actually.
People aren’t being difficult for the sake of being difficult; it’s the organization’s
subconscious attempt to protect its current state.
Call attention to this dynamic, this antibody response, when you see it. “I notice
we’re forming a committee to evaluate something we already know doesn’t work” is
a sentence you may need to say out loud. The immune response defeats initiatives
silently. By naming it, you transform a silent, automatic response into a visible,
addressable problem. Naming it is how you fight back.
The immune response takes different forms depending on where it originates. Know‐
ing where it comes from makes it possible to address it.

Why Legacy Vendors Fight
Your legacy observability vendor is a business responding to the incentives of their
own market. Their contracts renew automatically. Their sales teams are compensated
for selling dashboards, storage tiers, and seat licenses, not for whether you can
actually debug production. Their product roadmap was built around the legacy three
pillars model because that is what the market bought for 15 years.
To navigate this resistance, you must recognize their “hard sells.” They might offer a
new AI feature as a magic bullet to fix the gaps in their underlying model. They might
suggest that your lack of insight is a tier problem, not an architectural one (and you
need their premium tier). They might suggest that you need professional services to
“optimize your implementation.” They say the tools are fine; you are just using them
wrong.
They are not evil. (They are not even wrong.) Their incentives are simply misaligned
with your goals. While they make money when you buy more, you succeed when you
understand more. Those are not the same thing, and sometimes they are opposites.

Why Existing Teams Resist
The people who built and maintain your current observability infrastructure have
rational reasons to be skeptical of change.
Their identities are wrapped up in current tools. “I’m the Splunk person” is not
just a job description. It is years of accumulated expertise, certifications, conference
talks, and professional reputation. Asking someone to abandon that in favor of a
brand-new tool is asking them to start over. It can feel like an existential threat.

492   |   Chapter 28: The Organizational Shift
This is vendor-silo alignment: when someone’s professional identity becomes insepa‐
rable from a specific tool. It is not a character flaw. It is a rational response to how
organizations reward and recognize people. Breaking it requires offering something
better, not just demanding they abandon what they know.
Their headcount is tied to maintaining current systems. If those systems go away,
what happens to them? “Unified observability” sounds a lot like “fewer jobs” if you
are not careful about how you talk about it.
Existing teams have seen this before. The last modernization initiative. The one
before that. Grand plans that stalled, got defunded, or declared victory without
changing anything. Why should this time be different?
And here is the thing: they are often right to be skeptical. Most tool consolidation
efforts fail. Most transformation initiatives die quietly in committee. Their skepticism
is earned through experience.
Your job is not to overcome their resistance. It is to show them something different.
Acknowledge that the odds are against you: most of these efforts have failed before,
and they know it. Be honest that their jobs will change. Then be specific about what
that means: their tool expertise does not become worthless, it becomes portable.
Their headcount does not disappear, it shifts toward work that does not require
heroics to sustain. The current effort is different not because you say so, but because
you can show them evidence they helped gather.

Why Leadership Is Skeptical
Executives remember writing large checks for the existing observability stack. “We
already invested millions in this stack” is not an excuse for not replacing it. It is
a sunk cost that feels very real when someone proposes spending more money
to replace what was supposed to solve this problem. For executives, replacing the
current stack may feel like admitting a multimillion-dollar mistake.
A vendor might suggest, “You just need to upgrade to the enterprise tier.” Executives
can see the option as being cheaper and easier than admitting the whole approach
was wrong. They also remember the last consolidation attempt. The one that was
supposed to rationalize the tool sprawl and ended up adding three more tools to the
pile.
They are not wrong to be cautious. Your job is to show them that modern observabil‐
ity is about capability—the ability to understand production—not about consolida‐
tion or cost savings. Those might come later, but they are not why you are advocating
for change. Connect observability to the outcomes they are already accountable for.
Find the business results that are bleeding:




                                                          Understanding the Resistance   |   493
  • Revenue lost to outages in critical transaction flows
  • Customer churn tied to reliability incidents
  • Senior engineer salaries consumed by war rooms instead of product development
  • Bugs discovered by customers instead of caught internally
  • Release cycles slowed by deployment fear instead of accelerated by confidence

When you can show that the current state is costing them something they’re already
measured on, the conversation stops being about tools and starts being about risks
they can’t afford to ignore.

Why You Cannot Fight It Alone
Since the immune response operates on an organization-wide scale, you cannot
overcome it alone. You must transform individual frustration into collective power by
recruiting others who recognize that the status quo is no longer sustainable.

Building the Coalition
To bypass the collective resistance to organizational change, you need allies, not
supporters in principle, but people who feel the failure personally and have the
standing to do something about it. The difference matters. Passive agreement doesn’t
survive the first difficult meeting. What you need are people with skin in the game,
credibility in the organization, and the willingness to spend political capital when it
counts. So, where do you find them?

Find the People Whose Pain Is Immediate
Look for engineers who dread being on-call, not because they dislike responsibility,
but because they know that only two people can actually debug anything. When they
get paged, they are not responders; they are dispatchers, escalating to the same two
seniors every time.
Look for SREs who spend hours in war rooms doing correlation by hand: “Check the
database dashboard. Now check the API dashboard. Now check the customer service
tickets. Does anyone see a pattern?”
Look for security teams that cannot trace access patterns across services, platform
teams that cannot answer “What broke after that deploy?”, customer support that
cannot explain to users what happened, and engineering managers whose teams
refuse to ship on Fridays.
These people already know something is wrong. They live it. You are giving them
language and a path forward.

Honor the Heroes Who Held It Together
Before you successfully transition to modern observability, someone is keeping pro‐
duction running. Probably several someones. They wrote the scripts that still execute
at 3 a.m. They know which dashboard lies and which one tells the truth. They
remember why that one service restarts every Tuesday and what happens if you let it
skip.
These people are not obstacles. They are organizational memory. The knowledge in
their heads is not written anywhere, and you cannot buy it from a vendor.
So it’s imperative that you include them in your transformation plans early. Not as
an afterthought once you have your mandate, but during coalition building. Ask for
their perspective. Learn what they have tried before and why it failed. Understand the
constraints they navigate that nobody documented.
I learned this the hard way. During a cloud modernization effort, I failed to include
the principal network architect in early planning. In my head, he was a dinosaur
attached to legacy. The champion of the old way. He represented everything I was
trying to change, so I worked around him.
This was a mistake.
He controlled IP address allocation. When the work needed network resources,
requests that should have taken hours took weeks. Approvals stalled. Questions
multiplied. Everything ground to a halt.
I had to go back, acknowledge my error, and give him the respect I should have
offered from the start. Once he was genuinely included, once his expertise was valued
rather than bypassed, the obstacles disappeared. He became an asset.
It’s easy to cast the keepers of legacy systems as opponents. They slow things down.
They ask hard questions. They keep raising failures you’ve never heard of. But that
framing says more about your assumptions than their capabilities.
The heroes who held things together have standing in the organization. They have
relationships, institutional credibility, and control over resources you may not even
know you need. If you exclude them, they can slow your work to a crawl without ever
saying “no” directly. If you include them, their credibility becomes yours.
This is not manipulation. It is recognition. They earned their standing by solving
hard problems with inadequate tools. Honor that. The transformation you are plan‐
ning does not erase what they built. It builds on it.

What Makes a Good Ally
Pain is necessary but not sufficient. A good ally feels the pain personally, not
abstractly. They are in the war rooms, not reading reports about them. They have
credibility in the organization, which means people listen when they speak. They are
willing to spend political capital, which is rarer than you think. And they will stay
engaged through setbacks, because there will be setbacks.
One committed ally beats 10 passive supporters. You need people who will show up
to the difficult meetings and say the difficult things.

Building the Case Together
Once assembled, your coalition has one job: build the case that cannot be dismissed.
This is evidence-gathering, not consensus-building. You are not trying to get every‐
one to agree.
Run the five tests together. Document the results. When the ownership test reveals
that engineers have no idea if their code works, write that down. When the two-or-
three people test shows that the same three seniors get pulled into every incident,
write that down.
Collect stories: the incident that took four hours because only two people could query
the logs, the customer escalation where support had no way to see what happened,
the deployment that went wrong and nobody knew for three days.
Measure the burden: how many on-call escalations per week, how many hours spent
in war rooms per month, what percentage of bugs are discovered by customers versus
engineering.
The point is to collect real stories that have meaning in your organization. When
someone says, “I don’t think we have a problem,” the evidence says otherwise, and
that is what converts your vision for change into the leverage needed to secure a
formal mandate.

Securing the Mandate
Allies and evidence are necessary, but they are not enough to successfully change an
organization. You need authority—the organizational power to make the new way
mandatory, not optional. You need a mandate.
A mandate means that new services get instrumented in the new way. This is not
optional. New teams onboard to new standards; they aren’t grandfathered into legacy
approaches. Legacy systems migrate on a published timeline, not “someday when we
have time.”



496   | Chapter 28: The Organizational Shift
This makes the change a policy, not a suggestion. Without it, you are just adding
another tool to the pile. Without it, teams will naturally default to whatever’s easiest,
which is usually the legacy approach they already know. You need an organizational
forcing function to change that.

Sponsorship Versus Authority
There is a difference between sponsorship and authority. Sponsorship means an exec‐
utive says “I support this initiative” in meetings. Authority means that an executive
can say “New services must use this approach,” and make it stick.
Sponsorship without authority is theater. You will get polite attention in steering
committees while nothing actually changes. Teams will nod along and then do what‐
ever is easiest, which is whatever they were already doing.
You need someone who can make the new way mandatory for new work. That is the
minimum viable mandate.

Finding the Right Executive
The right sponsor is not necessarily the CTO. It is often the person who owns the
pain most directly.
It might be a VP of engineering who is tired of explaining incident costs to the board.
It might be the head of SRE who owns MTTR and on-call burden metrics that are not
improving despite years of investment. It might be a business leader who keeps asking
“what happened?” and getting unsatisfying answers.
Follow the pain upstream until you find someone with budget authority and organi‐
zational patience. This is a multiyear journey; you need someone who will still care in
year two.

The Pitch That Works
Securing a mandate requires more than a list of technical requirements; it requires a
narrative that resonates with your leadership’s strategic goals. When you walk into the
room, you are not pitching a tool; you are pitching risk reduction, capability building,
and operational velocity:
As risk reduction
    “We cannot reliably debug production problems, and that risk is growing.”
As capability building
    “Only three people in the organization can effectively investigate incidents. That
    is a single point of failure across hundreds of engineers.”




                                                                 Securing the Mandate   |   497
As burden reduction
    “Our best engineers spend 30% of their time in war rooms. That is expensive
    talent fighting fires instead of improving our products.”
Do not frame it as tool consolidation, even if consolidation is a benefit. Consolidation
efforts have a bad reputation because most of them fail. This is not about consolida‐
tion, but about capability building.
Do not lead with cost savings, even if savings will come. Cost-driven initiatives
get cut when budgets tighten. Capability-focused work survives because it addresses
problems that do not go away.
If leadership fears writing another check that leads nowhere, open standards are
the answer. Vendor-neutral instrumentation means the work your teams do today
remains valuable regardless of which backend you choose tomorrow.
If the executive cares about connecting system behavior to business outcomes, note
that modern observability makes that possible. Legacy observability does not. You
cannot measure what you cannot see.
To succeed, your pitch must use the evidence you’ve gathered to prove that the
current state is no longer an acceptable business risk.

The AI Forcing Function
Here is something leadership may not see yet: the transition to AI-assisted develop‐
ment will break legacy observability entirely.
AI coding assistants generate code faster than humans can instrument it. AI-driven
systems make decisions that are difficult to trace with traditional logging. The
volume, velocity, and complexity of AI-augmented software development will over‐
whelm approaches built for human-speed workflows.
Organizations that lack real observability, the ability to ask novel questions about
production, will find AI acceleration makes them more fragile, not more productive.
They will ship faster and understand less. That is not a competitive advantage; it is a
liability.
This is not a pitch for AI observability tools. It is a warning that the pressure on your
current approach is about to increase dramatically. The sooner you build genuine
observability capability, the better positioned you are for what is coming.
Securing the mandate is the final piece that transforms your technical vision for
change into an institutional priority. You are now in a phase where progress is
measured by adoption rather than permission. Next, you need a plan to make the
new way a reality.

The Roadmap
You have the diagnosis, the coalition, and now the mandate. The mandate creates the
organizational authority that makes the new way required. But a mandate alone is
not enough. Organizations that rely only on enforcement get compliance at best and
sabotage at worst. Organizations that pair authority with a product mindset get gen‐
uine adoption. Your goal is enthusiastic adoption. Engineers instrument thoroughly
because you treated them like customers and listened to their needs, not because they
were mandated to.
You need a roadmap that provides the strategy for success and doesn’t collapse under
its own weight. Approach implementation with a product mindset, focusing on depth
over breadth and enthusiasm over compliance. Use the roadmap to build momentum
incrementally by proving the value of observability.

Start Small: One Domain, Full Depth
Pick one business domain with a willing team. Not the most critical system, because
you do not want that pressure. Not the most neglected system, because you do not
want to fight technical debt while proving a concept. Pick something in the middle:
important enough to matter, functional enough to instrument cleanly, with a team
that wants to try something new.
Instrument it completely. Wide events with full context. Request identifiers that flow
across every service boundary. Enough cardinality to ask arbitrary questions.
Do not do shallow instrumentation everywhere. Instead, do deep instrumentation
somewhere. Your proof point needs to be undeniable, not broad.
For success criteria, pay attention to whether questions that previously required a
war room can now be answered with a query. If you can demonstrate that, you have
something real. Protect this team and this proof point. It’s your ammunition for
expansion.

Pave the Path: Make the New Way Easier
Adoption that requires heroics will fail. If instrumenting correctly takes a week of
work per service, engineers will not do it. If the libraries are confusing, they will use
them wrong or not at all.
Build instrumentation libraries that developers actually want to use: golden paths
where a few lines of code give you context that flows automatically across service
boundaries, and defaults that are correct for 90% of cases.




                                                                       The Roadmap   |   499
The goal is to make the new way the path of least resistance. When the right thing is
also the easy thing, adoption happens. When the right thing requires extra effort, it
does not.

Platform Engineering Principles
Platform engineering thinking is critical to success here. Without it, observability
teams become order-takers or, worse, enforcers. Both fail.
The core principle: you have customers, and you must serve them. Developers. SREs
who respond to incidents at 3 a.m. The security team tracing access patterns. Cus‐
tomer support trying to understand what a user experienced. Leadership connecting
system health to business outcomes.
Each customer has different needs. Developers need fast feedback on their changes.
SREs need quick paths to root cause. Security needs audit trails. Support needs cus‐
tomer context. Leadership needs to understand how system behavior affects revenue,
retention, and reputation. Your instrumentation and tooling must serve all of them,
not just the loudest or most technical.
This means your observability team operates like a product team. You research what
your customers actually need, not what you assume they need. You build for their
workflows, not dictate new ones. You gather feedback continuously and treat com‐
plaints as data, not ingratitude. You measure success by adoption and satisfaction, not
by features shipped or standards published.
When any of these customers struggle with your tools, that is not resistance. That
is a product failure. When teams find workarounds, that is feedback. The market is
telling you something.
One last principle: build as little custom software as possible. Your competitive
advantage is not a better tracing library. It is making observability work for your
organization. Use open standards and open source tools, like OpenTelemetry, where
you can. Buy commodity capabilities. Build only what’s actually necessary, does
not exist, or is a unique requirement of your business. Focus your energy on the
integration, the developer experience, and the organizational change. That is where
you add value.

Demonstrate Wins: Rerun the Tests
Remember those tests that reveal whether you have real observability or just moni‐
toring with a new label? Run them again after six months into the transition:
The ownership test
    Do engineers now get feedback on their changes, or does code still disappear into
    a black hole?


500   |   Chapter 28: The Organizational Shift
The two-or-three people test
    Has the fire brigade expanded, or is it still the same three people?
The mystery test
    Are old mysteries getting solved, or just accumulating?
The arbitrary question test
    Can more engineers answer novel questions without escalating?
The deployment confidence test
    Are engineers less afraid to ship?
These are the metrics that matter. Track them visibly. When they improve, make sure
leadership sees the improvement.
You can also track business-adjacent metrics if they apply: time to resolve customer-
reported issues, hours spent in war rooms per incident, percentage of bugs discovered
internally versus by customers. These are things organizations already measure. Con‐
necting observability improvement to existing metrics makes the value concrete.
Celebrate milestones. Do not declare victory. This is a long journey, and premature
celebration invites complacency.

Expand Deliberately
With the mandate in place, new initiatives have modern instrumentation built in.
Legacy migrates domain by domain, not as a big bang.
Stop the bleeding before you heal the wounds. Every new service instrumented
correctly is one fewer service you need to migrate later. Every team onboarded to new
standards is one fewer team resisting change.
Set expectations clearly. This is a multiyear journey, so treat it as an operational
capability, not a project. Projects end; capabilities evolve. Organizations that try
to transform everything at once usually transform nothing. But organizations that
commit to sustained, incremental progress actually get somewhere.
If you can influence budget, structure the transition as an incremental replacement.1
Each year, a planned percentage shifts from legacy to modern. Year one might be
10%. Year three might be 40%. This creates momentum that compounds and makes
the transition inevitable rather than aspirational.
Use your early wins to evangelize. Engineers who have benefited from modern
observability become your best advocates. Let them tell their stories. Their credibility
with peers exceeds yours.

Building the Team
The coalition got you the mandate. Now you need a team to make it real. Transfor‐
mation requires people. Not just executive sponsors and coalition allies, but a team
dedicated to making observability work. Their role is not to do observability for the
organization. It is to make observability possible for everyone in it.

What the Observability Team Owns
The observability team does not exist to build dashboards for other people; they
exist to build the platform that makes manual dashboarding obsolete. They own the
platform. They build and maintain instrumentation libraries and SDKs. They define
standards for what good telemetry looks like. They manage vendor relationships and
tool selection. They own the developer experience for observability.
What they do not own is instrumenting every service themselves. That does not scale.
It is the quickest way to become a bottleneck. Their job is to make instrumentation
easy enough that product teams can do it correctly without heroic effort.

Capabilities of the Observability Team
Effective observability teams require a mix of skills that span the full lifecycle of code,
from commit to production incident. This is not a comprehensive list, and every
company might implement this differently:
Pipeline integration knowledge
    Someone needs to understand CI/CD systems, deployment infrastructure, and
    where telemetry gets injected automatically. This is the plumbing work that
    makes instrumentation happen without requiring heroics from every product
    team.
Production expertise
    Someone needs to have felt the pain of debugging at 3 a.m. They understand
    which information actually helps during incidents, which queries answer real
    questions, and which failure modes matter. Without this perspective, teams build
    instrumentation that looks complete but misses what operators actually need.

Developer experience
   Someone needs to care deeply about how engineers interact with observability
   tools and libraries. Confusing SDKs get misused. Bad defaults propagate every‐
   where. This is product work applied to internal tooling.
Customer understanding
    Someone needs to stay close to every audience the platform serves. Developers,
    SREs, security, support, and leadership all have distinct needs and workflows.
    The team that builds only for engineers will leave half its stakeholders under‐
    served.
Standards and architecture
    Someone needs to define what good telemetry looks like across the organiza‐
    tion: naming conventions, context propagation patterns, cardinality guidelines.
    Without this, every team invents their own approach and nothing correlates.
Vendor and tool strategy
   Someone needs to own relationships with vendors, evaluate alternatives, and
   make build-versus-buy decisions. The bias should be toward buying commodity
   capabilities and adopting open standards. Custom code should be reserved for
   genuinely unique requirements.
Integration
     Someone needs to make all the pieces work together in your specific environ‐
     ment: connecting tools to workflows, adapting generic capabilities to local con‐
     straints, and building the connective tissue that makes everything function as
     a system rather than a pile of parts. This work is easy to undervalue but often
     determines whether the platform succeeds or just exists.
These capabilities do not map one-to-one to job titles. A single senior engineer
might cover three of them, a larger team might have specialists in each, and a
large enterprise may choose to have a small team of observability specialists backed
by a multidisciplinary virtual team. The point is that all of these capabilities must
exist somewhere. Gaps in any one will limit the success of your observability
transformation.

Bringing the Existing Team Along
If you honored the heroes early, some of these engineers may already be in your
coalition. They believed in the transformation before it had momentum. Now you
need to define their roles in the new world. For the rest of the legacy monitoring and
observability team, this is your best chance to convert them into supporters rather
than detractors.




                                                                 Building the Team   |   503
Here is where most transformations fail: they treat existing monitoring teams as
obstacles instead of assets.
These engineers have skills you need. They understand production systems. They
know where the bodies are buried. They have relationships across the organization.
The question is not whether they are valuable; the question is what role fits their skills
and interests in the new world.
Simon Wardley’s model of pioneers, settlers, and town planners is useful here:
Some engineers are pioneers.
   They love greenfield work, new technologies, and building things that did not
   exist before. These people will thrive in the early phases of transformation: build‐
   ing new instrumentation, experimenting with approaches, and proving concepts.
   Give them the uncharted territory.
Some engineers are settlers.
   They take what pioneers create and make it robust, scalable, and production
   ready. Once your proof point exists, settlers make it real. They build the libraries,
   harden the infrastructure, and create the documentation. They turn experiments
   into platforms.
Some engineers are town planners.
   They optimize mature systems, reduce costs, improve efficiency, and keep things
   running. As your new observability approach matures, town planners ensure
   it stays reliable and cost-effective. They may also continue maintaining legacy
   systems during the long migration, which is valuable work that keeps the organi‐
   zation running.
The failure mode is forcing pioneers to be town planners, or expecting town planners
to be pioneers. Match people to phases of work that fit their skills and temperament.

Breaking the Vendor Identity Trap
Remember the identity problem? “I’m the Splunk person” felt like an identity because
it was. Vendor-silo alignment is not just an organizational obstacle; It is a career trap.
When someone’s expertise is bound to a proprietary tool, their value depends on that
tool’s continued relevance.
You break that attachment by offering something better: skills that are not trapped
inside one vendor’s ecosystem.
Open standards make this concrete. Engineers who learn OpenTelemetry are learning
industry-wide patterns, not your organization’s idiosyncratic tooling. “I’m the Splunk
person” becomes “I’m a telemetry architect,” a skill that travels with them regardless
of which vendor comes next.

What Not to Promise
When building the observability team, do not promise “No one’s job changes.” That
is not true, and saying it destroys trust. Jobs will change. Some people will thrive
in new roles. Some will find better opportunities elsewhere. Pretending otherwise
insults everyone’s intelligence.
Do promise: “We need your expertise, and here is how.” Be specific about paths
forward: pioneers get new territory, settlers get scaling challenges, and town planners
get optimization work. Everyone gets a path that uses their strengths.
The goal is an organization where every engineer can see what their code does. The
observability team exists to make that possible.

Conclusion
This is organizational change. It’s slow. It’s political. It’s worth doing anyway.
You will face setbacks. Initiatives will stall when sponsors change roles. Budget cycles
will squeeze your timeline. Champions will leave for other opportunities. Some pilot
programs will fail, and skeptics will treat those failures as proof that transformation
was never possible.
Keep going.
The organizations that succeed treat observability as a foundational capability, not
a project. Projects end; capabilities evolve. Projects have deadlines; capabilities have
roadmaps. Projects get funded once; capabilities get sustained investment.
The goal is an organization that learns from production, not just survives it. That
capability compounds over time. Teams with modern observability debug faster, ship
more confidently, and burn out less. They spend their energy building instead of
firefighting. That difference accumulates.
Teams without modern observability have to hold war rooms, accept mysteries, and
learn about problems from customers. They move cautiously because they cannot
see. They exhaust their best engineers on incident response instead of product devel‐
opment.
This chapter helped you recognize the gaps. You now have the tests to diagnose them,
the framework to understand resistance, and the roadmap to close these gaps. What
remains is the commitment to sustained effort and the patience to see it through.
In the next chapter, you will learn about how to decide which tools to use. Should you
build your own, buy from a vendor, use open source, or use a custom mix of all three?
