        Systems Thinking for Software Delivery




In the previous chapter, we explored how AI amplifies the existing behaviors of your
organization. The critical question then becomes: how do we optimize the specific
behaviors that define organizational learning speed? To answer this, we must look
beyond isolated tools and examine software delivery as a complex sociotechnical
system.
Every complex system has points of leverage, and software delivery is no different. By
taking a step back from individual tools and looking at the system as a whole, we can
find the right leverage points to accelerate organizational learning.
The 2025 DORA report describes this need bluntly: “successful AI adoption is a
systems problem, not a tools problem” (page 4). It also states, “when AI dramatically
accelerates software development, our control systems—that’s us—must also speed
up....We need fast feedback loops—faster than ever—to match AI-accelerated code
generation” (page 9).
In this chapter, we’ll use systems thinking to illustrate the feedback loops that shape
incentives, norms, workflows, and technical capabilities. We’ll look at what sociotech‐
nical systems are, what levers shape them, and the essential role observability plays in
creating effective feedback loops. You’ll learn which information flows to target—and
why those produce better results than changing budgets, metrics, or org charts.

Sociotechnical Systems
Let’s start by using systems thinking to examine the software delivery process. Systems
thinking means holistically understanding complex problems by viewing them as
interconnected wholes, rather than as isolated parts. Systems thinking focuses on the
relationships, patterns, and feedback loops between elements to show how actions in
one area affect the entire system.

The first thing to acknowledge about software delivery is that it’s a sociotechnical
system. As shown in Figure 24-1, a sociotechnical system is any system where
technology and human behavior are deeply intertwined. Changing the technology
changes organizational behavior, and changing organizational constraints changes
system behavior.




Figure 24-1. People and structure influence what work gets done, how it gets done, and

Your production environment isn’t just code and infrastructure; it’s also the teams
that operate it, the structures they work within, the incentives they respond to, and
the communication patterns they rely on. People, structure, incentives, processes, and
constraints all shape each other.
The behaviors you want are produced (or suppressed) by how those elements inter‐
act. You must adjust and adapt for both in order to drive meaningful change.

You Must Optimize Both the Social and Technical at the Same Time
The Trist principle states that organizations must jointly optimize their social and
technical systems for peak performance. Improving one while ignoring the other
reliably produces suboptimal outcomes.1
Every technical decision (architecture, tools, platforms) has social implications (team
structure, hiring, processes), and every social decision has technical ones. At senior

In practice, the most common violation of this principle is when managers, directors,

Changing Information Flows
In her seminal work on systems thinking, Donella Meadows identified a hierarchy of
intervention points in complex systems.2 The weakest are surface-level adjustments:
tweaking budgets, changing metrics, shifting locations. These create temporary
effects the system quickly absorbs and neutralizes.
If your organization carries the unresolved sociotechnical debts from Chapter 23,
surface-level tweaks won’t help—those are what got you here. You need to apply force
at points of leverage to create lasting change. The most powerful interventions change
the information flows that govern how the system behaves.
An information flow is the transmission of data, signals, or knowledge between
components of a system—influencing how the system acts, adapts, or maintains
stability. Changing information flows changes what people can see. That changes
their decisions and behaviors.
Effective change isn’t about how much effort you apply—firefighting consumes enor‐
mous energy but doesn’t change the system. To change the system, you need leverage
points and feedback loops.
When you change your observability, you change the information dynamics that
govern how hundreds of other decisions get made, every single day. It’s a change to
your underlying sociotechnical OS.

Observability is an information flow. It’s your code reporting back from production
with vital information about your users, your product, your system, and your code.
Most people understand this. Less widely understood is the role observability plays
in sociotechnical systems: it creates cyclical information flows—feedback loops. No
observability? No feedback loop. Just a chaotic mess of causes and effects happening
all over, with no logical link between them.
This focus on feedback loops as the core point of leverage is the foundation for
everything that follows.

How Feedback Loops Drive Change
Most organizations are swamped. Resources are overcommitted, goals are slipping,
teams depend on other teams that are also behind, and constant firefighting makes
progress hard. With so little slack capacity, how does anyone move forward?
The only way out is to identify key feedback loops and address them upstream. That
work will create shifts so that teams can go from fighting raging bonfires every day,
to fighting smaller trash fires every few days, to fires that can be easily stomped out
to—ultimately—preventing the matches from being struck in the first place.
Software delivery is composed of feedback loops. A feedback loop exists whenever
actions influence outcomes, and outcomes influence future actions, as shown in
Figure 24-2. Did a team invest in great tools because they were such a great team? Or
did investing in better tools make them a better team? (The answer to both: yes!)




Figure 24-2. A simple feedback loop: actions influence outcomes, which influence future
actions. Both arrows represent observability.

There are two types of feedback loops: amplifying and balancing. These describe
whether a loop accelerates change or resists it. All organizations have both.

Amplifying Feedback Loops Accelerate Change
An amplifying feedback loop (also known as a “reinforcing” or “positive” loop,3 as
shown in Figure 24-3) is a self-reinforcing cycle, where a change in the system trig‐
gers a response that increases the original effects, causing the changes to accelerate.
If you dropped a rock at the top of a snowy mountain, snow would stick to it as
gravity rolled it downhill, making it heavier, stickier, and faster as it rolled, until
it became (or set off) an avalanche. If you dropped the same rock at the bottom,
without the downhill slope to kickstart a loop, you would get…a dent in the snow.




Figure 24-3. Gravity and momentum take whatever is happening (snow accumulating
on a rolling snowball) and make more of it happen (the snowball gets bigger).

Amplifying loops can spiral upward into virtuous cycles or downward into death
spirals. They amplify whatever you’ve put into them. Amplifying loops are the source
of exponential, nonlinear effects in organizations, and they are a powerful driver of
change.

Balancing Feedback Loops Create Stability and Equilibrium
A balancing (or “negative”) loop counters change by pushing in the opposite direc‐
tion, as shown in Figure 24-4. If you heat a room, the thermostat will kick in to
regulate the temperature. Without it, the room becomes an oven.

Figure 24-4. A thermostat regulates temperature. Once the room drops below 75 degrees,
it warms. Once it exceeds 75 degrees, it cools. The system reinforces equilibrium.

Balancing loops create stability and maintain equilibrium. They resist spiraling into
growth or decline, and they are the source of most self-regulating and persistent
effects in organizations.

Small Shifts Can Trigger Massive Changes
When a long-stable situation suddenly experiences dramatic collapse or upheaval,
it’s rarely attributable entirely to some new force in the system. More likely, an
amplifying loop has been straining against a balancing feedback loop for quite some
time, until a small shift in circumstances overwhelmed its ability to balance.4
This is often the story behind a talent exodus: a company seems to have stable reten‐
tion for years, but then loses half its senior engineers in three months. Beneath the
surface, dissatisfaction had been building for years: arbitrary promotions, canceled
initiatives, few advancement opportunities—an amplifying loop. But inertia, golden
handcuffs, and a tough job market kept people in place—a balancing loop.
Then one well-respected engineer quits, lands somewhere great, and posts about
it online. Other unhappy employees realize that leaving is possible; the balancing
loop is removed. More leave. The perception forms that “everyone’s leaving,” and
departures snowball until it becomes a reality.

The Difference Between a Virtuous Cycle and
a Death Spiral Is the Ability to Self-Correct
The difference between a virtuous cycle and a death spiral is rarely about intent. It’s
about whether the system can see clearly enough to correct itself. Let’s look at an
example.
Suppose team A decides to break up their monolith. They spin off one new service, it
works well, deployment gets faster, and they feel accomplished. They create another,
and then another. A virtuous cycle begins.
In the virtuous scenario, they instrument each service carefully, tracking cross-service
latency, deployment frequency, and failure rates. When their eighth service starts
causing timeouts and cascading failures, they see it immediately in their traces and
are able to understand why. They recognize they’ve hit the limits of their operational
maturity and pause. They invest in better tooling, improved debugging, and more
on-call training. Then they resume. The feedback loop gave them the visibility to
self-correct.
Now suppose that same team had no ability to see small failures—basic logs and
metrics, no traces or precision tooling. When the eighth service causes the same
problems, they may not even notice. If they do, they can’t explain why. They guess
that “better service isolation” is the answer, so they create three more services to “fix”
the dependencies, which makes debugging even harder. Incidents start taking longer.
They add more services to “simplify,” and conditions degrade further. Engineers start
leaving because nothing works. Two years later, the system is unmaintainable and the
team is working on a costly rewrite.
The intent in both scenarios was identical. The engineers were always trying to do the
right thing. The difference was whether the team could see clearly enough to know
when to stop, adjust, or reverse course.

Feedback Loops in Software Delivery Systems
Feedback loops are continuously at work in your software delivery organization. As
mentioned in Chapter 23, all core capabilities are loops:

  • Deployment: I shipped code. → What happened? → Adjust.
  • Code review: I wrote this. → Does it meet our standards? → Improve.
  • Incidents: Something broke. → What and why? → Fix and learn.
  • Product development: We built this feature. → Are users getting value? → Iterate.




                                                Feedback Loops in Software Delivery Systems   |   441
Each loop requires sensing, interpretation, and response. Observability is the sense
mechanism that enables all of your feedback loops to function. It’s the information
channel that connects actions to consequences across deployments, incidents, and
product decisions.
When sensing is degraded—lossy, laggy, unpredictable, wrong, or missing—organi‐
zations adapt in ways that reduce productivity. They slow down, batch changes,
introduce heavyweight approval processes, and institutionalize workarounds. The
system becomes safer in the short term but slower and less adaptable over time.
The quality of your observability plays a key role in how your sociotechnical systems
interpret, respond, and take future actions. Let’s look at how signal quality translates
into outcomes.

Observability in Amplifying Loops
Amplifying loops accelerate and escalate with each iteration, making them powerful
drivers of behavior change. Without good observability, your software development
loops are far more likely to spin into doom loops than build toward virtuous cycles.
Here are three concrete examples.

The confidence loop
High-quality observability creates a confidence loop, building the confidence to
deploy more frequently, leading to small-batch releases that are easier to debug. Fast
feedback enables faster issue detection, which decreases MTTR. Engineers learn to
trust their ability to fix problems quickly, so they take more risks and move faster.
The loop builds momentum with every cycle.
Poor observability triggers the opposite: fear of the unknown leads to infrequent
deployments. Changes pile up, deployments become large and failure-prone, and
incidents take longer to resolve. Deployments become something to fear. The team
slows to a crawl.

The learning accumulation loop
The learning accumulation loop is a virtuous cycle that rewards curiosity and creates
more of it. Engineers who can easily find answers ask more questions. They build
deeper, more accurate mental models, which enables faster debugging. Their intu‐
itions improve, driving continuous exploration—and expertise compounds, as shown
in Figure 24-5.

Figure 24-5. An amplifying loop escalates with each repetition until acted upon by
another force or new information. Amplifying feedback loops are the greatest drivers of
change in an organization.

But when answers are hard to find, engineers stop asking questions, as shown in Fig‐
ure 24-6. The system becomes opaque and teams rely on shallow pattern-matching,
and critical knowledge concentrates in a few individuals, creating serious institutional
risk if they leave.

The intention behind amplifying feedback loops and death spirals is usually the
same. People generally try to do their best. The difference in direction is driven by
whether they have enough clear, timely, accurate observability data to identify what’s
happening and self-correct.

The system quality loop
The system quality loop is one where robust observability lets engineers detect and
resolve small anomalies in real time—like subtle latency spikes and edge-case errors.
That creates a clean baseline, making future issues easier to spot and allowing quality
to compound.
Without it, small defects accumulate into a “normally broken” state. New prob‐
lems are masked by background noise. Teams get trapped in a firefighting cycle,
addressing only the most catastrophic failures while reliability and resilience steadily
degrade.

Observability in Balancing Loops
Balancing loops play a critical role in ensuring continuity and stability of operations.
But in order to balance, they need timely, accurate information.
Without good observability, balancing loops fail to provide balance or equilibrium;
instead they create wild oscillations or freeze up into risk aversion and stasis.

Incident involvement breadth
During major incidents, teams pull in more people for faster resolution and broader
learning. But too many people creates coordination chaos, while too few means
missed learning opportunities. The system should match incident participation to
complexity and learning value.
Without observability, teams either create incident mobs (where everyone gets pulled
in, and nobody knows who’s doing what) or exclude everyone except the hero engi‐
neer (where learning doesn’t spread and that person just burns out). They can’t tell
whether more people helped or just created confusion, and they swing between “all
hands on deck” and “too many cooks.”
With observability, teams can measure resolution time against participation, and
track learning spread against coordination overhead. They can detect patterns, like
“engineers who shadow more than three incidents learn to resolve independently,
but shadowing more doesn’t help.” They can build structure—defined roles, spectator
modes for learners, recordings for broader sharing. The system calibrates: the right
people in the room to solve it, the right people observing to learn from it.

Observability cost and usage regulation
Engineers instrument systems and run queries, consuming storage, compute, tokens,
and network resources. If they have no visibility into what that costs, they can’t
self-regulate.

Partial Feedback Creates Systemic Distortions
So far in our examples, we’ve assumed that observability is either very good or very
poor. Reality is usually more nuanced.
Inconsistent observability quality can be more damaging than uniformly poor
observability. A false sense of security builds while risk quietly accumulates. High-
quality observability needs to be present throughout the critical paths of your soft‐
ware delivery organization. When it isn’t, the effects ripple everywhere:
Feedback is lossy: you only see part of what happened.
    You deploy a change and the error rate doesn’t spike, so you assume it worked.
    But you’re not seeing that latency increased for a specific customer segment, or
    that a code path is swallowing errors, or that the change broke an edge case
    affecting 0.5% of users.
      You make decisions based on incomplete information. You might even double
      down on the bad behavior—“that deploy went fine, so let’s do more like that”—
      when it actually caused problems that you just can’t see.

Feedback is laggy: you see what happened, but only much later
    You ship on Tuesday. A customer complains on Friday. By then, you’ve shipped
    40 other changes. You can’t isolate cause and effect, and the feedback loop cycle
    time is so long that you can’t iterate on what you learn.
    You’re flying in the dark. You’re making the next decision before you know if the
    last one worked. That’s like trying to learn to ride a bike when the bike’s response
    is delayed by 30 seconds; you’re going to crash.
Feedback is unpredictable: sometimes you see things and sometimes you don’t.
    A cryptic error appears on your dashboards—twice a day? Three times? It’s hard
    to tell because the ingestion pipeline sometimes hiccups. You can’t tell if you’re
    seeing a real pattern or just gaps in the data.
    When this happens, engineers stop investigating intermittent issues because
    they’ve learned they can’t trust what they’re seeing. The feedback loops exist
    in theory but fail in practice.
Feedback is wrong: the signal is misleading.
    Your metrics say everything is fine, but customers are angry. Or alerts fire con‐
    stantly for nonissues, so engineers learn to ignore them. Eventually, your teams
    start missing real problems.
    False positives and false negatives both destroy the feedback loop—one by creat‐
    ing noise that drowns out signal, and the other by creating false confidence.
    Engineers learn that the observability system lies to them, so they route around it
    with intuition and heuristics.
Feedback is missing altogether: it’s an open-loop system.
    This may be the most insidious of all. You’re making changes with no way
    to know if they worked—or even worse, you do have a way, but no one ever
    looks. Every deployment is a coin flip. Engineers develop superstitions and self-
    imposed constraints—the classic, “don’t deploy on Fridays.” Because engineers
    have no way to confidently connect cause and effect, problems strike with no
    warning. The system is degrading over time in countless small ways, but nobody
    can see them until they are big enough to fail catastrophically. So, catastrophic
    failures are all you get. This is like driving with your eyes closed and only turning
    the wheel after you hit something. Of course you’re going to drive slowly: you’re
    terrified, and you should be.
Degraded feedback doesn’t just slow you down; it actively makes you worse. With
good feedback, you learn from every action and apply it to the next. With bad
feedback, you learn the wrong lessons, building incorrect mental models and losing
trust in your ability to change things safely. So you stop changing things, and the
system ossifies.

And because these are amplifying loops, degradation accelerates. Slow feedback leads
to less frequent deploys, which means even slower feedback. Lossy feedback leads to
incorrect fixes, which create more problems and add more noise.
The worst part is that engineers adapt to broken feedback loops by creating work‐
arounds that become institutionalized. They may create elaborate test environments
that can never replicate production, change approval boards that slow everything
down, or runbooks that are perpetually out of date. They may establish “safe deploy‐

All of these are theatrical attempts to add safety when you can’t see what your code
is doing. What you end up with is an organization optimized for not breaking things
rather than making things better. Which may even sound prudent, until you realize
that in a competitive landscape, standing still is falling behind.

Leverage Points in Sociotechnical Systems
Observability is a powerful capability, but it operates within a larger hierarchy of
system influence. To apply force for maximum impact, you need to find leverage
points—places where a small shift produces large changes everywhere else.
In software delivery, information flows and feedback loops are powerful leverage
points. But there are others outside the domain of observability.

Leverage Points and the Limits of Observability
In Thinking in Systems, Donella Meadows describes 12 leverage points in any complex
system, ordered roughly by their power to produce change.
The following is an abbreviated subset in the context of software delivery, from
highest to lowest impact. While points 1, 2, and 3 are not explicitly about information
flows, they do require high quality signals in order to function and course correct.
And points 4, 5, and 6 are exactly within the domain of observability:

 1. Goals: The goals refer to the purpose or function of the system. They’re the
    highest leverage point available to most executives. Individual contributors who
    don’t “own” the goal can and should play a vital role in vetting and influencing
    the goals that are set.
 2. Self-organization: Self-organization is the ability to add, change, or evolve system
    structure. Resist the urge to read this as being about org charts: this is about
    agency and autonomy, especially at the team level.




448   |   Chapter 24: Systems Thinking for Software Delivery
 3. Rules: Rules, followed or not followed, lead to incentives, punishments, and
    constraints. The right guardrails add consistency and predictability, and prevent
    everyone from writing their own database from scratch.
 4. Information flows: This refers to who does or doesn’t have access to information.
    Missing or flawed information flows are among the most common sources of
    dysfunction in any system. Information can also be lost in a flood of undifferenti‐
    ated detail.
 5. Amplifying feedback loops: These are the source of explosive change. The more a
    loop works, the more power it picks up to do more work.
 6. Balancing feedback loops: Though not drivers of change, they are essential none‐
    theless. As Meadows says, “a system with an unchecked reinforcing loop ulti‐
    mately will destroy itself ” (page 155). Balancing loops are reservoirs of stability
    and resilience.

Goals, autonomy, and rules are extremely powerful points of leverage, and while good
observability can help you design, build, and refine these things, it can’t overcome the
lack of them.
Feedback loops that are timely, precise, and relevant enable self-awareness in humans
and self-governance in teams. They generally produce the right sociotechnical system
behaviors without needing constant correction or oversight.
Excellent signal and fast feedback loops will never compensate for a lack of purpose,
disempowerment, or unchecked chaos in the system. But it’s equally hard to set the
right goals without good information.

Push in the Right Direction
One final caution: Meadows observes that people embedded in a system tend to
intuitively sense where the problems are. They can describe the system, identify the
forces at work, and guess with remarkable accuracy where the best leverage points
are.
However, people have a pronounced tendency to push the right leverage point in the
wrong direction.
Deploys are a classic example. Everyone knows that deploys introduce change, and
change can be hard. Earlier generations of software professionals saw this too—and
responded by introducing change advisory boards, monthly release trains, and a slew
of processes that made it slower, harder, and more dangerous to ship. They found the
right leverage point, but they pushed the wrong way.
Something to remember as you go looking for leverage points to push in your own
organization.

Conclusion
Let’s go back to the overarching goal: AI makes your ability to move swiftly with confi‐
dence more important than ever. The limiting factor for how fast your organization
can move is increasingly how fast you can validate and learn from the code you just
shipped.
AI amplifies the systems you already have. Fast, accurate feedback loops compound
your gains. Slow, lossy loops compound your failure modes. More buggy code
shipped means less understanding, and more complexity to navigate means moving
at a slower pace, with fewer chances to learn.
This is why observability is such a powerful leverage point. It is the only intervention
that can turn amplifying loops into virtuous cycles while also stabilizing balancing
loops toward self-regulation and healthy persistence.
“Observability” is many things: a type of software, a flow of information, and the
sense mechanism that creates feedback loops from cause and effect. When you
improve the quality of this signal, it becomes an accelerant that unlocks agency
and makes coordination easier. Improving it doesn’t just make one thing better—it
accelerates every feedback loop and thus your organization’s ability to learn, decide,
and adapt.
In the next chapter, we’ll apply these sociotechnical systems concepts specifically
to observability. In successive chapters, we’ll show you how to diagnose your cur‐
rent state, make the business case, and drive the necessary change inside your
organization.
