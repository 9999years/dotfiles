In the previous chapters, we established the why and what of accelerating organiza‐
tional learning speed through feedback loops. In this chapter, we get into the how.
Amplifying feedback loops can spiral into virtuous cycles or death spirals. Balancing
feedback loops can create stability or devolve into chaotic swings or rigidity. What
makes the difference between learning and flailing is how you’re using those loops,
and the quality of the observability tools feeding data into them.
We’ll start by looking at how feedback loops typically work in most organizations.
Then we’ll contrast them with the feedback loops that actually accelerate learning,
and examine why closing that gap has been hard for so many teams. We’ll look at
two generations of observability tooling and which loops each one supports, and we’ll
close by examining how AI is both accelerating the need for change and opening up
new ways of working.
This chapter focuses on recognizing the capabilities you need so that you can choose
observability tooling that fits. The next few chapters build on that by examining
the business case, how to diagnose your current investments, and how to drive
organizational change.

The Landscape Feels Noisy Because the Labels Are Noisy
Before we begin, let’s talk about the elephant in the room: the “observability” category
has become almost unfathomably large. It started as monitoring, logs, and application
performance monitoring (APM) thrown into a blender and quickly metastasized into
a budget line item that eats up to a third of infrastructure spend in some companies.
With that growth came aggressive marketing. The term is now applied to a wide

range of tools and niches: data observability, AI observability, large language model
(LLM) observability, and more.
As a result, many vendors claim similar outcomes using similar language, despite
products (and outcomes) that are fantastically different. Unfortunately for you—the
reader of this book and the consumer of those tools—that makes the entire landscape
a snarled, expensive mess. Let us be the first to say: we’re sorry. This is not what we
intended when we first coined the term.
But the lessons from earlier in this section can help. Zooming out, a systems lens
offers a cleaner organizing principle: instead of starting with vendor categories or
feature lists, start with the feedback loops you need to strengthen, and the decisions
those loops need to support. Our goal is to learn fast.
Let’s start with the loops that most organizations run today, and then look at the ones
you actually need.

The Loops Most Organizations Run
Today (and What Is Missing)
Despite the last decade and a half of DevOps culture and “you build it, you run it,”
the reality for most teams is this: of the two main feedback loops—development loops
and operational loops—only one of them includes production.

Development Feedback Loops
The software development feedback loop is fast, but isolated. It’s optimized for imple‐
menting and validating business logic. The phases are: build → run tests → iterate →
merge → move on, as shown in Figure 25-1.




Figure 25-1. The software development feedback loop focuses on local builds, running
tests, and iterating until tests pass. The loop continues until code is reviewed and merged.

Most developers live in their development environment, their task tracker, their chat
app, and a handful of other tools. They build, run tests, and learn. They build, run
tests, and learn—again and again—until they deploy to an integration environment,
click around manually, and submit a pull request. Once it’s reviewed and merged, the
job is done. On to the next feature.
Developers know their code will eventually get deployed to production, but they
often don’t know when or what happens afterward. Unless there’s a bug or an inci‐
dent, they typically don’t look at production at all.
Most organizations don’t have a feedback loop connecting developers to their changes
in production. The only loops that include production learning are operational ones.

Operational Feedback Loops
Operational teams run a different loop, oriented around service health. The phases
are: alert → investigate → fix, as shown in Figure 25-2.




Figure 25-2. The operational feedback loop protects service health. It’s triggered by an
alert, escalation, or customer complaint, moves to triage and investigation, and ends
when a fix is deployed.

For ops teams (i.e., DevOps, SRE, platform engineering, cloud engineering, or other
names), the feedback often comes from dashboards, alerts, and customer complaints.
They are typically working reactively, often scrambling to investigate, learn, and fix
what went wrong after it has already affected customers.
The ability to learn from these loops is limited, because it’s often not obvious what
caused the problem. Alerts are triggered by thresholds—which means they fire only
after the impact is significant, once a performance issue is bad enough to threaten the
whole system. Sometimes the operational loop is triggered by a recent deployment.
Far more often, it’s a customer complaining about something that shipped days,
weeks, or months ago, as shown in Figure 25-3.

When caused by an issue that shipped long ago, the issue is often the result of
combinatorial conditions. For example, when an issue has been lurking for a while,
it’s usually the result of compounding conditions. Data volume reaches a threshold
that causes timeouts, which stacks up threads, which triggers the alert. That’s why
operational loops more often look like Figure 25-4.




Figure 25-4. A more common operational feedback loop spans weeks, months, or years.
The compounding conditions that trigger an alert often surface long after the code
changes that caused them.

This is where the disconnect between development and operations becomes clear.
Operational loops are oriented around bugs, errors, reliability, and outages. Those are
important. But reliability is only a tiny part of what developers actually need to know
about their code in production.
Most developers should be asking questions like: how are users behaving? Is this
feature getting adopted? Where are users dropping off? Why are they quitting? How
does what we shipped map to what we intended? Reliability and bugs are part of that
picture, but not the main one.

Most developers don’t ask these questions, because they don’t have the tools to easily
ask these questions. As we discussed in the last chapter, if curiosity gets punished, it
eventually stops asking, and the opacity of the system becomes its own reinforcing
loop.

The Missing Loop
What’s missing is a consistent loop that connects the developer’s concern of “I wrote
this” to “I can see and understand what it did in production.” The developer loop
stops at merge; the operational loop starts when something breaks. The gap between
them is where learning slows down and costs compound, as shown in Figure 25-5.




Figure 25-5. In most orgs, there is no connection between the developer loop and
production: the developer loop stops after code is merged. The operational loop only
begins when something is visibly broken. The gap between the two is where velocity goes
to die.

The terms “inner loop” and “outer loop” are sometimes used for dev and ops, respec‐
tively. Fast iteration on the inner loop is fine—but once you’re ready to put code into
production, you need to follow it there and validate it.
When the developer loop doesn’t include production, all “learning” comes from tests.
Tests are a proxy for production. But as any experienced engineer knows, what you
learn when all your tests pass is…that all your tests pass. Depending on the quality of
your test suite, that may be a lot or a little, but it is never a substitute for what you’ll
learn in production. Production always surprises you, because people are agents of
chaos.
In order to accelerate organizational learning, we must build feedback loops that
make it easy to connect developer intent with consequences in production, because
that is the loop where value gets created.

Shipping Is Your Heartbeat
Every time you ship a diff, you create new value for the business, as shown in
Figure 25-6. This is the core loop of value creation, and it probably happens many
times a day.

As Darragh says, “shipping is your company’s heartbeat”—so it should be as com‐

This is a theoretical loop, of course.
This is how progress gets made at any tech company: build → deploy → observe →
learn → then wrap what you learned into the next thing you build (see Figure 25-7).
A healthy delivery system executes this loop repeatedly, applying what was learned

How to Build the Loop and Close the Gap
Production is a zone of chaos. Your system may be processing millions of requests
per second across thousands of services for millions of customers. In that environ‐
ment, even a fleet of AI agents scanning for anomalies is of limited use. Weird things
are always happening; weird things are the norm. You can’t distinguish actionable
signal from baseline noise just by looking at the whole haystack.
But if you know exactly what you’re looking for, when it happened, and where to look,
even very small needles—a single weird request—can be found quickly and easily
with the right tools.
To drive real sociotechnical change, the person who wrote the code needs to be able
to see what it’s doing, understand how users are experiencing it, and continuously
improve it from that understanding, as shown in Figure 25-9.




Figure 25-9. A developer encodes their intent in instrumentation, then deploys, and then
validates that intent in production. Precision tooling surfaces small issues missed by
preproduction testing. The developer adds fixes, redeploys, and looks again—and notices
something interesting: new user signups are climbing, driven by users from Finland. They
keep exploring to find out why.

You can’t close the loop by having one person write the code and another person
watch production. You can’t close it by having a developer glance at a dashboard

of high-level aggregate metrics. And you can’t close the developer learning loop by
connecting it to a downstream operational loop.
The way you close the loop is by tracking developer intent, from writing or generat‐
ing the code all the way through to production, and then validating that intent using
rich context data and precise observability tools.
“No spike in errors” is not precise. Precise looks like this: “Compaction rate for long
strings went up 5% and disk footprint went down 30% for requests with a specific
build_id, combination of feature flags, and test users on our 10% canary deploy
versus the 90% baseline—and the distribution of return codes looks unchanged.”

Why Closing the Gap Is Rare: The Economics of Cognitive Load
As you read this, it can sound so simple and powerful to close the gap. We believe it
is both. Yet very few teams—and almost no large organizations—actually operate this
way. Why?
Organizations often blame cultural factors: developers don’t care about production,
or they don’t use the tools available to them. A more useful explanation is that the
tools haven’t been worth the time and energy it has taken to use them.
The industry is trapped in a circular dependency loop: they don’t have precision data,
so it isn’t worth the lift for developers to look at their changes in production, so they
don’t look, so the tools never get any better.
Our systems evolved to tolerate a certain rate of change, held together with duct tape
and intuition. The old ways worked well enough, most of the time, so there has been
no competitive pressure to jolt us into disruptive tool changes.
AI looks like it may finally break this logjam by attacking on multiple fronts. AI
provides the technical breakthroughs, the competitive pressure, and the disruptive
need for developers to follow their changes into production, since that is the only way
to know what nondeterministic agents will do there.
Let’s look at the two generations of observability tooling on the market today, and the
feedback loops each was designed to support.

Two Observability Models, Two Feedback Loops
There are two generations of observability tooling on the market today: the three
pillars model and the unified model, as discussed in Chapter 1. We will recap them
briefly here, along with which feedback model each excels at solving.




                                              Two Observability Models, Two Feedback Loops   |   459
Three Pillars Model (Built for Operational Outcomes)
The three pillars model evolved out of the monitoring and logging space. It’s designed
for infrastructure, where engineers must operate hundreds if not thousands of third-
party components that they did not write and cannot change. Your networking gear,
hard drives, and container IDs spew out a steady stream of metrics and/or logs.
You have limited ability (or desire) to configure this output; you just need to find
somewhere cheap to stash it. So you stream metrics into a metrics store, and logs
into a logging store, and maybe traces into a tracing engine. (And if you have them,
you do the same for profiling data, errors, exceptions, anomaly detection, product
analytics, and so on. You can see where the cost ballooning comes from, since you
end up storing many different copies of the same data.)
This model is well-suited for infrastructure and the operational loop. It dominates
the ecosystem of integrations, so it’s easy to get started with. Where it flails—hard—is
with developer loops.
To understand the experience of each user or the impact of each change shipped,
developers need precision data, exploratory capabilities, and fast feedback loops. The
relationships between the datapoints are the most important parts of the data; the
three pillars approach destroys these at write time.
Any time you need to crosscut dimensions like user experience, application behavior,
or business context, users of this model will face correlation difficulties. The three
pillars model requires a lot of manual correlation and intuitive leaps. It works well
enough for fixing bugs and responding to incidents, but not enough to understand
what happened, the quality of your product, or how users are using it.

Unified Storage Model (Built for Developer Learning)
The second model, unified storage, emerged from the intersection of business intelli‐
gence and APM tools. In this architecture, signal types are all ingested as structured
event logs into a single, unified data store, typically backed by columnar storage. All
the relationships are preserved, removing the need for manual correlations or pillar
hopping (see Chapter 5).
Columnar storage engines support both high cardinality and high dimensionality
(defined in Chapter 1), so they support very precise questions.
Precision is a function of context richness and cardinality. The beauty of context
is that it makes data exponentially, combinatorially more powerful. If you have 29
fields on a single trace or canonical event, and you add a 30th, that 30th is more
powerful than all 29 combined, because you can now generate exponentially more
combinations and subsets of dimensional detail.



460   |   Chapter 25: The Observability Landscape Through a Systems Lens
We know these things intuitively. The high-cardinality identifier “Patrick Snuffleupa‐
gus” is more identifying than just “boy,” and neither is valuable out of context.
This model is well-suited for understanding the quality and behavior of your appli‐
cation code and the experience of each user. It supports the developer feedback
loop (deploy → observe → learn). Its flexibility and speed make it easy to turn
questions into fast feedback loops. This model works exceptionally well for turning
deployments into developer learning.
The unified model shines at answering exploratory, open-ended questions like, “Show
me all requests from mobile users in the European Union with feature flag X enabled
that had errors in the checkout flow.” These questions are impossible to formulate on
the fly with the three pillars model.
Where the unified model struggles is with operational infrastructure monitoring. It
can do some of this work, but it does not shine there.

AI Changes the Game and Opens New Interaction Models
At the time of writing, the SDLC is undergoing a compressed period of change. New
patterns and philosophies for code generation seem to emerge every passing day.
While we don’t know yet where we are headed, we can identify some patterns in
broad strokes.
AI has upended the economics of software development. Writing the code used to be
the most time-consuming and expensive part for many organizations; now it’s all but
free. The constraint has moved from “code producing” to “code coherence.” We can
produce artifacts much faster than we can reason about them.
The bottleneck has shifted from generating code to validating code. Generation is
cheap; confidence is not. In a world where most code is disposable and regenerable,
code functions more like a cache. And most of the code in the world is not green‐
field code; it’s brownfield or blackfield code,1 where production is the sole tattered
remnant of developer intent. If it’s going to become regenerable, that code will first
have to cross over, as discussed in Chapter 2.
Today, wise engineering leaders are laying the foundation for their teams to cross
over to generated workflows, and they are dealing with the problems that are already
here. The most pressing of which is this: when software changes faster than an
organization can understand its consequences, risk accumulates faster than it can be
resolved (or code simply never gets reviewed). The learning feedback loop becomes the
limiting resource.

AI can reduce or eliminate much of the friction that developers have encountered
by trying to fit infrastructure/ops tools to their use case, and MCPs (Model Context
Protocols) may have some ability to bridge the pillars and derive insights.2 AI can
deliver data directly to developers in their dev environments or via Slack.
Ultimately, much of the pain of the developer loop will be best solved by agentic
workflows. Developers can declare intent, write or generate code to implement it, test
it, deploy it, and validate that intent in production. Agents can do the tedious work
of checking back over time, circling back every hour, day, or week, so that even slow-
burning issues (a memory leak, a cache drift) can be identified and remediated before
they are big enough to pose a threat to the system and thus trigger an operational
alert.
Some of this can also be contrasted with a complementary approach: adversarial
“continuous review” agents that do things like flagging potential memory leaks,
inspecting if important constraints of the system are being respected, and so on.
Faster feedback loops are a unifying theme here, whether the entity receiving the
feedback is a human or not.
This is not just convenience; it’s a structural way to reduce loop friction and increase
learning speed. The pieces for this exist today. But they require a very specific techni‐
cal foundation: unified, structured, high-context events that AI can reason about. The
business intelligence approach is what makes these outcomes possible.

Both Feedback Loops Matter: Lead with the Right One
Operational feedback loops are essential safety mechanisms. They monitor your
infrastructure, track aggregate system health, and ensure you’re meeting your SLOs.
They’re the primary way to understand infrastructure health, and they’re the safety
net that catches the big, system-threatening problems that slip through testing, stag‐
ing, and progressive deployment.
But they can’t be the only mechanism by which developers learn what their changes
did—not if you want to develop at the speed that modern competition demands.
Operational feedback loops are downstream, delayed, and oriented around system-
threatening conditions. They’re driven by customer complaints and system failures.
By the time an operational loop activates, users have already been impacted and your
team is already in reactive mode.
Developer-facing observability moves learning upstream—toward prevention, itera‐
tion, and product quality. Developer feedback loops let you see problems forming,

Align on the Outcomes You’re Building Toward
The observability landscape can be legitimately overwhelming. Many of the hundreds
of observability vendors are functionally identical, other than pricing and packaging.
Other vendors are meaningfully and radically different under the hood. As a cus‐
tomer, it’s hard to tell the difference, because vendors are, how shall we say, “not
bound by technical accuracy in the sales process.”
But you do not need to start off by gathering detailed feature comparisons or per‐
forming elaborate bake-offs. A more effective way to start is by getting leaders aligned
around one question: which feedback loop is your bottleneck, dev or ops?
If your pain is operational stability—keeping systems up, getting visibility into infra‐
structure, managing chaotic incident response—then tools built for operational out‐
comes are the right place to begin. The three pillars model is mature, robust, and
purpose-built for infrastructure and other operational use cases.
If your pain is delivery velocity—i.e., you need to ship swiftly, with confidence, and
understand the quality of your product as experienced by each customer—then tools
built for developer learning are where to start. The unified storage model is built for
this.
If your pain is due to an unprecedented influx of AI-generated code that no one
understands, or if you are preparing to transition from handcrafted, artisanal code
to code written and reviewed by AI, or if you need to trace agentic workflows in
production systems, then you too should focus on developer learning.
There is no universal right place to start. However, most organizations are starting
from a place where they have invested heavily in their operational feedback loops
and very little in their developer loops. This is despite the fact that ops loops are
supposed to be a safety net, not the primary driver of organizational learning, and
are supposed to deliver rapidly diminishing returns on marginal investments once
stability is reached.
In sports terms, most organizations are blowing their wad on the goalie. They aren’t
even staffing the rest of the field, just giving the goalie more and more dough. And
then they wonder why they can’t score any points.
Most organizations need both operational feedback loops and developer feedback
loops. But you can’t build a business case without first building alignment around
which problem you are trying to solve, and which feedback loop is at the heart of
it. Without that shared understanding, you can spend limitless sums yet still fail to

produce meaningful sociotechnical change. The observability landscape is complex,
but the feedback loops that matter are not. Start there.

Conclusion
In this chapter, we cut through the noise of the observability landscape by focusing
on its core purpose: accelerating organizational learning through feedback loops. We
established that the reality in most organizations is defined by a critical gap between
a fast, isolated development loop and a slow, reactive operational loop. Relying solely
on operational feedback for production learnings means losing out on the continuous
learning necessary for value creation.
If you want to build at the speed of AI, you need to learn and validate at the speed
of AI as well. That means learning from production after each change, through
the developer feedback loop. Fortunately, AI is not only driving the need for these
capabilities—it’s also powering new workflows that enable them and substantially
reduce cognitive load.
In the coming chapters, we’ll get practical—covering the business case for observabil‐
ity, how to evaluate your current investments, how to drive organizational change,
when to build versus buy, and how to build strong vendor partnerships. All of these
practical steps depend on first getting clear on what you’re trying to accomplish.
