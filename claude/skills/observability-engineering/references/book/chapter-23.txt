The most disruptive consequence of the AI era isn’t faster code; it’s that your organi‐
zation’s learning speed has become its single greatest bottleneck. In this chapter, we’ll
address two uncomfortable truths: (1) in an AI era, the new constraint is how fast
your org can learn, and (2) the biggest drag on learning speed in most orgs is unpaid
sociotechnical debt.
Like technical debt, sociotechnical debt comes from choosing quick and easy sol‐
utions in the moment that create more work later. Most technology shifts have
required a corresponding change in how people work to actually succeed. But chang‐
ing behavior is often much harder than changing tools. Organizations that haven’t
made those behavioral changes have accumulated inefficient processes, misaligned
leadership, and unresolved cultural problems.
These debts show up as reduced speed, engagement, and flexibility. Left alone, they
can limit a company’s ability to grow, learn, compete, and ultimately survive in
fast-moving markets. For most organizations, this debt hasn’t been a dealbreaker—
yet. Most know it’s there, but they’ve worked around it. The common attitude is:
“we’ll fix it later.”
Boards (and competitive threats) have been pushing execs to develop an AI strategy,
so they can capitalize on faster delivery. And all that accumulated debt is now about
to collect a lot of interest, because:

  • In the AI era, software delivery is no longer constrained by how fast you can
    write code. It’s constrained by how fast you can validate, understand, and learn.
  • For software delivery, observability is what enables validation and understanding,
    which establishes the baseline for organizational learning speed.

    their own core product offerings. Those tools work well for infrastructure and
    operators, but lack the precision, context, and connective tissue it takes for soft‐

Many organizations have tried to make these technical shifts, but haven’t fully

An Open Letter to CTOs
Dear CTO,
By now, you’ve probably survived multiple “everything changes now” moments:
cloud, DevOps, microservices, Kubernetes, serverless, edge computing, and more.
Each wave delivered real capability. And over time, each one revealed the same
uncomfortable truth: most organizations change their infrastructure much faster
than they change how they work.
Beneath the surface, many fundamental behavioral patterns have stayed the same.
Dev teams still act like production is what happens after development is finished, not
like it’s the penultimate stage of the development process (and the only validation that
ultimately matters). Production is still organized around protecting the health of the
service against all threats, but anything that doesn’t threaten the health of the system
is poorly understood (or it is not known). Incidents still trigger blame, heroics, and
alert fatigue. And “transformation” still too often means adopting a new platform,
buying new tools, and renaming some teams—without reworking the incentives and
feedback loops that actually drive behavior change.
Now we’re in the latest “everything changes” moment with AI. And that shift has
revealed something exciting and new: AI doesn’t eliminate the need for engineering
discipline. It eliminates the excuses for not having it.
“Moving fast” used to mean shipping a few times a week without breaking produc‐
tion. Now it means moving at the speed of AI—from idea to code to deploy to

around rigor; it’s a stress test of it. Like the shifts before it, AI reveals another uncom‐
fortable truth—it doesn’t just make creating software faster; it makes everything
faster, including your mistakes.
That’s why this shift feels so urgent: you’re under pressure to roll out AI quickly, and
you can see all the failure modes—drift, regressions, compounding risk—waiting for
any organization that tries to scale speed without discipline.
The real advantage in the AI era is being able to accelerate without crashing. Your
engineering teams need to move swiftly and confidently. Their habits, feedback loops,
and operational clarity need to be strong enough that AI amplifies excellence rather
than chaos. Intuition does not scale. All the duct tape and baling wire that hold your

The behavioral patterns you’ve built up through cloud, DevOps, microservices, and

was years ago. The next best time is now.
That’s what we’re here to talk about. Which debts actually matter? Where do they fit

The Sociotechnical Debts That Will Hold You Back
Sociotechnical debt is the hidden, accumulated cost of misalignments between an
organization’s social structures (culture, teams, processes) and its technical systems
(software, infrastructure).1 Like technical debt, not all sociotechnical debt needs to be
(or ever can be) fully paid off. But debts that put your most important goals at risk
must be addressed.
Our guiding principle is this: any sociotechnical debt that prevents your teams from
moving swiftly and confidently today will also be a blocker to their ability to acceler‐
ate and build, ship, and validate at the speed of AI.
These debts are not the only obstacles in your path. AI skills and literacy are vital
for engineers, managers, and execs alike. Organizational scaffolding must be built,
guardrails established, tools tested and bought and rolled out. But as we said in
Chapter 1, this is not a book about AI. This is a book about observability, written for

When Developers Live In a World of Tests, Not Reality
In retrospect, the DevOps movement was trying to do something deceptively simple:
build a feedback loop between developers and their code in production. The point
wasn’t more automation; it was to bring developers closer to real outcomes—users,
reliability, cost, performance—so they could learn from each diff, and their software
would continuously improve.
We failed to achieve that goal. Twenty years later, most orgs are still stuck with dev
versus ops and a split-brain problem. Most developers live in a loop of “build, test,
merge,” and then move on to the next task while someone else handles deploying it.
Developer intent is forgotten and lost before it can be validated, and developers go on
viewing production as a place they go to fix bugs sometimes—not the source of truth
of user experience.
In those organizations, the only team with production in their feedback loop is
operations (also known as DevOps, infrastructure, platform engineering, SRE, etc.).
Feedback arrives as alerts, escalations, customer complaints, and support tickets. That
loop is necessary, but it’s laggy, lossy, and threshold-driven. It tends to surface issues
only after they’ve grown large enough to threaten the whole system, rather than when
they first begin to degrade user experience or product quality.
Elite engineering teams close that gap. They wire production signals back into daily
development so teams can learn from each release while the context is still fresh.
That’s part of what makes “move swiftly with confidence” possible: frequent deploy‐
ments, short lead times, lower change failure rates, and fast recovery when things go
wrong. Elite performance doesn’t happen by luck. It happens because teams can see
the effect of small changes and respond early.
That gap—between teams that have closed the dev/production loop and teams that
haven’t—has always mattered. AI makes it the ballgame. When code generation
accelerates, the constraint shifts completely: generating code is no longer the hard
part. Validating it and building confidence in it is. How soon can your teams detect
the impact of a change, understand it, and adjust? How small and precise are the
anomalies you can detect?

AI will help you ship lots of changes very fast, while the truth about what happened

A developer ships code on a Friday. On Monday morning, do they know how it per‐
formed in production—latency, error rates, user behavior—because that’s a normal
part of their workflow? Or, do they find out something went wrong two weeks later
when a support ticket lands in the wrong inbox? If it’s the latter, production isn’t
in your developers’ feedback loop. You’ve structurally chosen slower organizational
learning. That has to change.
Has your organization built fast feedback loops between development and production?
Is instrumentation a habit? Can every developer pinpoint the effects of the last diff they
shipped?

When Telemetry Gets Treated Like Infrastructure, Not Product
Here’s the debt that quietly piled up: as systems got more complex, most organiza‐
tions kept treating telemetry the way they always had—as something ops maintains,
something you bolt on, something you optimize for cost. They didn’t change how
developers related to their systems. They just built bigger monitoring stacks.
It was a reasonable response at the time. Back in 2009, a $50k monitoring bill seemed
reasonable. Most teams were running LAMP stacks. A senior engineer could more or
less reason about the whole system in their head. Monitoring—predefined indicators,
thresholds, alerts—worked well enough for that world.
Then came microservices, dynamic infrastructure, ephemeral containers, serverless,
edge, multi-cloud. Complexity didn’t just grow; it distributed. No single person could
reason about “the system” anymore, because the system was constantly changing. The
right response would have been to change how teams understood their systems—to
treat telemetry as a product capability, owned by developers, instrumented intention‐
ally, designed for exploration. Most organizations instead chose to go broad: more
tools, more dashboards, more alerts, more “visibility.”
When observability emerged in the late 2010s, that choice became explicit. You could
go deep—changing how your people understood your systems—or you could go
broad, piling on more signals without changing any of the underlying workflows or
architecture. Most orgs went broad. Most vendors, facing pressure from customers to
give them “observability,” simply rebranded the products they already had. The result
was a buffet of monitoring tools with observability logos on them.
The bill kept growing. A Gartner reference customer went from spending $50,000
a year on monitoring in 2009 to $24 million a year on “observability“ in 2024—a

48% year-over-year increase for fifteen consecutive years. That figure isn’t an outlier;
observability now accounts for 20–30% of infrastructure spend at most companies.
And yet: production is still poorly understood. Incidents still trigger blame and
heroics. Alert fatigue is still everywhere. The cognitive dissonance for CTOs is that
they’re paying observability prices for monitoring outcomes—large spend, sprawling
stacks, and workflows still dominated by thresholds and painfully slow debugging.
That mismatch was tolerable when change was slower. At AI speed, it becomes a
critical blocker. The limiting factor has shifted from “how fast can we produce code?”
to “how fast can we understand and validate that code in production?” AI increases
the rate of change, and a higher change rate increases the surface area for small, gran‐
ular failures—subtle latency spikes, partial outages, edge-case errors, cost regressions,
correctness drift. These won’t trigger a monitoring alert. They’ll compound quietly
until they become cascading outages. Teams that can’t see them early will find them
late, struggle to explain them, and won’t be able to trace them back to the specific
change that introduced them. That turns speed into risk.
When something goes wrong in production, can anyone on the team identify what
happened, regardless of their experience or familiarity with the system? Or, do most
incidents start with on-call engineers searching for the senior “rockstar” who holds all
the institutional knowledge and knows which dashboard to check? Do incidents take
15 minutes to resolve if a particular engineer is there, and 45 minutes if they aren’t?
If that sounds familiar, your sociotechnical practices haven’t changed. You’ve chosen
slower organizational learning.
Did you make the shift to truly observable systems, or did you just keep adding more
monitoring?

When Developer Tools Were Never Designed as Products
Internal engineering tooling was never designed: it accumulated. And it accumulated
from the infrastructure side of the house, where it was optimized for keeping systems
running, not for helping developers understand what their code does. Developers
were never treated like customers. Nobody ran discovery on their workflows, nobody
optimized for their cognitive load, nobody asked what would make doing the right
thing the easy thing.
The result is that “add good telemetry” isn’t one task from a developer’s perspective—
it’s a maze of decisions. Should that telemetry be a metric, a log line, a trace, a
profiling signal, an error, or an exception? If it’s a metric, should it be a counter,
a gauge, a timer, or a histogram? What cardinality is expected? After deployment:
when did your code actually ship? How many other changes went out at the same
time? Where does the telemetry you just wrote even show up? Navigating all of this
demands deep operational knowledge and uninterrupted time. Most developers don’t

have either. So the desired behaviors don’t materialize—even when everyone knows
what they are and agrees they’re important.
Platform engineering is the organizational response to that indictment. Done right,
it brings product and design thinking to a domain that has historically had none
of it, treating internal developers like customers, running discovery on their actual
workflows, and building paved paths that encode best practices into defaults, stan‐
dard libraries, and guardrails. Reliability and observability stop being heroic acts and
become the path of least resistance.
But most organizations have faced a fork here, and most have taken the wrong
turn. The first failure mode is treating platform like a project: launch it, celebrate,
then move the team on to other work. The second is pure rebranding: renaming an
existing DevOps or infrastructure team “platform engineering” without changing the
operating model, discovery process, or the orientation toward developer needs. Same
team, same incentives, same infrastructure mindset. Developers are still not being
treated like customers. The debt persists under a new name.
At AI speed, that gap becomes existential. AI increases throughput, which means
inconsistent practices and missing feedback loops get amplified exponentially. Your
wisdom must be encoded into the system—in defaults, in templates, in the path
of least resistance—because at 10x change volume, you cannot rely on developers
remembering to consult the right docs. If the default path is accumulated infrastruc‐
ture cruft rather than intentional product design, both your developers and your AI
will go off the rails. And they’ll do it faster than ever.
Pick a developer who joined in the last six months. Ask them to instrument a new
service correctly and show you what it does in production. Did they know where
to start? Did they get there without pinging three people on Slack? Or, did they
end up with something half-done because the right path was too hard to find? If it’s
the latter, your best practices are aspirational, not operational. You’ve chosen slower
organizational learning.
Does your organization use product and design principles to ensure that developers
using your internal tools naturally do the right things—because that’s the path of least
resistance?

These Debts Will Sabotage Your Adoption of AI
If these fundamental behavioral dynamics haven’t changed in the last 15–20 years,
why should they change now? This is a fair question, and the answer is that AI
has exposed a pattern that was always true: delivery speed is not the same thing as
learning speed.

AI makes it easier to produce software changes. But DORA’s 2025 findings underline
the catch: rather than creating organizational excellence, AI simply amplifies the
behaviors your organization already has. If your engineering system has strong
feedback loops and disciplined practices, AI compounds the advantage. But if it’s
held together by lagging signals, inconsistent standards, and operational heroics, AI
compounds the chaos.
That’s why the “fix it later” era is over. These behavioral debts become painfully
visible when change volume accelerates. The best time to have built organizational
excellence was incrementally, throughout the earlier waves of technological change.
The next best time is before you scale the consequences. What you’ve done in the past
and what you’re planning next determines how urgent this should be. Fixing these
issues now is a matter of risk management.
There’s also a fundamental shift in where developer attention should go. As code gen‐
eration gets easier, the highest-value work for developers increasingly shifts toward
system-level reasoning and user-centric decision making. Developers should focus
their cognition on understanding intent, impact, and verification: delivering what
users actually need, knowing how systems will respond, anticipating what could
break, and using production feedback to confirm or challenge what the team believes.
That shift is what unlocks your competitive advantage.
But that higher-order thinking only works if the organization has already made pro‐
duction feedback and system understanding part of daily development—supported
by the right practices and observability capabilities, rather than delayed escalations.
Reducing uncertainty contains costs. Empowering engineers to deliver value and
understand their systems is what drives talent retention.
Platform engineering is the practical bridge. It reduces cognitive burden and encodes
best practices into the default workflows that both developers and AI tools use. That’s
how you keep humans and AI aligned with the right way to build software, instead of
hoping everyone remembers to consult a wiki page while shipping 10x more changes
than ever.
Ultimately, the “why now?” is competitive. In the short term, everyone will see a pro‐
ductivity boost—all teams are shipping features faster than ever with AI. But without
validation, that boost will be short lived. Engineering teams that bake production
feedback into development will learn faster while shipping better customer experien‐
ces. Teams that don’t will ship more changes, but they’ll just be compounding chaos—
and that chaos will reclaim the gains through regressions, incidents, customer pain,
and even more debt to unwind.
In an AI era, will your team compound advantage or compound chaos? How prepared is
your organization to win at that challenge?

Turning the Ship Around
If these sociotechnical debts still exist in your organization, here’s the good news: you
can change this. The path isn’t mysterious—it mostly comes down to deciding what
you’re optimizing for, and then building the feedback loops to support it.
The proven strategy is to decide: how to measure value, what to build, and who builds
it. Then be clear on which actions need to change and ensure you have organizational
support. Let’s break this down.

Measuring Value
The observability tools you choose will be central to driving change. The specific
vendor matters less than whether the tools actually enable the workflows and deliver
the value your engineers need. The right choice should be evaluated on two criteria:
external outcomes (customer experience and business impact) and internal outcomes
(developer capability and delivery performance).
Unfortunately, many CTOs measure observability tools against neither. For that
cohort, observability is about operational outcomes: is the service up or down, is
something slow, is something erroring? It’s treated as a cost center and managed
like one. The only times developers are expected to look at production are for
bug fixes and incidents. In that scenario, there’s often little support for driving the
sociotechnical changes that matter.
Some CTOs have shifted toward measuring the value of observability through cus‐
tomer experience and business impact. That’s a step in the right direction, but still an
incomplete view for our goal here, which is to validate and accelerate organizational
learning speed.
Simply put, you must measure the value of observability against its ability to acceler‐
ate both developer learning and delivery speed—at the same time. Getting this right
matters enormously. This dual value is what reduces uncertainty, changes human and
system behaviors, retains talent, and creates a flywheel that accelerates organizational
learning. If it’s off, it slows down—or simply doesn’t help—your ability to turn things
around. (For more detail, see Chapter 26.)
For now, understand that observability can fundamentally change how information
flows through your systems. You’ll use it to drive learning feedback loops.

Build Good Feedback Loops
Your engineering organization is not a factory. It’s a complex sociotechnical system.
Engineering organizations are no longer constrained by their ability to produce code,
they are constrained by their ability to validate that code and learn from it. That is
why building good feedback loops is a strategic choice, not a tactical tooling upgrade.


428   |   Chapter 23: Organizational Learning Speed Is Now Your Biggest Constraint: An Open Letter to CTOs
Software delivery is built on feedback loops:

  • Deployments: Did the change work in production?
  • Code review: Is this safe, coherent, and maintainable?
  • Incidents: What broke and why, and what assumptions were wrong?
  • Product development: Are users getting value?

Observability is what connects the dots and closes the loops. It’s the shared sensemak‐
ing layer that turns production signals into understanding. When observability is fast
and accessible, the cost of curiosity is low, and engineers learn to ask more questions.
Systems become more transparent, teams catch small issues early, and mental models
get refined continuously. Learning compounds, and that compounding creates the
“swiftly and confidently” motion you’re after.
When observability is slow, expensive, or requires specialized expertise, the opposite
happens. Engineers stop looking. Production feedback collapses into delayed alerts,
helpdesk tickets, and customer complaints. Systems become opaque. Teams slow
down because uncertainty drives coordination overhead. Knowledge concentrates on
a few overburdened experts. Learning stalls.
This is why precise, excellent, ergonomic observability is one of the highest-leverage
investments an organization can make. It is the infrastructure for organizational
learning. This makes it cheap to validate each change and connect the dots between
developer intent and customer impact, which is what you must do to move at the
speed of AI without turning throughput into risk.

Change Actions That Drive This Strategy
A mindset shift is also necessary before teams can move swiftly and confidently at the
speed of AI.
Many organizations are spending more than ever on “observability,” yet still operating
with the same mindset they developed around monitoring. The result is predictable:
bigger stacks with bigger bills, and the same outcomes—noisy alerts, slow investiga‐
tions, and unhappy customers.
This mismatch is largely structural. Monitoring and observability are designed for
different jobs. As we discussed in Chapter 1, logs and metrics were built for opera‐
tions teams to monitor infrastructure: they enable operational outcomes. Modern
observability was built for software development teams to understand their product
and how users are using it; it enables software dependability and delivery outcomes.
If your organization uses monitoring-era tools and behaviors to pursue dependability
and delivery outcomes, it will keep getting monitoring results. The following three

shifts are necessary to convert observability from tool spend into organizational
capability.

Match tools to architecture and engineer workflows
If your architecture is distributed but your observability tooling is optimized for
monolithic-era workflows, there’s a mismatch. The cost shows up as friction: engi‐
neers have to manually stitch together context, and feedback loops slow down.
The legacy three pillars observability model—separate tools for metrics, logs, and
traces—often reinforces this mismatch. It creates cognitive overhead by forcing engi‐
neers (or AI in some cases) to decide where to start, jump between systems, reconcile
identifiers and timestamps, and make error-prone correlations. That overhead breaks
the fast learning cycles required to ship confidently at higher rates of change.
The observability vendor you choose matters less than functional fit: does the tool
enable fast, iterative investigation across signals and services, and does it support the
workflows that engineers need to connect change to impact? Make sure your tooling
matches the outcomes you’re building toward.

Run observability as a platform, not infrastructure
Observability shouldn’t be “owned” as a tool-operations function. The mission isn’t
to keep dashboards alive. The mission is to make it effortless for every engineer to
understand what their code does in production, as close to real time as possible.
That requires a product mindset: internal developers as customers, paved paths and
defaults, strong onboarding, and continuous improvement. It also means measuring
success in human terms: time to understanding, ease of investigation, adoption, and
confidence after a deploy—not just uptime and data volume.
This is also why rebranding an existing DevOps or infrastructure team as a “platform
team” often fails: if the operating model doesn’t change, the outcomes won’t either.

Treat product observability as an investment, not a cost center
When observability is managed as a cost center, the organization optimizes for
reduced spend rather than increased capability. At AI speed, internal capability
becomes the constraint. If engineers can’t see what they’re doing, throughput turns
into risk, coordination overhead grows, and learning slows.
Observability inherits the properties of the code you observe. If the code is a cost
center, observing it is also a cost center. But if the code is an investment, if it’s what
makes you money and is why you exist as a business, then building better products
and more performant interfaces is also an investment. That’s a job for observability.

Agentic workflows break the long-held mental model of “developers own preproduc‐
tion, operations own postproduction.” Nondeterminism is a feature with agents.
The only way to understand what they are doing is by instrumenting the code and
observing what they do.
Managing observability as an investment shifts the focus toward the outcomes you
actually need: faster feedback loops, better understanding, and sustained delivery
performance. We’ll cover this further in Chapter 26.

Make it real
Together, these three shifts make the earlier strategies executable. Without them, you
can talk about AI-driven productivity gains all you want—and for a while, those gains
will even be real. But eventually, as your team compounds risk with each uncertain
deployment, that productivity erodes and your debts collect interest.
Putting these shifts into practice should look something like this:
Adopt the loop of institutionalizing a deploy, observing, and learning.
   The best time to learn is right after you ship, while developer intent is crisp and
   context is fresh. If you rely on incidents or escalations as the primary learning
   mechanism, you miss most of the signal (see Chapter 25).
Treat observability like an internal platform product.
    Build like a product, enable developers, and optimize for adoption and time to
    understanding so that production learning becomes routine and scalable (see
    Chapter 28).
Align goals and spend to business outcomes.
    Choose tools and workflows based on the feedback loops you need to strengthen,
    and avoid the trap of paying observability prices for monitoring outcomes.
You’ll know these changes are working when the median time shrinks between a
new deployment and a validated understanding of customer impact (for example,
measured as mean time to detect and interpret impact). You should also see a shift
in where issues are first discovered (for example, measured as the percentage of mate‐
rial incidents first identified through internal telemetry versus customer reports or
support escalations). And your teams should start adopting the learning loops you’ve
provided (for example, measured as the percentage of deploys with a post-deploy
outcome check completed within a defined window).
With these shifts, you can lay the groundwork for your teams to accelerate their
learning, move swiftly with confidence, and turn those productivity gains into com‐
pounding competitive advantages.

  organization successfully operationalized fast organizational learning to transition
  to an AI-first culture. His perspective shows how these principles translate into
  competitive advantage in a fast-moving market.


The thing that sparked Fin into existence was an unignorable itch to make business
online a little less awful. To put an end to: “Dear valued customer,” “You are ticket
number 123,” and “Do not reply to this email.” Instead, to make business online
personal and human. Like you’d expect in a store you love: a friendly welcome,
personalized service, and real care if something goes wrong.
To do this, we made Fin. It didn’t magically make your business better, but for people
who cared, it gave them the right tools. And many did care. They realized good
service was good for business.
These tools didn’t exist yet. The shape and surface area weren’t figured out. And
figuring out the shape of a product—then evolving and refining it—benefits hugely
from fast feedback loops:
      Do a thing. Learn something. Do the next thing.

That is the core function of a software team. Do it well, pair it with product vision
and judgment, and you can build great products, fast.
Doing a thing means putting software in customers’ hands. Until you ship, your
software creates no value. If shipping is sluggish, risky, or error-prone, you’ll do it
infrequently—and sluggishness infects your entire company and culture. If shipping
is fast, reliable, and high-confidence, you’ll do it often. Frequent shipping means
frequent learning, better decisions, and faster progress in the right direction.
We wrote this down 13 years ago as: “Shipping is your company’s heartbeat.” It rings
true now more than ever.
And then came the AI wave.
Times of change force you to ask: what really matters? What do you keep? What do
you change?

For us, the focus on enabling incredible customer service stayed constant. But we
saw a clear line of sight to a future where AI could actually do the work of customer
service—and could do it faster and better than humans. That led to the birth of Fin,
the highest-performing AI agent for customer service. We pivoted our entire research
and development team to building it, and shifted our culture to be AI-first. Fin is now
on a trajectory to be far more successful than our original product ever could have
been.
Once again, we’re in uncharted product territory: a huge amount of software to build,
an uncertain shape, and fast-evolving technology. But that plays to our strength—
shipping and iterating at speed. And AI itself has given us new tools and agents to
move faster than ever before.
But it’s also made me appreciate the most essential skill of software engineers, espe‐
cially in an era where AI threatens to eat the job of writing code: the ability to
understand what’s really going on in the messy, wonderful systems we create. Without
fast understanding, you lose confidence and precision. You’re either guessing, or
wasting endless time trying to figure things out.
To be a great engineer, you need to be great at:

  • Understanding your customers: How they use your product, what’s working,
    what’s not
  • Understanding your production systems: Why something is slow, why something’s
    broken, where to fix it
  • Understanding how your software is built and deployed: Why it’s brittle or slow in
    places, and what it would take to improve it

Each of these gets harder in the AI era, which only increases the importance of this
core skill:

  • Products are more complex. Instead of deterministic behavior, AI is probabilistic.
    It will surprise you. It won’t just work because it worked on your machine.
  • Production systems now depend on costly, slow, unreliable (but magical) LLMs.
  • Development environments are cohabited by AI agents that are prolific at producing
    “sort-of-working” code. Your job is to harness the upside and contain the down‐
    side—while moving at speed.

The complexity ratchets up, but the truth becomes clearer: the bottleneck isn’t ship‐
ping (we’ve largely optimized that), and it isn’t even writing code (which is now easier
than ever). The bottleneck is understanding. Developing the depth and speed to know
exactly what to do next to make your product or system better.

If shipping is your heartbeat, then understanding is your breathing. Thankfully, we can
use AI tooling for faster and sharper understanding. The deeper and more effective

Conclusion
In the AI era, your organization’s competitive advantage is no longer determined by
how fast you can ship code, but by how fast you can validate code and learn from it.
Observability, when operationalized as a core platform capability, is the only system
that can bridge the gap between developer intent and production reality at the speed
of AI. It’s the only way to accelerate without turning your increased throughput into
systemic risk.
