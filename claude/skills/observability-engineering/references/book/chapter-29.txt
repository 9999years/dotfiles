          Build Versus Buy (Versus Open Source)




The choice between building, buying, or adopting open source software (OSS) is
rarely a simple technical comparison; it’s a high-stakes economic decision that deter‐
mines where your organization’s most precious resource—engineering cycles—will be
spent. While generating code has never been faster or easier in the era of AI, the
long-term cost of maintaining bespoke infrastructure remains as high as ever. Every
hour your team spends maintaining a nondifferentiating internal tool is an hour they
are not spending on the core products that drive your market share.
This chapter provides a framework to help you navigate these trade-offs with clear
eyes. We will examine the true costs—both obvious and hidden—of each approach
and help you recognize whether your investments will compound into a competitive
advantage or simply add to your technical debt. By the end of this discussion, you
will be able to distinguish between the infrastructure that should be a commodity and
what justifies custom development.
After all, build versus buy is not a binary choice; it’s a multidimensional spectrum.
Pretty much everyone is going to end up buying some software, using some open
source, and writing some code of their own. The important questions are: which,
where, and why?
This topic is not new, but the trade-offs and relevance have shifted quite a bit in
recent years. As mentioned in previous chapters, many companies now spend more
on their observability bills than almost on any other category of tools, second only to
cloud spend.

Which Do You Hate More: Your Money or Your Time?
There’s an old DBA joke: Oracle is what you use when you love your time and
hate your money; MySQL is what you use when you hate your time and love your
money. (Hey, we warned you the joke was old. Kids, pretend we said “DuckDB” and
“Snowflake,” OK?) While the technologies have evolved, the fundamental trade-off
remains constant.
In every build-versus-buy discussion, you are choosing which type of pain to tolerate:
The “hate money” path
    Teams on this side of the spectrum are all but impervious to sticker shock.
    They view engineering focus as their most precious and finite resource. To them,
    money is “cheap” if it buys back time and focus for core product innovation.
The “hate time” path
    For these teams, capital is the scarcest resource. They may find it easier to justify
    $1 million in engineering headcount than a $100,000 tool budget. They’re willing
    to accept the long-term costs to avoid an upfront bill.
The mistake most teams make is failing to realize that they are making this choice.
They view building an internal tool as “free” because the salaries are already paid,
ignoring the staggering opportunity cost of what those engineers aren’t building
instead.

Surfacing Our Biases
Now that you have considered whether you prioritize protecting your capital or your
engineering cycles, we must look at why your answer is likely skewed by human
nature. Let’s explicitly name some of the biggest biases and fallacies that reliably show
up in the build-versus-buy debate.
First is our own bias as employees of an observability software vendor. We’ve seen
over and over again that organizations radically underestimate the true cost of build‐
ing their own solutions, or even the cost of operating open source tools. What looks
like a cheap solution adds up swiftly once you factor in salaries, cloud spend, and
opportunity costs. As a result, we tend to think buying a solution provides better
value.

Second, and conversely, is the engineering bias. As Rick Clark—formerly of Master‐
card, VMware, Cisco, and Pivotal Software, whose experience we’ll be drawing on
extensively in this chapter—says: “Any time a CTO asks their engineers if they should
build or buy, the engineers are going to say ‘build.’ Engineers want to build just like
surgeons want to cut—they have a professional bias toward their skill set.”
This bias is human nature. We became engineers because we love to build. But just
because you can build something doesn’t mean you should. The opportunity cost
of building nondifferentiating infrastructure can be staggering. Maintenance costs
always dwarf the costs of building by a margin that’s large enough to be scary, yet
unknowable up front.1
Those two may be the biggest biases, but there are many more that skew the debate.
The sunk cost fallacy shows up regularly: “We spent so much time building this; we
can’t abandon it now!” and so does the NIH (not invented here) bias: “We are such a
special snowflake; no one else can possibly understand our requirements.” There are
valid fears of vendors going out of business, or using vendor lock-in and data gravity
to raise your prices. Migration itself may even loom as an outsized fear in your mind,
if your organization has tried and failed to migrate in the past.
By naming these biases up front, we can move past the emotional appeal of “building
it ourselves” or the marketing appeal of “buying the solution.” If you are running the
buying process, encourage each stakeholder to lay their real fears on the table so you
can collectively evaluate their degree of risk.
There are frameworks you can use to evaluate your options and drive the process
to a wise conclusion; we will introduce two in this chapter. But before we apply a
standard decision framework to this choice, we must acknowledge a fundamental
truth: observability isn’t like any other software.

Observability Is Not Like Other Software
For the most part, we have been treating observability like any other piece of soft‐
ware. It is not. Observability is the software that watches the watchers. This means
you probably have higher reliability requirements for your observability tools than
for other types of tools, and you may want to think seriously about how that risk is
bundled.
The biggest risk is this: if you run your observability services on the same cloud
provider, in the same region, using the same stack as the rest of your production
systems, it’s going to go down or suffer degraded service any time that any of your

The Four Quadrants: Build, Buy, Should Not Exist, or Decide
The four quadrants guide your decision making: build, buy, should not exist, or
decide. You should be able to map each piece of technology you operate or need to
acquire into one of the quadrants.

Build (upper right)
The “build” quadrant represents high business value and bespoke needs. This is
where your competitive advantages and organizational core competencies live. For
Honeycomb, one of these differentiators is Retriever, our custom-built columnar
storage engine (discussed in Chapter 13). These are the kinds of differentiators that
justify building custom solutions.

Buy (lower left)
The “buy” quadrant represents tech that has low business value and is a commodity.
This is where most infrastructure components live: service catalogs, identity manage‐
ment, logging infrastructure, and observability platforms. If it’s standard and doesn’t
differentiate you, buy it.

Should not exist (upper left)
The “should not exist” quadrant represents low business value combined with
bespoke implementation. These are things that are unique to your organization but
don’t actually help your business. They shouldn’t exist, but unfortunately they often
do. And when they do, they are often the worst kind of technical debt.
Here’s a real-life example of something an organization should not have built:
      [financial institution] had written its own threaded request handler module in Apache
      HTTPD 1.3.x. It was five times faster than the stock scheduler. It was tightly integrated
      with all the company’s processes and apps. Years later, Apache 2.0 came out and had
      a new module API and a threaded scheduler that was even faster, and the original
      developers were long since gone. They were stuck and actually disadvantaged with
      slower connections/sec than others. When support for HTTPD 1.3.x finally ended,
      they paid a consulting company over $10 million to fix their mess (but not before the
      whole thing turned into a regulatory compliance issue, because unsupported software
      versions can’t get security updates).

You are better off figuring out how to obliterate this software entirely. Find out how
to do the same sort of work with other open source or commercial systems.

Decide (lower right)
The “decide” quadrant represents “high” business value and is a commodity. These
are things that genuinely differentiate your velocity and product quality, but where
decent off-the-shelf options exist. This is where all of the hard and interesting conver‐

There is no one right outcome to be had here; you’ll have to convene the right
stakeholders and hammer this out between yourselves. Sometimes the decision may
come down to whether you have the right subject-matter expertise in house, or
how much of a competitive advantage you think is at stake. These discussions are
often heavily influenced by your organizational habits and ethos. Some shops are
commercial off the shelf (COTS) all the way. Others are heavily OSS-driven.
We caution you to avoid making these decisions by managerial fiat. These decisions
are heavily weighted toward the technical side of sociotechnical systems, and they
should be owned by your technical domain experts, ideally ones with skin in the
game. This is what staff+ engineers are there for. You will get better outcomes if they
have a say in the decisions that get made.

When you find yourself in the “decide” quadrant with a decision to be made, here are
the key questions to ask:
What precisely is the job to be done by this software?
   Develop an objective rubric that the organization can see and weigh in on. Start
   high level, with stories and scenarios, not with a laundry list of features or any
   vendor-specific implementations. We talk about this process more in the next
   chapter on vendor engineering.
How unique are our requirements?
   If you think “no one’s like us,” you’re probably wrong. Most companies are
   far more similar than they believe. Take your rubric and compare it to similar
   architectures and case studies from peer companies. How did they already solve
   this problem? What are the deltas for your use case that existing systems don’t
   solve for?
What’s the smallest version of this problem we can solve?
   Don’t build a massive custom platform if you can solve 80–90% of your needs
   with a commercial tool and build the remaining bits as extensions.
Do we have product management expertise to dedicate to this?
   Building internal tools requires product thinking, user research, iteration cycles,
   and roadmap management, as well as internal evangelism and marketing.
   Without that discipline, you’ll end up with an unloved tool that never sees
   adoption.
What’s our timeline?
   Can your business afford to wait months or years for a custom solution, or do
   you need to get started immediately?
Who will support it?
   Who will support it when the people who built it move on?
If you end up deciding to build your own because there were no solutions available
that fit the bill perfectly, but some were close, set a reminder for yourselves to check
back in a year.
One unscientific rule of thumb to keep in mind: the closer you get to laying bits down
on disk, the more conservative you should be about preferring battle-tested code.
User experience (UX) is on the other end of the spectrum; the closer you get to UX,
the more likely you are to end up writing custom code to handle your use case, and
the less paranoid you should be about it.
You need a really, really good reason to write a new database, operating system, or
filesystem format. But writing a new library on top of OTel to give your frontend
team intuitive, easy instrumentation that delivers end-to-end insights on app respon‐
siveness? Yeah, that sounds pretty normal.

Let’s apply our rubric to a few common “jobs to be done” in the observability arena.

Example 1: Custom SLO/error budget tracking
Jobs to be done: Engineers need to understand if their services are meeting reliability
targets and have budget left over for experimentation.
How unique are our requirements? Your service level objectives (SLOs) are probably:
latency percentiles, error rates, availability—same as everyone else. The deltas might
be: “We have unusual traffic patterns (batch jobs, retail cycles)” or “We need to tie
this to deployment gates.” Every vendor does this.4 You think you’re special because
you have 37 microservices? So does everyone.
Smallest version: Use vendor SLO features for 80% of services. Build custom tooling
only for the 3–5 services with genuinely weird requirements (maybe your machine
learning inference pipeline has different latency characteristics than HTTP services).
Do we have product management expertise? This is where internal SLO tools die.
Someone builds it, it’s hard to use, no one iterates on it, and everyone goes back to
their old dashboards.
Timeline: Your CFO wants reliability metrics next quarter. But you can’t wait a year
for your custom solution.
Who will support it? The senior engineer who built it just got promoted and doesn’t
want to deal with it anymore.
Verdict: Buy for 90% of use cases, and maybe build thin wrappers for genuinely
unique business logic.

Example 2: Custom instrumentation library wrapper
Jobs to be done: Frontend developers need to instrument their React apps without
becoming OTel experts. We want consistent patterns and our specific context auto‐
matically included.
How unique are our requirements? Your React app is…a React app. But the UX
requirement—making it dead simple for your team—is actually legitimate. The
generic OTel SDK requires too much ceremony for your developers’ workflow.

The pattern that emerges from these examples:
Infrastructure/storage
     This is almost always buy.
UX/workflow
   This is often build (small, focused tools, or thin layers and wrappers).
Intelligence/decision making (anything that will take you more than three months to

     Build only with strong project management commitment.
The “decide” quadrant is full of things where the technical capability is increasingly
commoditized, but your workflow, your business context, or your org structure
creates deltas. The question is whether those deltas justify a full rebuild instead of an
extension of vendor tools.

What About Open Source?
Open source and source-available code are options that don’t fit neatly into the previ‐
ous build-versus-buy matrix. It’s not quite “building”—you’re not creating something
from scratch. But it’s not quite “buying” either—you’re not paying a vendor to handle
the complexity for you. However, you may be purchasing systems integration work
to get it installed with the rest of your infrastructure or, if nothing else, service and
support to keep it up and running.
Open source is best understood as a third option that shifts where you pay costs.
Instead of paying upfront licensing or usage fees, you pay in:

  • Engineering time to evaluate, deploy, and integrate
  • Infrastructure costs to run the software
  • Ongoing maintenance and upgrade work
  • Lost opportunity costs of engineers maintaining infrastructure rather than build‐
    ing product features
  • Recruiting and retaining engineers with expertise in those specific open source
    tools

These four quadrants inherit similarities from the 2 × 2 matrix discussed in “A Simple

Create (upper right)
The build category is the same as the simple framework for evaluation: high business
value combined with bespoke solution.
This is software that has potentially significant intellectual property value. You may
decide to keep it as a trade secret, trademark it, or file for patents. It could become
the basis of your own COTS product or service offerings. More virtuous leaders
in the industry might even make it available as source available or OSS for other
organizations, thus bringing the cycle full circle.

Avoid (upper left)
The “avoid” category is also the same as the simple framework for evaluation: low
business value problems with bespoke solutions shouldn’t exist.

Adopt (lower left)
The “adopt” category represents low business value combined with commodity. This
is the realm of OSS and source-available licensed code, and where most infrastructure
components live: agents, SDKs, service catalogs, identity management, logging infra‐
structure, observability platforms. If it’s standard and doesn’t differentiate you—just
download it and adopt it. From a software developer perspective, the advantage is
that you can see the code in order to diagnose low-level problems and, in the case of
OSS, you can even contribute the fix.

Acquire (lower right)
The “acquire” category represents some business value above and beyond what can
be found in open source, and critical differentiation. This is the realm of COTS
software products and SaaS offerings with advanced features you are willing to pay
for. You can customize it for your environment, maybe write a few custom plug-ins
or connectors, but you can’t fundamentally change the codebase. In most cases, you
can’t even see the codebase, which might make it hard to diagnose and troubleshoot
the thorniest of problems.

Commission (middle right)
Straddling the border between “create” and “acquire” is the “commission” category:
the realm of custom engineering and work for hire. You do this when you need
software that isn’t available off the shelf, but it isn’t something you need to develop as
an internal core competency—or you simply don’t have time or internal resources. It’s
pure mercenary work for a job that needs to be done well and fast. You still have to
create the spec, but the hired gun does the job. Maybe it is just one of those custom
plug-ins or connectors for COTS software you acquired. Or maybe it’s something
decidedly more sophisticated, like customized dashboards, CI/CD workflows, or data
science algorithms and models. The advantage of commissioned software is that you
own the code after it’s written. Simultaneously, that’s also the big disadvantage.

This is far from a comprehensive accounting of all the ways you can acquire software.

scaling, changing, extending, refactoring, and monitoring that code. Which is as true
now as it has ever been.

The Real Costs of Building Your Own
Most companies today will end up buying services from one or more observability
vendors. We buy observability from a vendor for the same reason we buy DNS,
storage, networking, and email: because if it’s not your core differentiator, it probably
doesn’t make sense for you to expend valuable engineering cycles on building it
yourself. It’s probably a distraction from your business goals, and distractions can be
very expensive.

Opportunity costs are usually the biggest expense, because resources are finite. By
choosing to build an observability tool, what did you choose not to do with those
engineering cycles? What potential business value have you sacrificed?
Anything you can build in a year with a small team of engineers is unlikely to
compare favorably to a service with hundreds or thousands of engineers building
it. It’s not just a numbers game, either: observability companies are subject matter
experts—it’s what they do. Presumably it is not what you do.
To calculate opportunity costs, you must first calculate the financial cost of each
option, quantify the benefits received, and compare the two. Let’s start with an
example of calculating the costs of building your own.

The Hidden Costs of “Free” Software
You could choose to build your own observability stack using open source compo‐
nents that you then assemble yourself. For simplicity, we’ll assume this is the case.
(Writing the code yourself from scratch would cost at least an order of magnitude
more.) To illustrate actual operating costs, we’ll use a real example.
We spoke with an engineering team manager who was considering investing in a
commercial observability solution after having built an in-house ELK (Elasticsearch,
Logstash, and Kibana) stack. We’d given him a quote of about $80,000 per month to
provide observability for his organization.
He immediately balked at the sticker shock of a nearly $1 million annual solution and
pointed to the “free” ELK stack his team was running internally.
However, he wasn’t counting:

  • The cost of dedicated hardware to run the ELK cluster, at $80,000 per month
  • Three additional engineers recruited specifically to help run it at $250,000–
    $300,000 each per year
  • One-time recruiting costs at 15–25% of annual earnings, or about $75,000 per
    engineer
  • Organizational time spent hiring and training them to become effective

The seemingly free solution had been costing his company over $2 million each year—
more than twice the cost of the commercial option. But he’d been spending that in
ways that were less visible to the organization.
This pattern repeats constantly (see Chapter 27 for more analysis). Engineers are
expensive, and recruiting them is hard. It’s especially wasteful to devote their talent
to building bespoke infrastructure solutions in the name of saving money, when the
reality is often much further from the truth.

The Benefits of Building Your Own
In many organizations, the deciding factor comes down to justifying visible spending,
which is one of the worst ways to make a complex sociotechnical decision. But there
are real benefits to investing local engineering cycles into the domain:
Building creates in-house expertise.
    When you decide to build custom tooling, it becomes someone’s job to deeply
    understand your organizational needs and turn those into functional require‐
    ments. Over time, you develop institutional knowledge about both observability
    best practices and your specific systems’ characteristics.
Building enables deep customization.
    You can create integrations with your bespoke internal systems, enforce naming
    conventions that matter to your organization, and build workflows that match
    exactly how your teams operate. Rather than using generic prebuilt software
    designed for many use cases, you can optimize for your specific needs. (But note
    that this benefit can turn into a liability, if you aren’t careful; optimizations and
    customizations scale poorly compared to standard interfaces.)
Building teaches you the domain deeply.
    If you choose to build your own observability solution, you’ll inherently need
    to internalize and implement the concepts in this book—from the necessary


522   |   Chapter 29: Build Versus Buy (Versus Open Source)
     data granularity provided by events, to automating the core analysis loop, to
     optimizing your datastore to performantly return results against large quantities
     of unpredictable high-cardinality data. You’ll learn exactly where metrics serve
     your business and where they don’t by slamming headfirst into their limitations.
Building might be good for the bottom line.
    If you choose to build, it might be that what you’re building can save the
    company considerably over existing commercial offerings, especially if there are
    vendor lock-ins that you can’t otherwise escape. Yet remember to pay attention
    to the true cost of building as just outlined. Beyond cost savings, sometimes
    building software can actually become part of your company’s product or serv‐
    ices offering, actively bringing in revenue. Or it could be part of its intellectual
    property portfolio, such as being patented, if there is a chance to license it to
    others. However, be realistic as to the probabilities of your organization actually
    doing anything of commercial value with an internally built tool.
Building your own may be the only viable option.
    This may be the case if, for example, you want a cheap, basic monitoring service
    and none of the big vendors are building that.

The Risks of Building Your Own
The benefits of building don’t come without serious risks. The era of building infra‐
structure like infrastructure—and exposing the rough edges of that infrastructure to
software engineers—is over. Anything you build in-house for other engineers needs
to get built like a product.5
Product management expertise is rare.
    Does your observability team have a product manager (whether by title or
    function) whose job is to interview users, determine use cases, manage feature
    development, deliver a minimum viable product, gather feedback for iteration,
    and balance constraints against business needs? If not, you’re likely to build
    something that doesn’t meet user needs effectively. When that happens, it can
    damage senior management’s trust in engineering’s ability to make sound techni‐
    cal decisions.
     Product-led organizations will have in-house product expertise that can make
     custom development successful. But whether that product management expertise
     will be allocated away from core business objectives toward building internal
     tooling is another question entirely.

An unfortunately common pattern in enterprises is to devote development effort
toward building an initial version of internal tools and then move on to the next
project. Internal tools get abandoned once development stalls, adoption is low, and
the effort required to make them meet current requirements isn’t a high enough
priority compared to competing interests.

The Real Costs of Buying Software
On one hand, the costs of buying software tend to be more visible and upfront,
which is good. On the other hand, cost may or may not go up in tandem with
value. Recall that Gartner study we mentioned in Chapter 23, with a representative
customer whose observability costs have been going up 48% per year for the past 15
years straight. Are you getting 35,800% (358x) more value out of your tools now than
you were in 2010?

The Hidden Financial Costs of Commercial Software
Let’s start with the most obvious cost of buying software: the financial cost. In a
vendor relationship, this is the most tangible expense—you see it whenever your
vendor sends you a bill.
A reality of commercial software is that vendors must make money to survive, and
their pricing schemes factor in profit margins. You will pay a vendor more to use
their software than it costs them to build and operate it for you. That’s not unfair—it’s

524   |   Chapter 29: Build Versus Buy (Versus Open Source)
how businesses work. (If you are paying them less than it costs to run your workload,
you should be very concerned—about their survival as a business, and about whether
or not they care about retaining you as a happy customer.)
What is considerably less fair is obscuring the real costs, avoiding transparency, or
stacking so many pricing dimensions that you cannot reasonably predict what your
usage patterns will cost in the future.
A common antipattern to watch for is pricing strategies that obscure total cost
of ownership. These include per-seat, per-host, per-service, per-query, or per-any-
other-impossible-to-predict metering mechanism. Pay-as-you-go pricing may seem
approachable at first—until your usage explodes at a rate disproportionately more
expensive than the revenue it helps you generate.
Consider these questions as your organization grows:

  • Will your team size change as your startup becomes more successful? (Almost
    certainly yes.)
  • Will the number of hosts significantly grow in the future? (Likely yes, though
    instance resizing complicates projections.)
  • Will there be more services? (Possibly, especially if you decompose monoliths
    into microservices.)
  • What about the number of queries you run? (Almost certainly yes, if the tool is
    successfully adopted.)

When it comes to observability specifically, you should predict exponential growth
in data queries as a culture of observability spreads through your organization. The
recommended practice is to analyze observability data with increasing curiosity.
You should see query volume expand dramatically as more teams find value in
understanding system behavior.
Therefore, avoid observability tools with pricing schemes that penalize adoption and
curiosity. You want to ensure that as many people in your company as possible have
the ability to understand how your customers use your software, in as many ways as
possible. Observability is not just for engineers! Support, product, design, security,
marketing…the more people stay grounded in the truth of production, the better for
everyone.
As a consumer, demand that your vendors be transparent and help you calculate
hidden costs and forecast usage patterns. Ask them to walk you through detailed cost
estimates of both your starting point today and your likely usage patterns one to two
years in the future. Be wary of any vendor unwilling to provide exceptional detail
about how their pricing will change as your business grows.

The Hidden Nonfinancial Costs of Commercial Software
A secondary hidden cost of commercial solutions is time through vendor lock-in. Yes,
buying a ready-made solution lowers your time to value initially. But once you’ve
committed to a commercial solution, how much time and effort would be required to
migrate to a different solution?
The most labor-intensive part of observability adoption is instrumenting your appli‐
cations to emit telemetry data. Many vendors shortcut time to adoption by creating
proprietary agents or instrumentation libraries prebuilt to generate telemetry in
proprietary formats their tools expect. While those proprietary agents may be quicker
to start with, any time and effort devoted to making them work must be repeated
when evaluating alternative solutions.
The open source OTel project solves this problem by providing an open standard
that many observability tools support. OTel allows you to shortcut time to value
with proprietary tools by using OTel distributions (distros) that layer boilerplate
configurations on top of standard OTel libraries.
Your strategy to avoid vendor lock-in should be to use native OTel functionality by
default in your applications and rely on distros or exporters to handle vendor-specific
configuration. Should you wish to evaluate or migrate to a different tool, you can
replace configurations without rewriting all your instrumentation.

The Benefits of Buying Commercial Software
The benefits of buying software are mostly what you’d expect. In the next chapter, we
will talk in depth about how to build enduring, mutually beneficial partnerships with
software vendors, so we’ll keep this part brief:
The most obvious benefit is time to value.
    You don’t need to build a custom solution—you’re purchasing one that’s ready to
    go. In most cases, you can start within minutes or hours and quickly gain new
    understanding of your production systems.
Commercial solutions generally offer more streamlined user experiences than open
source alternatives.
    Open source tools are typically designed as building blocks for you to assemble
    yourself. Commercial software tends to be more opinionated—a finished product
    with specific workflows. Product designers work to streamline ease of use and
    maximize time to value.
You offload maintenance and support to your vendor.
    Rather than burning your own engineering cycles, you pay someone else to
    handle that burden. You benefit from economies of scale—your vendor operates
    the same infrastructure for hundreds or thousands of customers, driving down
    per-customer costs and improving reliability through shared learnings.
You potentially gain access to domain expertise.
    By purchasing tooling from a company whose core competency is solving
    observability challenges, you can tap into years of expertise addressing the
    problem in various ways. This commercial relationship can provide invaluable
    guidance that would otherwise take years to build internally.

The Risks of Buying Commercial Software
The benefits of buying commercial software don’t come without risk. Importantly,
some of the bigger risks are baked into the largest benefits:
By delegating responsibility to your vendor, you risk not developing your own in-house
observability expertise.
    In this way, that last benefit of buying commercial software is also one of the
    bigger risks. By simply consuming a ready-made solution, your organization may
    not dive deeply enough into the problem domain to understand how observabil‐
    ity applies to your particular business needs.

Commercial products are built for wide audiences.
   While they have opinionated designs, they’re also generally built to adapt to
   various use cases. By definition, commercial products are not specifically built to
   accommodate your special needs. Features critical to you may not be priorities
   for your vendor, leaving you waiting for functionality essential to your business.
You don’t control them.
    The vendor may get acquired, run out of money, decide to pursue a different
    target market, or have massive, days-long reliability outages of their own. (Then
    again, your company might too. Nothing is guaranteed, not even tomorrow.)
Fortunately, there’s a way to mitigate most of these risks and develop your own
in-house expertise while adopting commercial observability tools. Because this is
not the kind of all-or-nothing situation where you have to build everything or buy
everything. You can, and probably should, do some of both.

This Is Where Your Observability Team Comes In
Observability needs an owner, whether you buy from a vendor or not. It’s a massive

Buying a vendor solution doesn’t mean your company won’t need an observability

We know from industry research that the key to success when outsourcing work is to
carefully curate how that work makes its way back into the organization.6 This is what
your observability team is here to do.
Your observability team should:

  • Write libraries and useful abstractions.
  • Standardize naming schemes across the organization.
  • Handle updates and upgrades gracefully.
  • Consult with other engineering teams on effective instrumentation.
  • Manage vendor relationships.
  • Make decisions about adopting new technologies.
  • Educate teams about observability practices.
  • Deliver training and sample code.
  • Integrate telemetry data sources if your company still has three pillar tooling.
  • Look for ways to simplify and reduce friction and costs.

For organizations considering building their own observability stack for customiza‐
tion to specific needs, this approach is especially effective.

Get the Best of Both Worlds
Using an observability team to build on top of an extensible vendor solution minimi‐
zes sunken costs that distract from delivering core business value. Your team can
build integrations that mount the wheel to the fast-moving car you’ve already built,
rather than reinventing the wheel.

Important Caveats
Given the breadth of this topic, keep in mind that we are talking straight down
the middle of the industry. We are trying to address technology businesses that are
extremely price-sensitive as well as companies that are all but immune to cost.
If your company is an outlier in some exceptional way—maybe you’re a hyperscaler,
maybe you’d be fine with monitoring circa 2009 (if it came with a 2009 price tag)—
our advice may not apply to you. That’s fine. We trust you know your business better
than we do.
There are three key caveats to keep in mind, accordingly.
First, there is currently no large commercial offering for monitoring capabilities at

monitoring price points. There are some smaller shops that seem to offer this kind
of thing, but you’ll need to do your own independent research. If your needs are
simple—checking if services are up, down, or slow—and your architecture is straight‐
forward enough to reason about in your head, you may need to drive off the beaten
path a bit. The major observability vendors have largely abandoned this segment of
the market in favor of more complex (and expensive) use cases.
Second, the build-versus-buy quadrant may break down at hyperscale. If you’re with
Google, Meta, or another hyperscaler, the economics do shift. At that scale, it may
become cheaper to build your own solutions, even for commodity products. That’s
why Meta builds its own custom power grids.
We hear a lot of companies fret over whether any vendors can possibly handle
their scale. Let’s put it this way: if you aren’t building your own power grids, you’re
probably fine. (Even for hyperscalers, building their own products is usually driven
more by wanting control than scale.)

Conclusion
Observability is not like any other type of tool. It’s the software you use to understand
your own products: the code that makes them, the users that use them, and the
intersection of all three.
Ten to fifteen years ago, every observability SaaS option was basically a hosted version
of what you could run yourself. Vendors took open source monitoring/logging tools,
ran them as managed services, and charged for the convenience.
That’s no longer true. The products available commercially have meaningfully
diverged from what’s available via open source. For most companies (or at least
most of the companies with more money than time), the default position is to invest
in a commercial observability solution as a baseline, and focus engineering time on
building business-differentiating features. Build custom integrations and abstractions
on top of commercial tools where needed, but don’t rebuild the entire platform. For
most companies, observability falls into the lower-left quadrant: commodity software,
low business value.
The exceptions—companies that should seriously consider building—are those with:

  • Truly unique requirements that no commercial vendor addresses
  • Scale so massive that custom solutions become economically viable
  • Observability as their core business capability
  • Regulatory or security requirements that prevent a company from using external
    vendors, or even running other vendors’ code on their systems (if these exist)
