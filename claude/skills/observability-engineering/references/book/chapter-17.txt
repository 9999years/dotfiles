A shift toward a high degree of observability relies on high-context telemetry and
data-driven analysis. Often, teams quickly discover that “more visibility” also means
managing a lot more data. Once you’re capturing rich events across services, environ‐
ments, and teams, you run into a familiar problem from the broader data world:
semantic failures.
The same concept gets measured in multiple places, labeled in multiple ways,
or defined with slightly different rules. At first, it’s simply inconvenient. But left
unchecked, suddenly dashboards disagree, queries don’t line up, and nobody is quite
sure which version of the “truth” is actually the real truth. This is a well-known failure
mode in analytics and data engineering, and the observability domain is not immune.
This chapter introduces ontologies as a practical mechanism for establishing shared
understanding across development, operations, and business stakeholders. An ontol‐
ogy is a structured map of a domain: its core entities, the relationships between them,
and the rules that constrain valid behavior. In observability work, this map becomes
operational through semantic conventions—standardized names and schemas that
services emit consistently, so that telemetry encodes both system state and domain
meaning.
In this chapter, Frank Chen demonstrates how to define a minimal ontology and
how to reuse those definitions from development through production. It also extends

It’s 2 a.m. on a Saturday. Your pager fires. You open your laptop and stare at two
contradictory dashboards. The infrastructure dashboard is all green: “99.9% uptime,”
“latency < 200 ms,” “HTTP 200 OK.” According to the platform metrics, the system is
perfect.
But the business dashboard is screaming. Customers are reporting bills that contra‐
dict the laws of physics—showing curtailed solar exports instead of crediting them.
The AI agent you shipped last week to optimize grid interactions has started halluci‐
nating price windows that don’t exist.
Nothing is “broken” in code. The servers are up. The API is responding. The AI
is answering prompts. The failure is invisible to your standard observability tools
because it isn’t a failure of infrastructure; it’s a failure of semantics.
In complex systems—especially those combining rigid rules with nondeterministic
AI—data is everywhere, but truth is scarce. “Availability” can mean one thing to a
developer (the server is up), another to an SRE (the latency is low), and something
totally different to the business (the bill is accurate). I’m going to demonstrate how to
fix that silent failure using ontologies.
Before exploring how to design a minimal, practical ontology for a nondeterministic
AI system, let’s first take a look at how ontologies fit into observability.

Ontologies and Their Role in Observability
Ontologies are formal, explicit specifications of shared concepts and their relation‐
ships within a domain. They act as both a common vocabulary and a knowledge map
for humans and machines to forge a shared understanding of data and its meaning.
For clarity, I’m not talking about knowledge graphs used by academics. I’m talking
about defining a map of your domain—the core objects, relationships, and rules—
and using it as a layer in your organization and, in particular, your observability data.
This ontology is how organizations can guide their sociotechnical systems.
The goal of ontologies is to avoid fragmented definitions. Terms like “reliability”
create contradictory signals that sow divisions and chaos between your teams, as
shown in Figure 17-1.

Ontologies and semantic conventions are the missing layer in observability. For
observability work, an ontology turns scattered metrics and logs into named, linked
concepts that are codified in your team’s semantic conventions, runbooks, and docu‐
mentation. By doing that, terms like “uptime,” “user,” “availability,” and “incident” will
mean the same thing across multiple teams and tools. The practical payoffs are fewer
alerts, faster diagnoses, and signals that match from development to production.
When using ontologies and semantic conventions, you gain the ability to catch errors
that traditional monitoring misses, and you gain a powerful way to place guardrails
around nondeterministic AI.
An ontology remains an academic exercise until it is codified. In the next section,
we’ll look at how to design a minimal ontology.

Design the Ontology
In observability, we implement our ontology through semantic conventions (standard‐
ized names, structures, and schemas that every service in your system agrees to use).
Think of these semantic conventions as a translation layer for your organization. For
business stakeholders, they provide a clear vocabulary of “cost” and “usage” that maps
directly to customer value. For engineers, they ground abstract technical metrics, like

latency or throughput, into actual business processes. And for the system itself, they
serve as rigid schemas that turn ambiguous data into verifiable truth.

To build a reliable system, we need to start by mapping our “nouns” (entities) and
our “invariants” (rules). For our running scenario, consider an energy management
system designed to help customers understand and optimize their utility costs. The
system’s primary job is to ingest raw usage data and accurately price a bill based
on complex, shifting utility rates. To add value, the system also uses an AI agent to
generate billing proposals and optimization plans—such as suggesting when to shift
appliance usage to save money.

Define the Core Entities (the Nouns)
To ensure this system remains accurate and safe, we can reduce its complex world to
a minimal set of fundamental objects and define their relationships in a map:
IntervalSeries (the input)
      This consists of a timestamped series of raw usage data.
Tariff (the logic)
      This is the deterministic ruleset (time-of-use windows, rates) effective for a
      specific period.
BillSet (the output)
      The calculated result is produced by applying a Tariff to an IntervalSeries.
ActionPlan (the AI proposal)
      This is a tentative object unique to AI systems that represents the system’s struc‐
      tured interpretation of the user’s intent. It bridges the gap between vague natural
      language (“save me money”) and specific system operations. The ActionPlan is
      untrusted until validated.
Once we have these objects defined, we can define the invariants.

Define the Invariants (the Rules)
Invariants are the mathematical truths that must always hold. If an invariant is
violated, the system is broken, even if no exceptions are thrown. The core rules are as
follows:



314   |   Chapter 17: Ontologies as a Shared Language for Humans and AI
Coverage (completeness)
   Every timestamp in the IntervalSeries must map to exactly one pricing win‐
   dow in the Tariff. No gaps, no overlaps in pricing_coverage.
Arithmetic (integrity)
    The total_cost in the BillSet must equal to the sum of its individual compo‐
    nents (within a floating-point tolerance of ±$0.01). The “whole” must strictly
    equal the sum of its parts.
Determinism (reproducibility)
    Given the same series_id and tariff_id, the system must produce the same
    determinism_hash (BillSet) result, every single time. A rerun of yesterday’s
    data must yield yesterday’s result.
In a traditional system, you might treat these rules as simple unit tests. But when you
introduce nondeterministic AI, invariants become your primary line of defense. You
can’t write a unit test for every possible hallucination that an LLM might generate.
You can’t predict if the AI will suggest a tariff that doesn’t exist or a time-shift that
defies the laws of physics.
Instead of testing the input (which is infinite), you use invariants to constrain the
output. By treating these invariants as runtime assertions, you create a semantic
firewall. When the AI generates an ActionPlan, we simulate the result and check it
against the invariants. If the simulation creates a gap in coverage or an arithmetic
error, we know—with mathematical certainty—that the AI’s plan is flawed, and we
reject it. This enables you to deploy creative, nondeterministic agents into production
while maintaining safety guarantees of a deterministic system.

Once you have the core entities, you can define the invariants.

Glue the Schema Through Metadata
To implement this, you must establish semantic conventions that ensure every data
point has clear provenance. For example, when you look at a BillSet, you shouldn’t
just see a dollar amount; you should see exactly which input and which logic version
produced it. Provenance enables you to understand how, what, and why this thing
exists in your database. Metadata is the glue that lets you see what created a BillSet.
The following is an example of the simplified schema for our deterministic core,
focusing on how we link inputs to outputs:
      // IntervalSeries (the Input)
      {
        "series_id": "uuid-home-1234",
      ...
        "lineage": {"source": "smart_meter_api", "fetched": "yyyy-mm-dd"}
      }
      // Tariff(the Logic)
      {
        "tariff_id": "uuid-tariff-55",

    ...
      "rules": {"type": "time_of_use", "windows": [{ ... }]}
      "ruleset_version": "2025.12.1"
    }
    // BillSet (the Output)
    {
      "billset_id": "uuid-bill-100",
      "total_cost": 150.76,
    ...
      "lineage": {
                   "input_series_id": "uuid-home-1234",
                   "applied_tariff_id": "uuid-tariff-55",
                   "calculation_engine_version":"2025.5.1"
                 }
    }
When building your schema, ensure that you:

  • Map every output back to its specific input series_id.
  • Version your logic (e.g., ruleset_version) so results are reproducible.
  • Embed lineage directly into the metadata to automate troubleshooting.

By explicitly linking the input_series_id and applied_tariff_id within the Bill
Set metadata, you transform a simple cost figure into a traceable audit log. This
structure allows your observability tools to automatically correlate spikes in cost with
specific changes in logic or input data.
By establishing these foundational schemas, we prepare the system to handle “parsed
intent”—transforming vague AI suggestions into the structured snapshots required
for the deterministic validation that we will explore in the next section.

Schematize Intent with the ActionPlan
If your system uses AI to process or generate actions, you must also define a schema
for intent. While the objects discussed previously capture what actually happened, the
introduction of AI adds a new requirement: capturing what might happen and why.
We cannot trust an LLM to “fix the bill” directly. Instead, we force the model to fill
out an ActionPlan schema.
The ActionPlan schema acts as a constraint. It forces the model to convert “vibes” or
conversational nuance into discrete, measurable fields that the deterministic system
can validate. It is a parsed intent: a structured snapshot of what the AI thinks the user
wants, as shown in this schema definition:
    // ActionPlan (the Parsed Intent)
    {
      "plan_id": "uuid-plan-99",
      "status": "PROPOSED",

        "predicted": {"bill_delta_usd": -94.50},
        "actions": [
           {"device": "ev_charging", “time_shift”: {"start": "22:00", "end": "06:00"}},
        ],
      ...
        "lineage": {...}
      }

Notice that with provenance, you can reason about how the input (IntervalSeries)
quantifies the BillSet. With the ActionPlan, you can reason about why the AI
suggested a change before you ever allow it to execute.
With the nouns, invariants, and now the ActionPlan defined, you have established
a bare minimum ontology. This core contract provides the foundation you need
to begin enhancing your system with automated validation, CI/CD pipelines, and
shared instrumentation.

Validate the Contract: Continuous Integration
Evaluation of AI-driven actions and intent should be boring and repeatable. If you
are building a system that relies on AI agents, you cannot rely on “vibes” or manual
testing. You need a rigorous evaluation pipeline that treats your ontology as a con‐
tract.
This is one place where observability “shifts left.” In a traditional workflow, we often
think of a vulnerability as something that happens after deployment. But with a
strong ontology, we can observe the system’s behavior before it ever touches produc‐
tion data. By using immutable fixtures (e.g., a standard input IntervalSeries and
Tariff for the “golden path” and “edge cases”), we can assert that our invariants hold
true under every code change.

Layer Three Gates for Defense
Instead of scattered lists of tests, we organize our CI gates into three distinct layers of
defense:
Deterministic layer
    The first layer checks the rigid physics of the system. We verify determinism:
    given the same IntervalSeries and Tariff, the system must produce the
    exact same BillSet hash. If the hash changes, the code has drifted. We verify
    arithmetic integrity, ensuring that the total_cost strictly equals the sum of its
    components. Finally, we verify coverage, ensuring that 100% of the input data
    maps to valid logic windows without gaps or overlaps. These are binary pass/fail
    checks. If any of these fail, the build is broken.




318   | Chapter 17: Ontologies as a Shared Language for Humans and AI
Simulation layer
   Once the math is solid, we check the logic. For an AI agent proposing an Action
   Plan, we run a simulation. We take the agent’s proposed changes, feed them into
   our deterministic engine, and verify the prediction. Did the agent claim this plan
   would save $20? The simulation must confirm that delta within a tight tolerance.
   If the AI hallucinates savings that the physics engine cannot reproduce, the gate
   closes.
Semantic layer
   The final layer evaluates the quality of the communication. This is where we
   use golden dialogs (predefined ideal interactions) to score the AI’s explanations.
   For example, we check for a confidence floor in which the system rejects any pro‐
   posal where the model’s internal confidence score drops below a safe threshold
   (e.g., 0.6).

Establish the Team Workflow
When a gate fails, the ontology tells you where to look. If a structural gate fails, the
engineer fixes the schema. If a coverage gate fails, they inspect the logic mapping. If
a semantic gate fails, they tune the prompt or the retrieval context. By treating these
failures as observable events—emitting the same JSON structure in CI as we do in
production—we ensure that “fixing the build” is indistinguishable from “fixing the
system.”

Create Signal Parity in Production:
Continuous Deployment
The ultimate payoff of an ontology with semantic conventions is signal parity within
a continuous deployment (CD) pipeline that goes to production. In most organiza‐
tions, the code that tests the system in CI is completely different from the queries
that monitor the system in production. This disconnect means that a bug caught by a
“unit test” feels completely different from the same bug triggering an alert.
We close this gap by ensuring that our operational dashboards hang directly off the
ontology’s objects. We emit the exact same structured payload in production that we
generated in our CI pipeline. This means that the logical assertion preventing a pull
request from merging is the exact same query that triggers an incident in production.

Understand the Hierarchy of Signals
Instead of generic “error rates,” our semantic conventions allow us to monitor specific
categories of failure that map to business impact.
First, we monitor structural integrity. We track pricing coverage to ensure no inter‐
vals were missed, and we validate tariff effective dates to guarantee the ruleset
matches the time period. A drop in coverage is not just a “data error”; it is a violation
of the system’s contract.
Second, we monitor logic stability. We look for bill anomalies by comparing current
outputs against historical baselines or “twin” simulations. We also track ranking
drift—if the system suddenly recommends a different tariff for the same usage pat‐
tern, the logic has become unstable.
Finally, we monitor AI reliability. Because our AI workflow emits standardized
ActionPlans, we can track the proposal acceptance rate and the evaluation pass rate
in real time. If the model’s fuzzy-match score against golden dialogs drops, or if users
start rejecting proposals at a higher rate, we know the model has drifted, even if the
infrastructure is healthy.

Create Shared Instrumentation: The Universal Payload
To make signal parity real, we define a set of shared instrumentation fields used in
every workflow, whether it’s a deterministic batch job or a dynamic AI interaction.
Every log line emitted by the system must carry the context required to reconstruct
the decision.
This payload begins with identity and versioning. We log the home_id and series_id
to bind the data to the user, but we also strictly identify the tariff_id and rule
set_version. This enables us to answer the question “Did the bill change because the
usage changed, or because we deployed a new rule?”
Next, we include the invariant results. The payload explicitly states the pricing_cov
erage score and the determinism_hash. If a calculation fails, the log doesn’t just say
error; it identifies exactly which invariant was violated.
For AI-driven workflows, we might enrich additional context for models and
prompts in our observability workflows. We want to capture the model_name (e.g.,
GPT-5) and the prompt_version. By standardizing these fields, a “low acceptance
rate” dashboard in production can immediately drill down to see if the issue is
isolated to a specific prompt version or model variant.
By codifying these shared instrumentation fields, we move beyond theoretical map‐
ping and into active system defense. This universal payload ensures that every
component—whether deterministic code or a generative model—speaks the same

language. With this semantic foundation in place, we can now examine the “AI
sandwich architecture” where these definitions serve as the critical interface between

Implement the AI Sandwich Architecture
To operationalize the minimal ontology we have defined (comprising our nouns
like the BillSet and our invariants like arithmetic integrity), we use the AI sand‐
wich architecture. This architectural pattern sandwiches a nondeterministic AI layer
between two layers of deterministic, rule-based logic. This ensures that the dynamic,
AI-driven process remains grounded in the bedrock of truth provided by the deter‐
ministic workflow.
To guarantee reliability, we must strictly separate the systems’ physics from its
imagination. By decoupling the “physics” (our immutable business rules) from the
“imagination” (the probabilistic nature of LLMs), we prevent nondeterministic AI
hallucinations from corrupting the core integrity of our financial data. Figure 17-3
illustrates the decoupling of these two worlds.

bility. The AI workflow (bottom) must pass through its proposed actions against a

We divide the application into two distinct loops, each with its specific responsibili‐
ties and observability needs:
Deterministic workflow
    The deterministic workflow is the boring, safe part of the system. It takes an
    input (IntervalSeries) and applies a set of rigid rules (Tariff) to produce an
    output (BillSet). It is mathematically pure; if you run it 10 times, you get the
    exact same hash 10 times. We use this workflow for everything that requires legal


                                                    Implement the AI Sandwich Architecture   |   321
      precision: pricing a bill, comparing historical costs, or generating the “ground
      truth” for a simulation. Here, observability focuses on invariant enforcement. We
      monitor coverage, arithmetic integrity, and hash stability. If this layer fails, the
      code is broken.
Dynamic AI workflow
   The dynamic AI workflow is the creative part of the system. The AI analyzes
   the user’s context and synthesizes an ActionPlan. We treat this object as “intent
   capture”—a snapshot of what the AI thinks the user wants, frozen in a format
   we can simulate. Because LLMs are nondeterministic, this workflow is prone to
   hallucination. The AI might propose charging an electric vehicle (EV) during a
   time window that doesn’t exist, or it might optimize for a tariff the user isn’t on.
If we treat rules as guardrails, we never execute the AI’s proposal directly. Instead, we
feed the ActionPlan into the deterministic workflow to validate it. We treat the AI’s
output as a hypothesis and the rules-based engine as the test. We can ask:

  • Does the proposed plan result in a valid BillSet?
  • Does the simulation confirm the predicted savings?
  • Does the plan violate any hard constraints (like 100% coverage)?

If the deterministic layer says “no,” then the AI’s proposal is rejected before the
user ever sees it. In our observability data, we record this as a “proposal rejection.”
This metric—the rate at which the guardrails reject the proposed plan—becomes our
highest-fidelity signal for AI reliability.
Instead of executing unchecked proposals, the rules-based engine acts as a continu‐
ous validation layer, rejecting any output that violates our core invariants before
it ever reaches the user, and sending a signal back to restart the loop. In our
observability data, this proposal-rejection rate becomes our highest-fidelity signal
for monitoring AI reliability in production.

Close the Loop: Production Driving Tests
In the AI sandwich architecture, production is not just where code runs; it’s where
test cases are born. We “close the loop” by taking the telemetry and edge cases
captured in production and feeding them directly back into our development and
testing cycles. When the AI creates a proposal that the rule engine rejects, that is a
learning opportunity. We don’t just log the error; we capture the specific inputs (the
prompt, the IntervalSeries, and the Tariff) that confused the model. We scrub
this data of personally identifiable information and promote it to a golden dialog
in our CI environment. This creates a continuous feedback loop where real-world
failures automatically become the next generation of automated tests.

By treating production traces as future test fixtures, we create a data flywheel. If the
model fails to understand a specific edge case—like a leap-year tariff change—we
capture that artifact, add it to the eval suite, and block future deployments until
the model passes that specific scenario. We track the “eval pass rate” as an SLI. A
sudden drop in this rate is treated exactly like a latency spike: a reliability incident
that requires immediate remediation.

Putting Ontologies into Practice
To visualize how this architecture functions in a live environment, let’s walk through
two specific scenarios using our example ontology.

Scenario A: The deterministic comparison
Consider a user who wants to know if they should switch pricing plans. The system
begins by binding the user’s historical IntervalSeries and fetching three candidate
Tariffs. It then calls the deterministic engine to generate three distinct BillSets.
Before returning any data, the system performs an invariant check, asserting that
every single interval in the history maps successfully to a specific price window. Once
validated, the system ranks the BillSets by total_cost.
From an observability perspective, the logs emit a payload containing the determin
ism_hash and the ruleset_version. This creates a perfect audit trail; if the user later
calls support to ask why the numbers changed, an engineer can retrieve and replay
the exact tariff version used in that specific calculation.

Scenario B: The AI proposal
Now, consider a user asking an AI agent to “save me money.” The model generates
an ActionPlan suggesting a shift of EV charging to 10 p.m. Instead of executing this
immediately, the system enters a simulation phase. It creates a synthetic IntervalSer
ies (a counterfactual copy of the data modified by the proposed actions) and runs
this counterfactual through the deterministic workflow to calculate the new cost.
The system then performs a verification step, comparing the deterministic engine’s
calculated savings against the AI’s predicted savings. If the AI predicts a $20
saving, but the engine calculates only $0.50, the system flags the proposal as a
hallucination and discards it. The observability layer records this event with a spe‐
cific structured value like: proposal_status=rejected and rejection_reason=simu
lation_mismatch. This ensures that bad advice is caught by the system, not the
customer.

Conclusion
We started this chapter at 2 a.m. with a pager barking and dashboards lying. The root
cause was disjoint language. Dashboards disagree, alerts duplicate, and the on-call
engineers burn out because teams don’t share the same nouns and verbs. Ontologies
and semantic conventions are not just documentation; they help shape your observa‐
bility strategy. These objects and invariants help transform unknown-unknowns into
clear, actionable signals.
This dimensional thinking matters for software teams working with nondeterministic
AI as part of their workflows. You should model input and output as untrusted,
understand its respective components, validate workflows when possible with rules-
based invariants, and use these invariants consistently.
Ontologies with semantic conventions won’t make outages or observability challenges
disappear, but they transform that 2 a.m. pager bark from a confusing hunt for
disjointed data into a directed search. By following the advice in this chapter, you
ensure that the next time you’re woken up, you have the shared vocabulary needed to
identify exactly which invariant was violated and fix the problem before the alert even
hits your phone.
