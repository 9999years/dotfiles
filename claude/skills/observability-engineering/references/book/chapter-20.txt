Observability is often framed as a tool for reliability—keeping the lights on and fixing
what breaks. However, a mature observability practice also serves as the foundation
for performance engineering: the disciplined pursuit of making systems faster, more
efficient, and more cost-effective. In this chapter, we explore how to move from
reactive firefighting to proactive optimization by leveraging the overlap between these
two fields.

The Case for Performance Engineering
Imagine you had a mystery bug that was silently adding latency to every request in
your services. That was the situation our team found ourselves in, and the catalyst for
our performance engineering journey.
In December 2021, the Honeycomb engineering team was surprised to find a discrep‐
ancy between the request latency for our ingest endpoint as measured by the load
balancer logs and the auto-instrumentation spans in our application traces. The effect
was most pronounced for POST requests to our ingest service endpoint that ingested
single wide events rather than larger batches. Somehow, time was going missing, and
requests that would take tenths of a millisecond to complete on the server side were
showing up in the load balancer as single-digit milliseconds.
Larger requests to our batch endpoint also showed the same discrepancy of single-
digit millisecond variation between the data sources, but the difference was less
noticeable as a proportion of the request time because batches vary in size and can
take tens to hundreds of milliseconds. Outside the visibility of our instrumentation
for traditional observability, something was slowing down every request.

Building a Performance Engineering Practice
Performance engineering addresses two critical business needs: lowering costs to aid
the bottom line, and speeding up customer experiences. While it certainly can be a
fun academic exercise to hyperoptimize a specific function, real impact comes only
from moving the needle on tangible metrics. For any given experiment, you should
first gather baseline data on performance and cost in advance of running the test.
Are you working on the right part of your codebase to have the largest possible
impact? And can you quantify any future improvement you might make—whether
to latency, data size, dollars, or effective tons of carbon dioxide emission? Thinking
more broadly about a performance engineering function, dedicated headcount only
may make sense if given the correct mandate and with appropriate scope.
In many cases, where the cloud bill is small, investment in performance engineering
may simply not feel worth it relative to the salaries of the engineers required to
optimize versus build new features or maintain reliability. Similar to considerations
on payoff of time spent automating versus how much time the automation will
save, we would caution developers to avoid premature optimization where the salary
and opportunity cost outweighs the cost or customer performance gain. However,
failing to implement any guardrails can lead to waste, excessive expenditure, or
customer-facing latency creeping up—and collectively, we are wasting a significant
amount of resources and potentially worsening the climate crisis. Therefore, knowing
how to employ performance engineering is still valuable, even if you decide that now
is not yet the time to invest in doing it.
For us, the investment was worth it because of our relatively high cloud bill relative
to our headcount—and the fact that we operate a software as a service (SaaS) startup,
where gross margins and other engineering-influenced business metrics directly
affect our viability as a company. Having intentionally built a culture of observability-
driven development meant that our developers embraced performance engineering
relatively quickly.
Like observability-driven development, performance engineering requires identifying
the most impactful possible changes, adding instrumentation, making the change,
and then inspecting the results in production. The team already had a sense of
curiosity and ownership, but was stymied by the ad hoc nature of the siloed tooling
that we had employed in our initial forays into performance engineering. We needed

Optimizing Cost Without Modifying Application Code
Getting performance engineering onto the engineering roadmap for teams can be
challenging. Developers prefer the experience of receiving performance and cost
gains automatically with no additional effort on their part to change code. Fortu‐
nately, there are a few options for optimizing on the backend without requiring
developers of each application to take manual action. In this section, you’ll learn
about three techniques for hands-off optimization.

Infrastructure Purchasing Models
One of the most important factors impacting the overall cost of your infrastructure is
the type of compute platform you use. Even before choosing specific instance types,
you must first choose a product based on its developer ergonomics and economic
model. We don’t mean choosing between an m4.large versus an r5.xlarge (although
we’ll discuss that topic later in this chapter). There are six models for obtaining
compute capacity:

  • Capital expenditure (building your own datacenter (Oxide Computer Company)
  • Reserved capacity (AWS Savings Plans, Azure savings plans, Google commit‐
    ted/sustained use discounts)
  • Persistent, defined capacity (Amazon EC2, Azure virtual machines [VMs],
    Google Compute Engine)
  • Interruptible, defined capacity (Amazon EC2 Spot, Azure Spot VMs, Google
    Preemptible VMs)
  • Persistent, elastic capacity (AWS Fargate, Azure Container Apps, Google Cloud
    Run)
  • Interruptible and flexible, for short requests (AWS Lambda, Azure Functions,
    Google Cloud Functions)

Each of these methods of procuring infrastructure has benefits and drawbacks in
terms of money, customer experience, and the developer time required to care for
them. With a function as a service (FaaS) approach like Lambda or Azure Functions,
you achieve perfect utilization—only paying when your software is actively running.
You need not worry about the software system–level dependencies or underlying
hardware. As a trade-off, you pay a higher unit cost for each CPU-second (or CPU-
millisecond, if your provider supports granular billing), because the cloud provider is

                                         Optimizing Cost Without Modifying Application Code   |   373
assuming the risk of capacity management and is unable to sell all of their capacity
pool. And some requests may take longer to serve if the cloud provider needs to
allocate and boot up new capacity for your workload.
Running entire virtual machines (VMs) instead of using serverless means you can
keep your processes active, persist data to a cache, or maintain connections to depen‐
dencies, making requests return a lot faster. However, you’re paying for that server
time while you’re not using it, and you need to make sure you can scale up within
time if you get more requests than you expected. And if you’re using self-hosted bare
metal rather than VMs, your CPU-hour costs will be much lower, but you’ll have to
make the capital outlay for the hardware and assume responsibility for provisioning
and availability.
Then there are the models lying somewhat between—for instance, purchasing
reserved capacity, or using a blended serverless compute engine that abstracts away
the underlying VMs but prices for the CPU consumed by each task rather than the
duration of each request. When using VM-based infrastructure, you can also achieve
significant performance and cost optimizations by using a container orchestration
approach, such as Kubernetes, to pack multiple applications into a single VM. How‐
ever, the conversation about containerization is partially orthogonal to the compute
model: serverless functions can operate with raw binaries, supplying their own OS
and container, and it’s possible to run Kubernetes, Red Hat OpenShift, Amazon
Elastic Container Service, or another container orchestrator on top of any type of
underlying compute—ranging from bare metal to Fargate.
Which should you choose? Well, that’s dependent on how your system scales, and
also how your consumers use that application. Ultimately, you’re going to want a mix
of different capabilities to make it work optimally. For instance, you may want to
run your database servers in the VM model, managed through Amazon Relational
Database Service or Google Cloud SQL. Your user-facing frontends might run via
serverless, and you might assign batch or streaming worker pools via Fargate or
Cloud Run. The choice of platform is not a fixed, one-time decision. Over the longer
term, optimizing the cost of compute requires using observability data to your load
profile and deciding when to change even if there is some operational work required
when migrating between compute models. And the options are not all or nothing; a
hybrid approach may make sense.
If you have a very spiky workload, you may still want to try Lambda as a compute
layer, but this could prove to be very costly (or result in persistent cold starts and
refused connections) if you get a spike that’s unexpectedly large. If, however, the
time of executing Lambda directly correlates to your revenue, this may be fine.
Observability for the serverless case is relatively straightforward, as the duration of
trace spans straightforwardly converts to the total price—in fact, you can calculate

additive measures such as “request cost” and graph those dimensions directly in your
tool of choice!
Observability plays a key role in gathering the data needed for deciding when it’s
time to migrate onto an additional compute platform or retire an existing one. Con‐
current execution counts—derived from either provider-exported metrics or from the
number of concurrent trace spans—enable you to determine how much serverless
concurrency to provision with your cloud provider. Utilization metrics enable you to
determine when a sustained workload could be shifted toward a more server-ful or
sustained-use model. Just be mindful of the human cost of managing and operating
toward the physical end of wrangling real servers.

Fleet-Wide Optimization
Once you have nailed down what compute model(s) you are using, the next step
is making that compute cost as little as possible. There are three broad techniques
to try: squeezing, optimizing, and migrating architectures. An architecture switch is
a complex effort with correspondingly huge potential payoffs, but there are many
smaller such experiments and projects we undertake on an ongoing basis at Honey‐
comb to optimize our costs and improve the performance our customers see.
Assuming you have set SLOs (see Chapters 10 and 12) and have a healthy culture
of chaos engineering, the first step is to see how few machines you actually need
to still achieve those SLOs. Reduce task counts, reduce CPU and memory alloca‐
tions per task, and set autoscaling targets to higher target utilization—with the
ultimate goal of shutting down VMs. In the past, standard practice was to run Intel
architecture–based instances with hyperthreading or simultaneous multithreading
(SMT) at 40% utilization, and Arm instances (or any instance with SMT disabled) at
70–80%. However, that recommendation may no longer hold for the kinds of cores
you are using. Test until latency actually begins to degrade. Your observability data
will come in handy, especially given that degradation may impact a small tail of
complex but crucial requests rather than the overall system.
Always test against the most recent generation of hardware that your vendor offers.
As this book has and will continue to go several years between revisions, any specific
recommendations we might make would rapidly become out of date. After taking
away instances and shrinking underutilized instances, checking the appropriate ratios
of CPU to memory to storage may help you save money. Most cloud providers
charge a premium for higher-RAM configurations, as well as charging for use of
their managed durable block storage products (e.g., Amazon Elastic Block Store). For
I/O-bound workloads, you may benefit from using NVMe drives directly attached to
instances (e.g., AWS -d suffix and i- prefix instances, or Azure L-series instances)
instead of durable block storage for data volumes.

Another potential avenue to make large-scale performance and cost gains is via
recompiling your binaries to run on a more efficient CPU architecture designed
for the cloud. We’ve previously written on the Honeycomb blog about our switch
to the Arm64 architecture using AWS Graviton instances. Microsoft offers Cobalt
Arm instances, and Google offers Axion Arm instances. Each generation of Arm
processors appears to show 30% price-performance gains per generation, outstrip‐
ping the rate of price-performance gains of the x86 architecture. All of these are
worthwhile competitors to their equivalent-generation Intel and AMD competition,
but do require you to potentially reconfigure or recompile your workloads.
Today, there are many guides for carrying out an Arm64 migration, but the basics are
to switch over container or OS base images to the corresponding architecture, then
test that your container or binary build pipeline still works using the new architecture
and new base images. You’ll then need to test deploying the new architecture, sending
it representative traffic, and then load testing to determine how it performs relative to
your original configuration. Remember, because most Intel/AMD instances use SMT
and Arm does not, you may find that it is safer to run your Arm64 instances more
saturated since you will not be contending against your own workload. Always test
to breakage/saturation to see the true performance and packing gains and not the
superficial results!

can help you make the most efficient choices about your underlying architecture
and sizing. Kubernetes also enables you to make more efficient use of machines
by packing multiple workloads onto the same hosts, ensuring that a misshapen
workload consuming a high amount of CPU but low RAM can be paired with one
that consumes little CPU but maintains a lot of state in RAM, better utilizing the
capacity without stranding any.

The Kubernetes metrics that will be your biggest friends are the pod CPU utilization,
pod memory requests, and limit usage values. That usage is the genuine demand that
cannot be eliminated through clever bin-packing and scaling tricks. So, at the end of
the day, to progress any performance engineering efforts will require reducing that
demand. However, you should be wary of potential pitfalls with observability tooling
when examining usage by pod within a given deployment. The intended surge in
pods during the rollout will inflate the usage for a wider time window, as will the
appearance of pods that terminated just after the start of the time window, and pods
that started just before the end of the time window. Thus, a service might appear to
have up to double the number of pods that it normally has during an aggregation
window, causing the sums of CPU consumed by all pods to appear artificially inflated.
To avoid this kind of artifact, plotting each configuration template hash separately
within the service avoids the apparent duplication of monitored resources.

In addition to seeing which services are consuming the most actual resources, you

Besides the resources we intend to run in a cluster, we need to understand how

you cannot fit two 20-core pods on the same machine, and DaemonSets should take

Cost Optimizing Serverless
For serverless, the underlying compute platform takes on the responsibility for man‐
aging utilization and reducing wasted resources. The primary cost driver is the
duration that your functions execute for, but there are subtleties lurking in the weeds.
For instance, startup time and cold starts result in billing, as does the amount of
CPU/RAM that you assign to each function, and the cost of the API calls required to
invoke your function and that the function itself makes.
Your cloud provider’s metrics may be a good start for understanding the amount of
time taken by each function. For instance, Amazon CloudWatch offers metrics for
the number of concurrent executions, invocations, and execution duration. Similarly
to earlier sections about not using the resources that you don’t need, taking away
excess memory and CPU will save you money—to a point. That point comes when
increased execution time or instability such as OOMing makes further reductions no
longer worthwhile. Lambda Power Tuner can be a powerful tool for minimizing the
cost function that is a product of CPU and execution duration, by executing your
function many times with different sizing parameters.
Cold starts—where your application is not yet running at the time that your provider
needs an instance of your function to serve a request—don’t only incur a latency
penalty to the end user. Cloud providers have pushed the cost of initialization work
done during a cold start onto the client, meaning that you are billed for the time it
takes to become ready to serve. Such work might include setting up fresh connections
to dependencies, loading datasets into memory, or more. To measure and lower this
cost, consider implementing observability into the runtime cycle of your serverless
applications. You’ll need to implement OpenTelemetry instrumentation into your
main function, create a span at the start of the main function, and end it just after
setup has finished but before allowing the serverless runtime code to take over
execution. Then, for each invocation, create a “running” span for the duration of
execution, and persist a “sleeping” span started just before the function is frozen to be
sent once the function is thawed.

Be aware that the costs of getting data to your serverless functions may be more sig‐
nificant than you expect. For instance, the cost of S3 read requests is approximately
25% of the total cost of running the serverless functions that power the Honeycomb
Retriever storage engine (see Chapter 13). Reducing the costs of compute can only
contribute so much to reducing the total cost of ownership as a result.
While provisioned concurrency may appear attractive, it essentially trades money
for cold-start latency and gives you the worst of all worlds in terms of cost. You
have to handle manual capacity planning, you don’t receive a meaningful discount
in exchange for committing to usage, and you’re still unable to amortize setup costs
across longer application lifecycles, as you would with a persistent application.

Observing the Costs
Making your workload execute faster will almost always be the most effective way to
lower your compute costs. Whether that’s optimizing a serverless function’s execution
time or your API hosted in a container, no savings actually manifest until you
consume less of whatever the billing dimension is. The question is, how do you safely
reduce resources and ensure that you’re not adversely affecting the user experience?
There are multiple dimensions to the cost of your compute layer. It’s not simply
“time,” because the different machines and instance sizes also have an impact on
price—otherwise, you could just throw more compute resources at your workload to
make it “faster”! So, you’ll want to identify what dimensions to optimize.
However, consider also whether giving a workload more memory or faster network
might actually make the execution happen faster too, and avoid otherwise idling or
wasting resources. Observability can help find these bottlenecks by showing which
steps are the slowest. Conversely, it may be that a majority of your workload cost
is not the compute but is instead some ancillary dimension such as networking or
storage.
Ingesting your cloud bill into your observability tooling can help identify opportuni‐
ties for improvement, focusing on the greatest sources of spend first, and drilling
down into what common tags or attributes those share in common. Tagging also
ensures accurate chargeback or other attribution of cost to use cases and potentially
to individual tenants in an SaaS model. The rows in the cost and usage report
translate well into wide events; the cost in dollars is one field, the service is another,
the usage type is another, and so on and so forth. Each tag on a resource becomes a
column, and each value of the tag becomes the value’s field. Aggregations work the
usual way via performing summing operations grouped by resource, tag, and so on.




                                           Optimizing Cost Without Modifying Application Code   |   381
Observability tooling also contributes to the cost of running a workload, but can be a
valuable source of data for investigations. For thoughts on how to improve your ratio
of value to cost, see Chapters 15, 26, 27, and 29 for build-versus-buy and cost levers
that you can use.
In addition to focusing on the highest consumption tags and services, it can be
valuable to look for quick wins that require no code change. For instance, if you have
a consistently high utilization of raw compute VMs, your service provider may have
savings plans or committed use discounts available. Having the data available to see
what the minimum hourly spend is will enable you to rightsize a commit and ensure
that it will be effective over the contract term.
Likewise, if you are spending a lot of money on load balancers, network address
translation (NAT) gateways, or data transfer between availability zones, consider
reconfiguring your topology. Keeping data transfers within the same availability
zone—going directly from node to node—avoids unnecessary costs for shuffling
bytes around. In some cases, the cost of the network can outstrip even the cost of the
compute or the storage!

How Application Observability Reduces Cost
Having insight into the performance of your workload enables you to gain both
direct and indirect benefits. When you improve performance, you can frequently
remove resources to get the same performance as before, or reduce waste that was
caused by the additional usage. However, as you’ve learned in the previous section,
it’s important not to waste time on the smaller or less vital components of your
application. Only focus attention on optimizing the most resource-intensive or most
user-visible parts of your application.
In order to identify where you’re spending the most time for your most important
requests, a first step is to use distributed tracing (as we’ve discussed in the rest of
the book), and wrap major functionality in spans. By visualizing the concurrency
of spans and the sum of their duration, you can get a quick and dirty idea of what
the critical path is and what you might want to optimize. Anything that makes a
call to another service or performs a computationally expensive operation on data is
worth wrapping in a span. The good news is that your existing observability should
cover these cases well through automatic instrumentation of remote procedure calls
(RPCs), plus manual instrumentation to fine-tune coverage for internal spans within
your services. Always aim to account for where your application is spending its time
to the nearest tenth of a second (hundred milliseconds). Long trace spans with no

Due to the overhead of instrumentation, there is a limitation on how much detail
you can obtain per span. If you were to wrap every function in a span, you would
slow down your application and do just as much instrumentation work as real work!
Instead, you need to consider how to aggregate smaller units of work into larger units
of work that measure in tens of milliseconds or more, rather than in microseconds or
single-digit milliseconds. For that finer degree of detail, you’ll need to use profiling at
the individual function level. We’ll discuss this approach in the next section.

Using CPU Profiling Tools
How, exactly, did we get the insight into where our code was slow in the original
example at the start of the chapter? pprof is the name of the specific tooling that the
Go programming language offers, but the generic technique is called profiling. Profil‐
ing allows for determining how much time programs are spending in each function,
line of code, and even assembly instruction. The key insight behind profiling is that
we can interrupt a program at any point with OS signals, and retrieve the previously
running program counter (the assembly instruction currently being executed) and
the stack frames above it. A lightweight interrupt handler can record the stack info
to a ring buffer, and we can periodically read the ring buffer to populate maps with
counts of how often a given program counter was observed, and with what parents.
Given a binary with appropriate symbol tables, we can symbolize the program
counter to lines of code and function names. Sampling the call stack every millisec‐
ond might be overkill and introduce too much overhead, but sampling every 10
milliseconds might give a picture of which assembly instructions and lines of code
are most likely to crop up. Over time, a statistical picture of a program approximates
reality—similar to sampling of distributed traces, a single sample might not be rep‐
resentative, but a thousand or a million recorded datapoints give a pretty accurate
picture of the percentage of time spent in each function or assembly instruction.
We’re big fans of distributed tracing, but it’s not the best tool for every job. Profiling
enables us to get granular detail, instead of having to create a span for each and every
function call within a service, let alone each line of code.

Setting up and interpreting profiling data
Profiling Go binaries as a one-off is remarkably straightforward, thanks to features
of the Go runtime. There’s a built-in HTTP handler that you can enable on your
application—but remember to set the HTTP server listening on a different port that
is not internet visible. Then, curl the endpoint while the behavior you’re looking for
is manifest, save to $profile, wait a minute, and then you’ll have a file that you can
feed to go tool pprof $profile. We recommend running with -http :8080 for
a friendlier web user interface; you’ll also want to supply the original source code
directories for line-level detail, and the original binary corresponding to the running

server in order for assembly-level detail. You can also use -profilecpu with go test
-bench to write profiles for your canned benchmarks.
In other languages, you’ll need to consult runtime documentation—for instance, Java
uses Java Flight Recorder (JFR). In the worst case, you might need to use the perf
tools bundled with your Linux kernel, or use an eBPF collector agent. Regardless of
how you collect them, what should you do with the results of profiles?
Profiling tooling typically defaults to either a listing of source code with statistically
estimated numbers of milliseconds attached, or a directed acyclic graph (DAG) ren‐
dering—with boxes representing functions and their total time, lines representing
calls between functions, and line weights representing seconds spent on each path
in the call tree. Both of these can be somewhat obtuse to work with, so instead we
recommend a different approach.
If you took the advice to run go tool pprof with its web frontend, you can open up
the visualization menu and choose a flame graph instead. Flame graphs are a more
intuitive way to work with the data. Width represents the total amount of time spent
in a function and its children, stacking one function beneath another represents a
child function call, and individual icicles or flames represent collections of full stack
traces sharing the same functions in order in the stack frames.
This enables visualization for glanceability of three things that are not obvious when
looking at raw source dumps or the full DAG:

 1. What the most expensive part of the code is
 2. If it’s the code or the libraries being called
 3. How much is attributable to one function itself versus its children

The best targets to optimize are those functions that are called often with medium-
sized running time (e.g., the library function that is called from 10 different places
and constitutes a significant chunk of the running time of each callsite), or those
that are often found close to the root of the flame with little time spent in their
children. This has parallels to how you might reason about breaking down traces
and optimizing the single largest blocking request preventing completion of a traced
request—except instead of each bar representing a specific request or subsection of a
request, each bar represents the aggregated sum of time spent making calls to a given
function, even if many different shorter calls were made.

Scaling out profile collection and analysis
While it’s handy to catch something misbehaving in staging (or in a unit test or
benchmark), problems rarely manifest in the real world while we’re looking. And by
the time we’re alerted, they may have already disappeared. We need our systems to

give us the telemetry continuously for us to interrogate later, so that we don’t need to
catch them in the act. This is the whole reason why we have observability, right?
Fortunately, you can either run profiling continuously with agents that hook into the
runtime, or continuously scrape the profiling HTTP endpoints on your applications.
The data volume is manageable because it’s highly redundant—while each minute-
long profile segment might be five megabytes (MB) in size, two hours of data is not
going to be 120x as expensive. The same function calls and even lines of assembly
repeat often, and the data compresses nicely.
This is the value proposition behind continuous profiling tools such as Grafana
Pyroscope, Polar Signals, and zymtrace. They handle the lift of both the agent and
of the collection and visualization pipeline so that you can get answers when you
need them. That way, you won’t be trying to remember which debug endpoint to
curl, or how to run the pprof debugging command at 2 a.m. We’re believers in
running less software at Honeycomb, and this is one wheel we were glad to not
have to reinvent. With OpenTelemetry offering a common profiling agent, SDKs, and
encoding of profiling data, the vendor-neutral future of the profiling observability
signal has never looked brighter.

Using the Correct Observability Signals, Together
Engineers tend to prematurely optimize: we will jump into optimizing SQL queries or
look for loops that could be trimmed with early exits. However, without knowing that
these are actually the biggest impact on your code, it’s essentially educated guesswork.
For seasoned engineers, those who have spent time in that ecosystem or that domain,
this can actually be very effective. But how do we give all engineers this capability?
To some degree, profiling requires you to already know you are interested in a very
specific portion of your system. Therefore, it’s a tool that is best used in conjunction
with a tool like tracing. Using tracing—both from an analytics perspective and from a
waterfall-view perspective—enables you to validate hypotheses quickly.

When to use each signal type
In our opinion, tracing gives you a bird’s eye view and enables you to navigate the
system as a whole. High-cardinality data analysis enabled by the unified observability
model equips you to explore individual fields and their values to understand outliers
and weird behavior. Analyzing hundreds of millions of traces can give you astonish‐
ing insight into what real customers are experiencing in the system. But sometimes
you really need to grab the microscope to look at the rings (lines) of the tree (code)
rather than look at the forest of microservices as a whole. Profiling is that tool.

First, you might begin an investigation by asking:

  • Is this endpoint normally slow?
  • Is this database query slow only when it’s called for this API endpoint?
  • When this endpoint is slow, is it always this database call causing it?

Armed with this information, you can establish before, and after, your change
whether you’re targeting the right area of the system to make the biggest impact.
Having a scientific or engineering mindset involves employing the scientific method
to hypothesize testable changes and then performing the experiments required to
validate—not just throwing spaghetti at the wall. Sometimes, your change will be
visible in the profiles from processes at large, while having only a small change at
the trace level. Other times, it’ll be the reverse: much faster traces, but with the
percentage of time spent in each function remaining unchanged.

Blending performance engineering and observability
What if you didn’t have to make the trade-off between request-level but outer-
function-level granularity for tracing, and inner-function-level but only process-wide
granularity for profiling? When profiles are indexed at the level of individual seconds
of data, or are tied to individual trace spans, magic happens. You can understand
what was slow, for whom, why, and how, all at the same time instead of guessing.
It’s premature optimization to write the very most efficient, inscrutable code every‐
where. But having the visibility post facto to know what’s actually being called in a
tight loop and needs to be ratcheted down enables you to optimize just in the right
places, with an explanation as to why you’re doing it. We admit to feeling skeptical
about the “day 2+” value of profiling. Wouldn’t we just squish the “obvious” bugs and
then stop finding value? Well, that’s not true. You’re always adding new services or
changing them, and creating new bugs along the way.
The golden example of this discovery for us at Honeycomb was finding that we
were serializing a very complex query—containing nested derived columns—from
protobuf to JSON on behalf of a user (see Figure 20-4). We were also then invoking
Lambda hundreds of thousands of times, doing the serialization each time for each
invoked Lambda—on a single Retriever merge node that was already running very
hot from coordinating other queries. While our Lambda functions are massively
parallel at mapping across millions of files on S3, the nodes dispatching Lambda
invocations have finite resources.

wrong and how to fix it.

Conclusion
Like the practice of observability, performance engineering is not a one-and-done
activity. Instead, it is a scale with diminishing returns toward the very top end, and
large gains at the beginning. And it must be continuously reinforced. Incremental
0.5% or 2% improvements lead to compounding benefits over time, even if they are
not as flashy as a 30% performance optimization on a fresh, unprofiled codebase. The
core loop is: measure to find potential improvement, do the improvement, measure
the effects, and repeat.
