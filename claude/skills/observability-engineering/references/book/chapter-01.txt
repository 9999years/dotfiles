                                          What Is Observability?




The topic of observability has generated a steady supply of attention and buzz over
the past 10 years, ever since it was first introduced to the field of software develop‐
ment. However, as with most complex topics, a surging tide of practitioner adoption
and interest in observability was swiftly countered by a wave of oversimplification
and appropriation, fueled by hype and marketing budgets.
When you see this cycle play out repeatedly, it is easy to be cynical: maybe words
just don’t mean things when money gets involved. It’s worth taking a step back to
consider: why did we all get so excited in the first place? What was so revelatory, and
why did it matter?
This chapter examines the mathematical origins of the term “observability,” and how
the software development industry has interpreted it and adapted it to our world.
We also look at the tectonic plates that are colliding at almost every company right
now, between the old ways (which evolved out of server ops and still dominate the
ecosystem) and the new ways—derived from first principles and far more expansive
in capabilities, yet swimming upstream against the way things have always been done.
Along the way, we’ll argue that observability isn’t just a debugging technique or
a category of tooling; it’s a property of software dependability, as fundamental as
reliability or availability.
Observability as a concept was born out of necessity, when traditional tools and
techniques simply could not keep up with the long tail of novel problems coming out
of our increasingly complex systems.
Our best guess is that AI will be the thing that forces teams to finally move away from
the operator model of telemetry and toward a more unified model, the way business
data did in the 2000s. Observability is the last line of defense for AI-generated code,
and agentic behavior is inherently unpredictable and changes over time. The only way

production.
Then again, we thought the same thing 10 years ago. We thought there was no way
the three pillars model could survive the complexity of microservices, serverless, and
polyglot persistence. But somehow, it did. We are engineers, not prognosticators.
We have a lot to pack into this chapter. Let’s get started.

The Origins of Observability
The term “observability” was coined by engineer Rudolf E. Kálmán in 1960, in a
paper that we now cite as the birth of modern control theory—the mathematics
of systems you want to steer toward a desired state despite noise, disturbance, and
incomplete information.1
Kálmán’s key insight was that observability and controllability are duals. A system
you cannot observe is, in a precise mathematical sense, a system you cannot control,
and vice versa.
For Kálmán, observability meant asking the question: given the outputs you can
measure, can you infer the complete internal state of the system? If yes, the system is
observable. If no, some internal state is permanently hidden, and no amount of clever
engineering will recover it, because the information was never captured to begin with.
Software borrowed this from systems engineering. Like most things we borrow, it
arrived with some of the original meaning intact and some parts lost in transit. (The
controllability part has no equivalent in software.)
But when we apply the principles of observability to the squishier domain of soft‐
ware, it opens up radical new ways of interacting with and understanding the code
you write.

Applying Observability to Software Systems
Software engineering has a long, rich history of borrowing from other domains:
biology, architecture, and every type of engineering. We can do this in software
because we are less tethered to physical constraints. The farther up the stack you
go, the more you are unconstrained by practicalities like gravity, mass, or the speed
of light. The only structure we get is the structure we give ourselves, using logic,
language, and metaphor.

Software may not be grounded in any of the constraints of its constituent parts—
atoms, electrons, fiber optics, and so on—but software has its own constraints and
considerations to worry about. And the principles behind Kálmán’s definition of
observability have proven useful for reasoning about modern software systems.
Our systems and practices have changed continually over the years. Our understand‐
ing of observability has changed as well.
In the first edition, we described observability as a new breed of tooling, defined
by the capabilities you could unlock by rethinking data storage and analysis. This
definition ruffled many feathers—and understandably so, since it implied that more
traditional tools afforded no observability whatsoever. We came to disfavor it for a
different reason: it emphasized features and capabilities, but what people actually care
about are outcomes.
We have come around to a more encompassing view since then, which is that
observability is a property of software systems, like reliability or performance. We will
argue that the observability of your software is a core attribute of its dependability.
“Dependability” is another borrowed concept (from systems engineering, this time)
we now rely on. Software dependability is typically expressed on a spectrum ranging
from low to high, as are each of its attributes.
Let’s start by examining the other properties of software dependability. Then, we’ll
look at how to measure the property of observability.

Properties of Software Dependability
Software has eaten the world. Every time we call a friend, take the train, or get paid,
we rely on a massive invisible web of software behaving more or less as expected,
despite the evergreen fact that all hardware will break and all software has bugs.
Failures are a fact of life. The software industry may have made only modest gains
at preventing bugs, but we’ve done a lot to make our systems more resilient to them.
As technology plays an even greater role in our lives, our tolerance for unpredicta‐
bility has decreased. This makes dependability—“the trustworthiness of a computer
system”—a central concern of our era.
Software dependability cannot be quantified numerically. There’s no such thing as
a dependability score of 9 or a grade B+ for dependability. Some components, like
availability, often get measured with nines—e.g., uptime that is 99.9%, 99.99%, or
99.999% available. You can measure seconds of availability, but that metric tells you
nothing about dependability. Nines don’t matter if users aren’t happy.

Luckily, there’s a well-established path for measuring dependability characteristics.
In 1995, Jean-Claude Laprie proposed a framework for measuring software dependa‐
bility by evaluating six complementary properties that together describe a system’s
overall trustworthiness:2
Availability
    The ability of the system to deliver services when requested
Reliability
    The ability of the system to deliver services as specified
Maintainability
   The ability of the system to undergo repairs and evolution
Safety
    The absence of catastrophic consequences
Confidentiality
   The absence of unauthorized information disclosure
Integrity
     The absence of improper system alterations
To assess dependability, you have to look at three things together: what the system
is supposed to do (functions), what it actually does (behavior), and how it’s built
(structure). This applies across both hardware and software, including the environ‐
ment they operate in—and, crucially, the people who interact with the system. Laprie
defined software systems as inherently sociotechnical.
That last point is important and easy to overlook. You can’t fully analyze dependabil‐
ity by staring at code. The humans in the system are part of the system.

Observability Is a Property of Dependable Software
In the mid-1990s, Laprie’s framework was comprehensive enough to collectively
describe software dependability, just like monitoring was enough to meet the reliabil‐
ity needs for systems of the era.
Monolithic systems were relatively predictable. The problems you’d encounter in
production were mostly binary: the site was either up, or it was down. (Sometimes, if
you were unlucky, it had a third state: inexplicably slow.) Monitoring was well-suited
to this. It detected whether a service was up, down, or underperforming, and engi‐
neering teams responded with break-fix work to restore availability.

In the modern cloud era, many infrastructure failure modes are abstracted away
and handled by the platform. Monoliths gave way to microservices. The industry-
wide push to improve resiliency made everything more distributed, bringing many
second-order effects. The most significant of these was a radical change in how we
think about availability.
The cloud era ushered in a sprawling zoo of partial failure states. The previously
rare third state—inexplicable slowness with no smoking gun—became the most com‐
mon failure mode: a partially degraded load balancer; a hardcoded config pointing
to us-east-1 from containers in India; a dependency running on another team’s
infrastructure, behind an API, where you can’t even see what it’s doing. Sounds like a
Monday.
Simple monitoring systems can tell you that your system is mostly up or down. More
sophisticated monitoring can tell you where something is slow. But monitoring can’t
tell you why. You need all three to resolve issues in production.
Software dependability now has to include how quickly faults are detected, how
quickly their sources can be identified, and how effectively teams can reason about
why those faults are occurring, even when they are quite small. In other words, you
need to be able to do this at any level: from a high-level view of the entire system,
down to each network hop between services in a given session, and any subset or
combination of requests in between.
This is why we believe it’s time to add a seventh property to Laprie’s list:
Observability
   The ability to understand or debug any given system state
This isn’t a minor amendment. We’ll spend the rest of this book unpacking why it
belongs there and how to achieve it.

How Observable Is Your Software?
Properties of dependability, like reliability, security, and maintainability, aren’t meas‐
ured numerically; neither is observability. But you can reason about it qualitatively,
and that’s a useful place to start.
Fault detection is relatively straightforward: do you know an issue is occurring before
your customers report it? If you’ve managed a modern cloud application, you know
how common it is for monitoring to miss critical issues. Your customer support team
flags a spike in complaints, so you check the dashboard but everything looks fine—if
that sounds familiar, you have a fault detection problem.




                                                           How Observable Is Your Software?   |   7
Triage—figuring out where an issue is occurring—gets harder as architectural com‐
plexity increases. In low-observability systems, this often manifests as alert storms:
a failure in one underlying system triggers dozens or hundreds of alerts in others.
When a critical dependency fails, do you know exactly where to look? Or do you have
to check multiple sources and sleuth your way to the source of the cascade?
Understanding why an issue is occurring is usually the hardest part. Once you’ve
found the source, you have to figure out what caused it. Identifying the causal trail
can be very challenging, but it’s the meta process where teams struggle most: figuring
out how they figured it out, and encoding the trail of breadcrumbs into the system
instead of living exclusively in the heads of senior engineers and manifesting as
intuitive leaps.
As Scotty from Star Trek once said: “I could tell you the speed that we were traveling
by the feel of the deck plates.” Good for you, Scotty, but that doesn’t help the rest of
the crew. Can we get the current warp speed displayed where others can see, please?
Can any engineer on your team, regardless of experience, quickly diagnose complex
issues by interrogating the data your system emits? Or does a correct diagnosis
require deep institutional knowledge and a sixth sense?
The answer to that question is how observable your software is. The changes that AI
is bringing to the industry will make this exponentially more relevant.


                                             Cardinality
    In mathematics, cardinality refers to the number of elements in a set. In databases,
    it means the number of unique values in a column. A column called Status that
    contains “true” or “false” has a cardinality of 2, while a universally unique identifier
    (UUID) or any other unique set of values has the highest possible cardinality.
    High-cardinality data is the most descriptive and identifying type of data, and it is
    vital for observability. Think of all the most useful attributes you would like to search
    or group by: build number, commit hash, container ID, span ID, user ID, app name,
    query hash, etc. All of which are high cardinality, and that is why they’re useful.
    High-cardinality values can be downsampled (e.g., bucketing last names by prefix,
    or replacing all HTTP error codes with “5xx”), but this cannot be undone. If you
    aggregate the data before writing it out to disk, you can never put Humpty back
    together again.
    Cardinality is a never-ending nuisance for teams that rely heavily on metrics. High-
    cardinality data is the most valuable data, but metrics aren’t built to handle it. A
    single metric can increase your bill by tens of thousands of dollars per week if enough
    cardinality slips into the data unnoticed.

In a software system with a high degree of observability, you should be able to:

  • Understand any system state that your application may have gotten itself into,
    even completely novel ones, without shipping new code to handle it (which
    would imply you needed to know about it to find it).
  • Compare one set of feature flags against any other set, then break down and
    group by endpoint, API key, query hash, or any other combination of interesting
    dimensions, with no limits on cardinality.
  • See how users are interacting with the feature you just shipped. Trace by transac‐
    tion request, browser session, mobile app session, or chat history.
  • If any of your service level objectives (SLOs) are burning hot, you should be
    able to select the violating events and surface any patterns. (“The spike in slow
    requests is coming from read replicas in Australia, from a build ID we just
    deployed to 1% of canaries using flags x, y, and z; the client error string says DNS
    is timing out.”)
  • Find outliers. Any kind. Easily.

To answer these questions, you need precision, flexibility, and (ideally) speed.
Software systems with lower degrees of observability are ones where these questions
can only be answered with an enormous investment of time and manual labor, or by
an expert who can practically intuit the answers.

Two Competing Models for Telemetry:
Three Pillars Versus Unified Data
Any tool can be evaluated by how well it helps you achieve a high degree of observ‐
ability. The principles are what matter—and the principles behind the two most
common approaches are very different.
These two tectonic plates are on a collision course with each other right now, all over
the industry, as the immovable rock of “how we’ve always done things” meets the
unstoppable force of AI and competitive pressures. Will engineering teams upgrade
their tool kit, or will the three pillars adapt and prove impossible to dislodge? We’ll
find out.

The Three Pillars Model
The first plate is the old way. We call it the “three pillars”—metrics, logs, and
traces—but that’s a polite fiction. There are far more than three types of signals:
errors, exceptions, profiling data, product analytics, security events, etc. Each type of
signal gets streamed off and stored in its own special silo, as shown in Figure 1-1.

Figure 1-1. How the three pillars model stores telemetry.

Each type of signal is shipped off and stored in its own separate silo. Metrics go to a
time-series database (TSDB), logs go to a log-aggregation service, and traces go to a
tracing service. If the code generates exceptions, errors, profiling data, or any other
signals, each would be stored in its own silo.
To illustrate the point, consider a simple web checkout request, like this:
     GET /api/checkout trace_id=7f3a9c user_id=u_8821 200 OK 142ms
It would generate at least three distinct sets of telemetry data: metrics, logs, and a
trace, as shown in Figures 1-2, 1-3, and 1-4, respectively.

Figure 1-4. The trace answers that question instantly. You can see that db.query_start
consumed 89 milliseconds of the 142 milliseconds, and most of that was in db.execute.
You can see where most of the time went (into db.execute). Because that shares a

Most of the software engineers in this industry have never used anything but the
three pillars approach and its silos. Why? Because infrastructure and operations have
always owned the tooling, and the three pillars suit their needs.
If you work in infrastructure, you likely operate hundreds or thousands of compo‐
nents that you did not write and cannot change. Third-party code is going to emit
what it wants to emit; you get little say. You just have to put it somewhere and make
the best of it.
That’s infrastructure, and it generates a roaring stream of logs and metrics at all times.
The obvious solution is still the right solution: store the metrics in a metrics store,
and store the logs in a logging store. And preferably cheap—these signals have some
value, but not much.
That’s the infrastructure problem. The development problem is different.
For the code that your team actually writes—the services that define your company,
your competitive differentiators, your product—you have complete control over what
gets emitted, how it’s structured, and where it goes.
You are not stuck taking whatever the code emits and making the best of it. You can
instrument deliberately. You can make good decisions about what context to preserve,
which relationships to maintain, and how to organize your telemetry to preserve
optionality for your future self.
And it unlocks tremendous value if you do so.

For the most part, teams haven’t been doing that. They’ve been handed ops tools
and told to make do—applying an infrastructure model designed for code you don’t
control to the code that matters most. Metrics and logs made sense for the router.
They’re a significant mismatch for the billing service.
This matters because if the true cost of software is measured by the cost of maintain‐
ing it, telemetry is probably the most powerful leverage point you have. Telemetry
is your code talking back to you from production, telling you what’s happening.
The decisions you make about how to emit, store, and analyze that data are not
infrastructure decisions but product decisions. Most teams have been making them by
default, not by design.
The first tectonic plate, in short, is a set of tools built for operators and inherited
by engineers. But it has accumulated enough ecosystem gravity that it remains the
default—so deeply embedded in team structures, vendor relationships, and the ergo‐
nomics of muscle memory that most organizations never question it.

These principles are worth naming explicitly, because they represent a genuinely
different set of assumptions about what telemetry is for:
Data is made valuable by context, and that value increases combinatorially, not linearly.
   Each additional piece of context you append doesn’t give you one more thing
   to query; it makes the entire dataset exponentially more valuable4 by giving you
   new combinations with every field that already exists. The relationships between
   fields are the most powerful part of the data. Those relationships should be kept
   as intact as possible, not fragmented across silos. The technical term for this is
   “high dimensionality.”
Details are the most identifying part of any dataset.
    High cardinality—user IDs, trace IDs, build IDs, session IDs, any field that can
    take millions of distinct values—is a superpower, not a problem to be engineered
    away because metrics systems can’t handle it. The things that make each request
    unique are precisely the things that let you find it later.
Pre-aggregation is a one-way trip.
    If you aggregate your telemetry at write time, you can never un-aggregate it.
    The bucket is permanent. If you store raw events or traces, you can derive any
    aggregate on the fly—including ones you haven’t thought to define yet. The cost
    of pre-aggregation is the permanent destruction of future investigative options.
You don’t know what you don’t know.
    Preserve optionality for your future self by storing trace data or wide, structured
    log events (also called “canonical logs”) in raw form, so you can aggregate at
    query time. Any schemas, indexes, or aggregates at write time create dead ends.
    A telemetry system that forces you to define your questions in advance is one
    where you will only ever find what you expected to look for.
Static dashboards have limited value.
     What you need is flexibility and explorability: the ability to slice and dice, zoom
     from aggregate to individual request and back, and follow a trail of breadcrumbs
     wherever it leads. An investigation should feel like being in conversation with
     your data, but this requires preserving the relationships between datapoints. A
     static dashboard is a stock phrase; a dynamic interface is a conversation.
Fast feedback loops are everything.
    Queries that take minutes break your train of thought. If you can’t iterate
    rapidly—asking a question, getting an answer in seconds, asking the next—then
    you’re not exploring; you’re filing tickets.

                                     Dimensionality
  “Dimensionality” refers to the number of dimensions, features, or independent
  attributes a dataset possesses. It is a measure of the complexity and size of the data in
  mathematics and machine learning.
  In the context of observability, dimensionality refers to the number of unique fields
  in your structured log events or traces. High-dimensionality datasets are critical for
  observability (and for AI) because it is a way of measuring the richness of your
  context.
  If a structured log has four dimensions or attributes, you can combine them in 15
  possible ways. The math escalates quickly:
  4 fields
        6 pairwise combos, 15 possible combinations
  8 fields
        28 pairwise combos, 255 possible combinations
  30 fields
       435 pairwise combos, 1.1 billion possible combinations
  This is why the three pillars model is so costly. When you scatter signals across siloed
  tools by type, you don’t just lose some of the value; you lose most of it.

Observability Is the Validation of Developer Intent
For the past decade, the argument for observability has mostly centered on developer
productivity and incident response. Both real, both valuable. And both, it turns out,
are vastly understated as framings for what’s actually at stake.
The entire software development lifecycle (SDLC) is being radically upended at this
very moment. When agents can write, test, review, and ship code faster than people
can, verifying that code becomes the bottleneck.
Telemetry is what’s left. It’s the last line of defense, the safety mechanism for the whole
collapsed and compressed lifecycle.
The field of software engineering has a long and storied tradition of treating produc‐
tion systems like a foreign land, one they may travel to when called on to fix a gnarly
bug, but not their preferred locale. If a bug makes it to production, as goes this line of
thought, a developer must have failed in some way.
This has always been wrong. The line between bug and behavior is not always clear.
What code will do in the chaos of production is not always knowable. By introducing
nondeterminism as a feature, not an emergent property, AI shatters these fictions for
good.
Only production is production. Test in prod, or live a lie.

The Agentic Incursion Has Just Begun
What’s coming soon for most (and already here for many) are agents running in pro‐
duction. Not just writing code, but also testing it, deploying it, running it, observing
it, and acting on what they find. (See Chapter 10 for more on the topic of agents and
observability.)
Agentic investigations are not deterministic. They change over time as they learn new
things. Agents put the lie to the idea that preproduction testing can ever be sufficient.
The only way to learn what an agent will do is by instrumenting it and observing what
it does in the real world.
All the wisdom and connective tissue that senior engineers carry around in their
heads—check latency by build ID after a risky deploy, watch cache behavior for days
after a schema migration, look for N+1 query patterns when billing jobs slow down—
needs to be encoded into the system, or it doesn’t exist.


16   |   Chapter 1: What Is Observability?
Agents running in production can only be as good as the data they have access
to. Agents need context, relationships, and a diversity of sources. They need some
ontological grounding via semantic conventions, and data with enough precision
detail to track subtle changes over time. Most of all, they need us to think critically
about the intuitive leaps we make and convert them into breadcrumbs of context,
cardinality, and other clues.
High degrees of observability gives agents a fast, exploratory feedback loop with
real, precise signals. Is this build ID showing elevated latency on this endpoint? Is
this feature flag variant producing different error rates than the control? Is this user
cohort experiencing something the rest aren’t? The three pillars require intuitive leaps
to move between pillars. Unified, high-cardinality, and context-rich telemetry do not
require such leaps.

Guardrails Are Having a Moment
This is an exciting time to be in software. There’s a necessary and important conver‐
sation happening right now about which guardrails you need to test in production in
order to validate developer intent safely and dial it up incrementally.
Feature flags, progressive delivery, canary deployments, SLOs, characterization tests,
automated rollbacks, etc.—these techniques are not new. They’ve been available for
years (more on this in Chapter 2). Everybody agrees it would be great to have
them, yet most organizations do not have them. Implementing these tools has been
time-consuming and hard, putting them well out of reach for the median engineering
organization.
AI changes this calculation in two directions at once. It is an enabler—it makes many
of these things easier, including auto-instrumentation, anomaly detection, and the
kind of systematic validation that used to require dedicated platform teams. And it
is a forcing function—your competitors are using AI to build products and features
at unprecedented speeds, and the pace of change is not going to slow down to wait
for you. If you want to go fast, you need guardrails. If you want to cross over from
handcrafted, artisanal code to the software factory, that’s how you get there.
This is our opportunity to bring the engineering values we have long endorsed to the
mainstream—ownership, agency, a blending of disciplines. But none of it is possible
unless you can trace your intent into production and validate precisely what it is
doing.

Conclusion
Although observability has been around for half a century, observability in software
systems is a recent adaptation. For software systems, observability is a property of
dependability: it measures your ability to understand or debug any given system state,
represented on a continuum.
Modern systems are so complex, widely distributed, and resilient that mild degra‐
dations of service (and other unintended consequences of intentional change) are
harder than ever to identify and diagnose. Many leaders seem to be pinning their
hopes on AI SRE and agentic solutions to save the day. The extent to which this will
work is not yet known, but our track record with “magic” is not outstanding.
The three pillars approach (logs, metrics, and traces) works well for infrastructure,
but not for software development use cases. The pillars lack precision and depth.
Engineers have always bridged that gap with intuition and acts of heroism, but agents
can’t do that.
Storing all the signals together and widening the data is a technological and social
step change. It solves the problems of correlation and cardinality that have plagued
the three pillars and makes it possible to understand or debug any system state,
regardless of complexity, at a much more efficient price point.
In the next chapter, we will talk about how to build confidence in your code as it
crosses over to production.
