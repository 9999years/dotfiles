In the last few chapters, we’ve considered situations requiring analysis that are initi‐
ated by engineers curious about a specific aspect of their system, as well as how AI
agents can proactively perform these types of analysis. But an equally important con‐
sideration is how we conduct reactive investigations and maintain system stability,
and analyze the situations that come looking for us. How should you be setting up
alerts in high-observability systems in order to defend the experience of your users?
In this chapter, we’ll look at why threshold-based alerting so often burns out opera‐
tors through noisy alarms (alert fatigue), why distributed systems make those prob‐
lems worse, and how SLOs provide a more reliable, user-centered alternative. When
you can debug production safely and effectively in high-observability systems, service
level objectives (SLOs) provide several advantages over their traditional alerting
counterparts. We’ll examine those advantages and include a real-world case study
of accelerating the adoption of SLOs and getting to market faster by starting with
standardized, high-quality telemetry (guest contributed by Mat Vine).
One of the hardest parts of the SLO adoption journey is simply getting started by
drawing a line in the sand. Conventionally, that has required a great deal of expertise,
experimentation, and time. In this chapter, we’ll also show you how to leverage
generative AI to accelerate the process.
We’ll start by recapping the state of monitoring and alerting in low-observability
systems.




                                                                                     173
Threshold-Based Alerting Creates Alert Fatigue
In threshold-based monitoring, alerts are set up to measure what the team already
views as its internal priorities, or what’s easiest to measure. Metrics are used to track
simplistic attributes derived from the system state in hopes that a specific threshold
value correlates with service failure or may be a leading indicator of troubles ahead.
For example, trigger an alert if CPU utilization is above 80% for five minutes, or if
available memory drops below 10%, or if disk space is nearly full, or if more than x
many threads are running—or any set of other simple measures.
These signals can be useful as data, but as alerting triggers they’re often terrible.
Deviations that spiked CPU utilization don’t tell you whether your customers are
having a bad experience. That might indicate that a backup job is running. It might
be garbage collection. It might be a real outage. Or it may be the circuit breaker
kicking in to prevent cascading failure, so the actual outage is longer than the spike
we observe. The traditional monitor can’t distinguish that granularity, so it triggers
the alert. Over time, the team may intuitively learn that the alert isn’t accurate
enough and automatically dismiss it. When these signals are used as “potential-cause”
indicators—indirect proxies that may or may not correlate to user impact—they tend
to create a high percentage of false alarms.
Experienced engineering teams that own the operation of their software in produc‐
tion will often learn to tune out (or even suppress) these types of alerts because
they routinely go off and do not correlate with actual production impact or urgency.
Teams that do so regularly will adopt phrases like “Don’t worry about that alert; we
know the process runs out of memory from time to time. We might write automation
to restart it if the memory gets too high.”
Becoming accustomed to alerts with many false positives is a known problem and a
dangerous practice. In other industries, that problem is known as normalization of
deviance: a term coined during the investigation of the Challenger disaster. When
individuals in an organization regularly shut off alarms or fail to take action, they
eventually become so desensitized about the practice deviating from the expected
response that it no longer feels wrong to them.
At best, failures that are “normal” and disregarded are simply treated as background
noise. But that’s not a harmless coping mechanism. When ignoring alerts becomes
normal, the one alert that truly matters has to fight its way through a wall of learned
distrust. The background noise sets up all the wrong incentives leading to oversights
from cascading system failures.
In the software industry, the poor signal-to-noise ratio of threshold-based alerting often
leads to alert fatigue—and to gradually paying less attention to all alerts, because so
many of them are false alarms, not actionable, or simply not useful. Unfortunately,



174   |   Chapter 11: Using Service Level Objectives for Reliability
with threshold-based alerting, incidents compound the noise as every alarm fires
simultaneously.
Post-incident reviews often generate action items that create new, more important
alerts that, presumably, would have triggered in time to prevent the problem. That
leads to an even larger set of alerts generated during the next incident, unless
stale or flappy alerts are cleaned up. That pattern of alert escalation can create an
ever-increasing flood of alerts and an ever-increasing cognitive load on responding
engineers to determine which alerts matter and which don’t.
That type of dysfunction is so common in the software industry that many moni‐
toring and incident-response tool vendors proudly offer various solutions labeled
“AIOps,” which group, suppress, or otherwise try to process that alert load for you.
Additional layers mean additional complexity and potential sources of missed or
spurious alerts.
How did that become the acceptable and “normal” state of affairs? If the future
of running software in production is doomed to generate so much noise that it
must be artificially processed, it’s safe to say the situation has gone well beyond a
normalization of deviance. Industry vendors have now productized that deviance and
will happily sell you a machine learning solution for its management.
Metric and threshold-based alerting were once, long ago, the best tools we had to
understand the state of complex systems. As an industry, we now are layering on
further dysfunction and bandages rather than questioning whether those primitives
are the correct ones to build on.
Distributed systems make that dysfunction harder to ignore. When teams scale
the number of intercommunicating services they manage, and failure modes grow
exponentially, what used to just be annoying becomes an unacceptable reliability risk.
Modern systems require an alternative approach.

Threshold Alerting Is Only for Known-Unknowns
Threshold alerting works best when you can enumerate failure modes and write
checks for each one. In smaller, self-contained systems (a.k.a. monoliths), operations
teams can write specific monitoring checks for each precise state they expect to see
in production and, generally speaking, that works. But let’s be clear: threshold alerts
work best when you already have a fairly crisp and comprehensive mental model of
what “bad” looks like. In other words, they’re a great fit for known-unknowns.
A “known-unknown” is something that you are aware exists, but you don’t have
specific information about. In other words, you know your system has specific risks,
but not which ones will occur, or their exact severity. Known-unknowns are gaps in
knowledge that we recognize, and that we (typically) have a plan for.


                                             Threshold Alerting Is Only for Known-Unknowns   |   175
With threshold alerting, these are failure modes you know exist, you can name, and
you can measure, even if you don’t know whether they’re happening right now. For
example, known-unknowns are failure modes like:

  • The disk is 95% full.
  • The queue depth is over 70%.
  • TLS (Transport Layer Security) certification expires in 10 days.
  • The error rate is over 10% for five minutes.

You can write a check to measure system state, define a threshold, and perform an
action if the threshold is crossed (for instance, sending an engineer a page), because
this condition reliably predicts imminent customer pain, and we know what to do
about it.
In distributed systems, failure modes multiply. Distributed systems don’t eliminate
known-unknowns; they just become a tiny fraction of all possible failures that could
occur. As systems grow more interconnected, the number of plausible things that
could go wrong stops being additive and it becomes combinatorial.
Teams shifting from smaller or self-contained to distributed systems often respond by
trying to codify intuition: every remembered outage becomes a new alert, every scary
graph becomes a new dashboard, and every “we should have known” incident review
becomes another defined threshold.
For a while, that approach feels like rigor. In practice, it becomes an arms race
you cannot win. Trying to anticipate every possible failure or system state leads
to hundreds or thousands of checks that are brittle, expensive to maintain, and
rooted in tacit knowledge from past outages. That approach isn’t sustainable. The
checks aren’t maintainable, often the knowledge isn’t transferable, and past historical
behavior doesn’t necessarily predict future failures. And you’re still not focused on
what customers are actually experiencing.
Distributed systems are where it becomes much more of the norm to encounter
unknown-unknowns. Unknown-unknowns are the failures you didn’t know to antic‐
ipate. They’re things like emergent behaviors, weird interactions, cascading retries,
partial brownouts, dependency timeouts that only matter under a particular traffic
mix of very specific factors, a “harmless” deployment that tips a downstream cache
into eviction churn, and on and on and on. These are risks or factors so unforeseen
that they weren’t anywhere even near the radar—often a completely novel and never
previously contemplated issue.
You can’t write threshold alerts for issues you can’t yet imagine. Modern application
architectures regularly distribute load across multiple disparate systems. They split
up, shard, horizontally partition, and replicate data across geographically distributed


176   |   Chapter 11: Using Service Level Objectives for Reliability
systems. While these architectural decisions optimize for performance, resiliency, and
scalability, they can also make systemic failures near impossible to detect or predict.
Here, unknown-unknowns aren’t rare edge cases. They’re the default tax that you pay
for scale and complexity.
Worse, threshold alerts tend to fuse symptom with cause in a way that’s comforting,
but misleading. In a monolith, an abnormal number of threads could mean trouble—
or it could mean garbage collection doing its job, or the app loading an updated
library for the first time. In a distributed system, abnormal threads in one component
might be unrelated and benign noise, or it might be the first breadcrumb of upstream
latency that will hit customers in 10 minutes.
At 2 a.m., when production is inexplicably broken, those distinctions matter. The
on-call engineer isn’t just being asked to respond; they’re being asked to divine
whether the alert is real, whether it matters, and where to start looking. Thresholds
are supposed to reduce uncertainty. Most often, they just move that uncertainty into
your incident response channel.
Threshold alerts can also be triggered by automation. Modern systems already
remediate many common failure modes without human help: autoscaling, failovers,
circuit breakers, load balancing, and so forth. When your system heals itself, trigger‐
ing a notification isn’t necessary. Not only is that not an emergency but it’s a terrible
distraction. If your alerts wake people up for conditions that self-resolve, you’re
not building reliability—you’re manufacturing burnout. Those issues still deserve
investigation, but that’s a low priority accomplished during normal business hours.
Here’s the boundary: threshold alerting is a tool for known-unknowns. It can be a fit
for conditions with strong, repeatable, direct correlation to user impact and with a
clear remediation action. But they’re not a fit for detecting unknown-unknowns. No
static list of checks can cover the full space of emergent failures in complex systems.
In systems with high degrees of observability, we’ve seen how the core analysis
loop lets you quickly debug anomalies. When you’re able to readily debug issues in
production, the way to detect unknown-unknowns isn’t by elusively attempting to set
alerts for every possible condition. Instead, you focus on keeping only helpful alerts
and removing the rest.

User Experience Is a North Star
To echo the words of the SRE book once more, potential-cause alerts have poor
correlation to real problems, but symptom-of-user-pain alerts better allow you to
understand user impacts and the state of the system as experienced by customers.
How, then, do you focus on setting up alerts to detect failures that impact user expe‐
rience? This is where a departure from traditional monitoring approaches becomes
necessary. The traditional metrics-based monitoring approach relies on using static
thresholds to define optimal system conditions. Yet the performance of modern
systems—even at the infrastructure level—often changes shape dynamically under
different workloads. Static thresholds simply aren’t up to the task of monitoring
impact on user experience.
Setting up traditional alerting mechanisms to monitor user experience means that
system engineers must choose arbitrary constants that predict when that experience
is poor. For example, these alerts might be implemented to trigger when “10 users
have experienced slow page-load times,” or “the 95th percentile of requests have
persisted above a certain number of milliseconds.” In a threshold-based approach,
system engineers are required to divine which exact static measures indicate that
unacceptable problems are occurring.
Yet system performance varies significantly throughout the day as different users,
in different time zones, interact with your services in different ways. During slow
traffic times, when you may have hundreds of concurrent sessions, 10 users experi‐
encing slow page-load times might be a significant metric. But that significance drops
sharply at peak load times when you may have tens of thousands of concurrent
sessions running.
Remember that in distributed systems, failure is inevitable. Small transient failures
are always occurring without you necessarily noticing. Common examples include a
failed request that later succeeds on a retry; a critical process that initially fails, but
its completion is merely delayed until it gets routed to a newly provisioned host; or
a service that becomes unresponsive until its requests are routed to a backup service.
The additional latency introduced by these types of transient failures might blend




178   |   Chapter 11: Using Service Level Objectives for Reliability
into normal operations during peak traffic, but P95 response times would be more
sensitive to individual datapoints during periods of low traffic.
In a similar vein, these examples also illustrate the coarseness of time-based metrics.
Let’s say P95 response time is being gauged in five-minute intervals. Every five
minutes, performance over the trailing five-minute interval reports a value that
triggers an alert if it exceeds a static threshold. If that value exceeds the threshold, the
entire five-minute interval is considered bad (and conversely, any five-minute interval
that didn’t exceed the threshold is considered good). Alerting on that type of metric
results in high rates of both false positives and false negatives. It also has a level of
granularity that is insufficient to diagnose exactly when and where a problem may
have occurred.
Static thresholds are too rigid and coarse to reliably indicate degraded user experi‐
ence in a dynamic environment. They lack context. Reliable alerts need a finer level of
both granularity and reliability. This is where SLOs can help.

Reliable Alerting with Service Level Objectives
SLOs are internal goals for the measurement of service health. Popularized by the
Google SRE book, SLOs are a key part of setting external SLAs between service
providers and their customers. These internal measures are typically more stringent
than externally facing agreements or availability commitments. With that added
stringency, SLOs can provide a safety net that helps teams identify and remediate
issues before external user experience reaches unacceptable levels.

Much has been written about the use of SLOs for service reliability, and they are
not unique to the world of observability. However, using an SLO-based approach to
monitoring service health requires having observability built into your applications.
SLOs can be, and sometimes are, implemented in systems without observability. But
the ramifications of doing so can have severe unintended consequences.
SLOs quantify an agreed-upon target for service availability, based on critical end-
user journeys rather than system metrics. That target is measured using service level
indicators (SLIs), which categorize the system state as good or bad. Two kinds of SLIs
exist: time-based measures (e.g., “99th percentile latency less than 300 milliseconds
over each five-minute window”) and event-based measures (e.g., “proportion of events
that took less than 300 milliseconds during a given rolling time window”).



                                                 Reliable Alerting with Service Level Objectives   |   179
Both attempt to express the impact on end users but differ on whether data about the
incoming user traffic has been pre-aggregated by time bucket. We recommend setting
SLIs that use event-based measures as opposed to time-based measures, because
event-based measures provide a more reliable and more granular way to quantify the
state of a service. We discuss the reasons for this in the next chapter.
Let’s look at an example of how to define an event-based SLI. You might define a
good customer experience as being a state when “a user should be able to successfully
load your home page and see a result quickly.” Expressing that with an SLI means
qualifying events and then determining whether they meet your conditions. In this
example, your SLI would do the following:

 1. Look for any event with a request path of /home.
 2. Screen qualifying events for conditions in which the event duration is less than
    100 milliseconds.
 3. If the event duration is less than 100 milliseconds and was served successfully,
    consider it OK.
 4. If the event duration is at least 100 milliseconds, consider that event an error
    even if it returned a success code.

Any event that is an error would spend some of the error budget allowed in your
SLO. We’ll closely examine patterns for proactively managing SLO error budgets and
triggering alerts in the next chapter. For now, we’ll summarize by saying that given
enough errors, your systems could alert you to a potential breach of the SLO.
SLOs narrow the scope of your alerts to consider only symptoms that impact what
the users of your service experience. If an underlying condition is impacting “a user
loading the home page and seeing it quickly,” an alert should be triggered, because
someone needs to investigate why. However, there is no correlation as to why and how
the service might be degraded. We simply know that something is wrong.
In contrast, traditional monitoring relies on a potential-cause-based approach: a pre‐
viously known cause is detected (e.g., an abnormal number of threads), signaling that
users might experience undesirable symptoms (slow page-load times). That approach
fuses the “what” and “why” of a situation in an attempt to help pinpoint where
investigation should begin. But, as you’ve seen, there’s a lot more to your services than
just the known states of up, down, or even slow. Emergent failure modes can impact
your users long before coarse synthetic probes will tell you. Decoupling “what” from
“why” is one of the most important distinctions in writing good monitoring with
maximum signal and minimum noise.
The second criteria for an alert to be helpful is that it must be actionable. A system-
level potential-cause alert that the CPU is high tells you nothing about whether users
are impacted and whether you should take action. In contrast, SLO-based alerts are


180   |   Chapter 11: Using Service Level Objectives for Reliability
symptom based: they tell you that something is wrong. They are actionable because
now it is up to the responder to determine why users are seeing an impact and
to mitigate. However, if you cannot sufficiently debug your systems, resolving the
problem will be challenging. That’s where a shift to observability becomes essential:
debugging in production must be safe and natural.
In an SLO-based world, you need high-observability tooling because it allows you to
debug from first principles. With rich telemetry, you can start wide and then filter
to reduce the search space. That approach means you can respond to determine the
source of any problem, regardless of how novel or emergent the failure may be. You
can improvise and adapt solutions to find any problem in your system.
Instrumentation that provides rich and meaningful telemetry is the basis for that
approach. Being able to quickly analyze that telemetry data to validate or falsify
hypotheses is what empowers you to feel confidence in removing noisy, unhelpful
alerts. Decoupling the “what” from the “why” in your alerting is possible when using
SLO-based alerts in tandem with observability.
When most alerts are not actionable, that quickly leads to alert fatigue. To eliminate
that problem, it’s time to delete all of your unactionable alerts. If that seems infeasible,
it likely means you’ll also need a cultural shift to support that change.
To illustrate how culture change is achieved around the adoption of SLOs for mon‐
itoring, we’ll look at a real example with a firsthand account from Mat Vine with
ANZ Bank—a multinational bank and financial services company headquartered in
Australia.

Case Study: Changing Culture Toward SLO-Based Alerts
                                              This section is contributed by Mat Vine,
                                     Cloud, Quality, and Reliability Lead at ANZ Bank

When I first joined an incumbent bank, I was challenged to build the SRE practice
within the transformation arm of the enterprise. On my first day, I sat in on my new
team’s first “major incident” rehearsal. The very next day, my boss asked for a launch
readiness estimate. I thought 12–18 months was a realistic timeframe. He countered
with a goal of 12 weeks before our pilot launch. For many in operational roles, this
immediate, high-pressure shift in expectations would be enough to send them into a
panic. Ultimately, would you believe that our reliability team was able to green-light a
launch within 12 weeks? Why was my estimate so off? Using high-quality telemetry,
defining SLOs, and setting error budgets enabled us to ship with confidence.
Investing in telemetry first is what buys you the leverage to move faster on the
cultural and organizational work that actually sticks. Fortunately, as a major Google
Cloud partner, we had the privilege of leadership who’d already been educated about


                                          Case Study: Changing Culture Toward SLO-Based Alerts   |   181
SLOs and error budgets through Google’s Customer Reliability Engineering program
and the SRE book, written by Googlers and published by O’Reilly. We had a modern
cloud native architecture, built predominantly in-house, and we’d made early bets
on OpenTelemetry—which gave us a level of telemetry standardization that we’d
eventually heavily lean on.
On the surface, this was a luxurious greenfield launching pad. It should have made
me far more optimistic than my estimate of 12–18 months suggested. The reality,
though, was that we were in a phase of rapid development approaching our pilot
launch. Infrastructure and software were being built and changed quickly. The prod‐
uct itself was still being designed, tested, and reimplemented. These were surprisingly
poor conditions from which to start an SLO revolution. So, why didn’t we just
fall back to tried-and-true threshold alerting? We viewed that as a one-way trap
because arbitrary thresholds tend to calcify. The easiest objective to set is “whatever
performance we have today,” but that creates a feedback loop where you are alerting
on statistical deviations rather than customer impact.
Instead, we realized that a small, core set of user journeys touched the majority of
our critical assets and dependencies. By focusing only on those, we could achieve
high system coverage without drowning in noise or tempting team members to
proliferate more thresholds. This enables us to treat the infrastructure of alerting
as a commodity, while spending our limited time on higher-value work: building
consensus on how the system should perform, rather than just alerting on how it was
performing.
In mature organizations with established SLO adoption, you would start by introduc‐
ing SLOs at the design phase for each new component or system. But in a new,
rapidly developing system, without any immediate operational pressure or customer
satisfaction to balance, SLOs are too abstract. My estimate of 12–18 months was
based on a then-current lead time of one year, during which no SLOs had been set. I
thought there to be two fundamental conditions for SLOs to deliver real value:
Real-world demand on your systems.
    This means not theoretical traffic, not load tests, but actual users depending on
    the systems you’ve built.
Scale—real scale.
    SLOs need to be anywhere across your organization where there exists delivery
    or operational pressure, not isolated to a single team or practice area. SLOs need
    to be understood as a part of your team’s operating model.
So, how could we replace these?
In our scenario, the approaching deadline forced clarity. We stopped debating what
might matter and started identifying what would matter on launch day. This meant
narrowing down to less than a handful of user journeys across multiple problem

domains that would be genuinely important to the day-one customer experience.
There would be time later to flesh out more comprehensive SLOs.
Our early bets in OTel had taken the form of an in-house package which extended
the OTel SDK with our own domain-specific semantic conventions. Because most of
our services were already emitting telemetry with predictable schemas, we were able
to avoid spending weeks writing regular expressions and mapping data fields across
heterogeneous logs. That amount of time, which would have been enormous if we’d
had a less standardized environment, was reinvested into more valuable activities:
mapping user journeys, rationalizing which metrics made sense as SLIs, and building
consensus across development, operations, and product stakeholders. Although we
didn’t have real-world demand, we knew that we could get faithful estimates with
load testing, and adjust in real time after our product launched.
SLOs, like any high-observability implementation, are an investment. If the only time
your teams interact with observability data is during incident response, you’re either
underinvesting in making that data useful, or you’re paying too much for it. The
same applies to SLOs; they require organizational conditions to thrive. Teams need
to be challenged to make decisions that balance risk and velocity. If that type of
pressure doesn’t exist—as it rarely does during the early development phase—SLOs
are unlikely to gain the traction needed to make an appreciable dent in the way you
build or operate your systems.
I’m skeptical of applying the standard technology rollout for SLOs where you pick
one high-performing team, get them to use the tool, and then hope that the trial
usage tells you enough about how to scale it organization-wide. The problem is that
high-performing teams almost never need SLOs. Those teams prioritize and govern
themselves naturally. They balance risk and velocity through their own internal
processes—be it internal tech debt policies, toil indicators, or strong stakeholder
relationships. That performance tends to come with trust already built. For these
teams, SLOs aren’t a needle mover.
If you’re starting from scratch, instead identify the most problematic or important
areas in your organization. Target the teams that are supporting the systems most
critical to your service, those experiencing the highest operational stress, or those
with the least productive or trusting relationships with product and business stake‐
holders. These are the teams where SLOs will shift behavior and decision making.
When you target these teams, make sure to focus energy directly on what matters:
establishing clear objectives, rationalizing the indicators you’ve selected, and building
genuine consensus on those choices across all stakeholders. Wrangling telemetry data
into the correct shape might eventually be necessary, but it’s almost never the most
important thing to do first. If a lot of your time is being spent there, you likely don’t
have the foundation to launch from.

My experience is that high-quality telemetry requires investment as a standalone
capability. Good telemetry—standardized, semantically rich, and with high cardin‐
ality—provides immediate benefits for teams. It can help them learn about their
systems and alleviate operational stress when it arises. Likely, the investment in a
mostly standardized telemetry solution will pay off for most organizations even if the

The reality is that my original estimate of 12–18 months was both right and wrong.
Making SLOs a genuine part of our SDLC, bringing them into product prioritization
conversations, and eventually mapping hundreds of user journeys and business pro‐
cesses into defined objectives did take 18 months. Fortunately, establishing a strong
enough base for us to have confidence in our product’s beta launch was achievable in
12 weeks. We implemented relatively comprehensive SLO-based monitoring systems
within a few months because our early investment in OTel and the standardization
introduced enabled us to focus on architecture, business alignment, and our opera‐
tional practices.
This difference matters when scaling across an organization. Everyone involved in
the software delivery lifecycle falls in love with the idea of error budgets at first sight.
But the work to set objectives, agree on them, and build consensus on what a working
system is and how often it should work is both difficult and necessary. Every time you
get pulled into crafting regex or stitching together session IDs across heterogeneous
data formats, you lose time on the harder, more valuable work. By embracing unified
observability from the start, and building SLOs based on that observability data, we
lowered the friction required to succeed with SLOs.

Accelerating SLO Adoption with Generative AI
Mat Vine’s account of SLO adoption at a regulated enterprise highlights many of the
challenges and benefits of SLO adoption. Seeking organizational alignment can be
particularly challenging for many teams. Often, that’s what seems to make drafting
SLIs and SLOs deceptively hard when you’re getting started. It’s less that teams face
difficulty in writing good queries, and more that SLOs force you to get organizational
clarity.
You have to decide which user journeys matter most, what “success” means for
those journeys, and how much imperfection the business is willing to tolerate. That
decision tree requires translating product intent into measurable criteria, aligning
stakeholders who may have competing priorities, and then choosing thresholds that
strike a balance between not constantly generating noise nor quietly missing a degra‐
ded experience. Early SLO efforts often get stuck in ambiguity: everyone agrees



184   |   Chapter 11: Using Service Level Objectives for Reliability
reliability matters, but nobody wants to be the person who picks the number or owns
making the trade-off.
Generative AI can help accelerate this early stage. LLMs can help by solving the
blank-sheet-of-paper problem: they can turn vague intent into a concrete draft
fast enough to then allow the real work to begin—review, debate, calibration, and
iteration. There is no substitute for actual human understanding, ownership, and
judgment.
In our practice as SREs, we have found LLMs to be useful for scenarios beyond
simply writing application code. After all, infrastructure can be written as code, and
so can observability primitives such as saved queries, dashboards, and SLOs. Those
artifacts encode assumptions about what matters and how to measure it. With that
context, LLMs are particularly good at taking ambiguous human language about
requirements and producing draft recommendations that you can refine.
Conventionally, it would take hiring an experienced SRE (as an employee or consul‐
tant) to propose reasonable SLO targets and explain trade-offs. In many cases, an
LLM can provide a workable first pass when you give it the right context about the
service and the business expectations. It doesn’t replace expertise, but it can reduce
the time it takes to create good working drafts.
Even without post-training, custom prompting, or retrieval augmented generation
(RAG), using open-weights models can still yield surprisingly good results. For exam‐
ple, when asked what reliability target to set for a public-cloud checkout workflow
processing $1 million per month, a model might initially recommend an aggressive

From Targets to Implementation: Drafting a Service Level Indicator
LLMs are also useful for moving what to measure toward concrete expressions. They
can then help you craft SLIs corresponding to the SLOs leveraging your observability
tooling when given sufficient context about the telemetry data. Event-based SLIs
often require carefully defined eligibility criteria and success criteria. Models can
draft those expressions quickly. But details matter, and the mistakes tend to be subtle,
like missing an important filter, choosing the wrong error signal, or failing to align
with your system’s query or grammar conventions.
The following example demonstrates how the conversation from earlier can be con‐
tinued, assuming the user fills in the blanks for the grammar and functions available
in their query language:
      Prompt = Now, you will be generating an expression in a domain specific language
               to define a service level indicator (SLI). The SLI measures the
               performance and reliability of a service per request based on

    Here is the title describing the goal of the overall SLO:

    <title>

    </title>

    <description>
    Checkout should succeed within four seconds, 99.99% of the time.
    </description>

    Use $PROVIDER’s field syntax, looking it up if necessary from the documentation.
    Assume standard OpenTelemetry semantic conventions.

    separate <reasoning> and <expression> in your results.

    Claude Opus 4.6:

    time." The 99.99% target and the rolling time period are configured on the SLO
    object itself; the SLI expression only needs to classify each event as true
    (good), false (bad), or null (not applicable).

    There are three components to express:

    1. **Qualifier** (scopes to checkout events only): Events where `http.route`
       matches the checkout endpoint. Non-matching events return null and are
       excluded from the SLO calculation. Adapt the route string to match your
       actual instrumentation.

       server-side or client-error failure). Using the stable OTel semantic
       convention field `http.response.status_code`. If you’re on older
       conventions, substitute `http.status_code`.
    3. **Latency criterion**: The span duration is at or below 4000ms. `duration_ms`
       is Honeycomb’s standard span duration field.

    The `IF()` wrapper ensures non-checkout events return null. The `AND()` inside
    requires both success and latency criteria to be met for the event to count as
    "good."

    Configure the SLO with `target_percentage: 99.9` and an appropriate `time_period`

Once upon a time, you would need to use the Google SLO workshop materials or
read the entire SLO book by Hidalgo (mentioned earlier) to learn how to do this.
Now, you can get 90% good enough by using an LLM with appropriate prompting,
and iterate from there. It can be toilsome to help hundreds of teams come up with
SLOs and keep them up to date. We can better scale ourselves by writing best
practices and embedding them into prompts rather than becoming a bottleneck of
expertise.
At the end of the day, it’s better to have more people using imperfect SLOs than to
have SLOs remain a tool of the elite SRE-enabled teams. And if your observability
tooling allows you to edit SLOs with retroactive effect, the consequences of a mistake
or oversight are relatively minor. It is much easier to iterate and adjust your SLOs
once you have practical feedback on what’s working versus what is not.
The right operating model for getting started is to treat the LLM as a draft generator,
not an authority. Validate outputs against real telemetry. Run them on production-
like data. Use your expertise to review them the way you would review a pull request.

Going further, a strong prompt establishes what fields exist in an SLO definition,
what good titles and descriptions look like, how event-based SLIs should evaluate eli‐
gibility versus success, and how to produce an improved (but functionally equivalent)
version of an SLO definition in JSON. It also asks for concise feedback and a clear
explanation of changes.
For example, your prompt may look like this:
      Prompt = You are an AI that takes a user’s service level objective (SLO),
      reviewing it based on best practices, and provides suggestions for improvement.

      The SLO has exactly six things: name, description, time period in days, rate, SLI
      alias, and SLI expression that gets computed on each event.

      When grading an SLO, you think step by step through each component and provide
      concise feedback on how it could align better with best practices.

      An SLO aims to measure the reliability of a product, feature, or service and
      provide a realistic target to manage the system according to.

      Good titles clearly describe the critical user journey and its success criteria
      using affirmative language.

      Good descriptions also use affirmative language to elaborate on what success
      means, and also elaborate with specific eligibility criteria for events and the
      evaluation criteria to compute for them. The evaluation criteria often has a
      numeric threshold, or checks a boolean value like success or error.

      SLIs typically have an eligibility criterion for which events are in scope,
      and a success criterion representing whether each in-scope event delivered a
      satisfactory user experience. If the eligibility criterion is not met, the
      derived column should evaluate to NULL.Otherwise, it should evaluate to TRUE
      on a good user experience and FALSE on a failed user experience.

      Good SLIs typically have as simple of an expression as possible.

      Finally, when you finish reviewing an SLO, you provide a JSON object of a
      modified SLO definition that aligns better with best practices, and then an
      explanation of why it’s better.

      When you suggest an improved SLI, make sure it’s always functionally equivalent.

Once you find the best prompts that work for your organizational context and your
tooling, you should check them into version control, keep them simple, and make
them consistent across the team. Instead of requiring every team to “borrow” SRE
expertise to get started, you embed that expertise into a repeatable workflow that can
be shared among teams, individuals, and organizations.
Sharing that expertise can be a key accelerator. It’s better to help more teams adopt
imperfect but improving SLOs than to keep SLOs confined to the teams already
fluent in them. The key to improving SLOs is ensuring they are reviewed and refined,
which is simple when SLO definitions can be edited retroactively.
In summary, you can supercharge your SRE practices and roll out SLIs and SLOs
faster with the combination of good observability tooling and a small amount of LLM
assistance. Over time, your teams may take deeper ownership and refine definitions
by hand; either using the model as a reviewer, a drafter, or simply as a rubber duck to
understand the trade-offs.

Conclusion
In this chapter, we introduced SLOs as a more effective alerting strategy than tradi‐
tional threshold monitoring. Alert fatigue is so prevalent in our industry, largely
because traditional monitoring relies on potential-cause signals that are easy to mea‐
sure and trigger often, yet are often very weakly correlated with real user impact.
Alert fatigue can be solved by focusing on creating only helpful alerts that meet two
criteria. First, they must trigger as reliable indicators that the user experience of your
service is in a degraded state. Second, they must be actionable. Any alert that does not
meet that criteria is no longer serving its purpose and should be deleted.
SLOs help because they decouple the “what” from the “why.” They alert on symptoms
that map directly to customer experience rather than on presumed causes. When
SLOs are measured with event-based SLIs, alerts tend to be more reliable and less dis‐
ruptive, with fewer false positives and false negatives—especially when distinguishing
systemic problems from sporadic failures.
However, that’s only half the solution. SLO alerts can tell you that user pain exists,
not why it’s happening. To make SLO-based alerts operationally effective, production
systems must be debuggable, which is where having a high degree of observability
becomes essential.
Adopting SLO-based alerting is a sociotechnical challenge. Removing unactionable
alerts requires shared standards, confidence in the new signals, and a deliberate
shift away from long-held traditions. Mat Vine’s firsthand account illustrates getting
buy-in and operationalizing these practices.
