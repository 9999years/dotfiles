In this chapter, we’re going to dive deeper into the role of AI and agents in the world
of observability practices, and how they can help you implement observability-driven
development.
We need to say this part out loud: this is one of the chapters that risks aging in dog
years. The agentic AI space is moving fast. Models are improving, context windows
are expanding, and the ways people build and operate agents are changing so fast that
any advice we give could feel dated by the time you read this. So, rather than try to
predict the exact shape of the tooling landscape, we are going to focus on something
more durable: how to think about this problem space and which first principles
should guide your decisions as the technology continues to evolve.
Today’s agents are typically constrained by incomplete context, uneven tool use, and
limits on what they can hold in working memory. Some of the advice in this chapter
is a function of those limitations. But we hope that the deeper lesson you’ll take from
this content isn’t about any one particular limitation or architectural pattern.
Observability has always been about the tension between context and precision.
Whether held by humans or machines, the outcome will continue to depend on the
quality, completeness, and accessibility of the context available to them.
To be clear, we authors are under no illusion that this space will do anything but keep
up the dramatic rate of change. However, we believe the principles in this chapter
should hold for the foreseeable future: instrument richly, preserve context, design
strong feedback loops, and be mindful of where automation helps and where the best

The way engineers understand and operate software is changing in ways that would
have seemed far-fetched just a few years ago. Observability has always been about
giving humans better tools to make sense of their systems: better dashboards, bet‐
ter query languages, better alerting pipelines. The feedback loops that have shaped
software engineering for decades—the cycle of writing, deploying, observing, and
fixing—were built on the assumption of a human at every gate, synthesizing signals,
and making judgments about what to do next.
That assumption is being quietly dismantled, and the implications for how we think
about observability are significant. As AI agents begin to take a seat at the software
development table—surfacing data but also reasoning over it, acting on it, and
increasingly shaping the systems that produce it in the first place—we are witnessing
an inflection point for the entire software development lifecycle. This shift promises
a world where agents handle the mechanical investigative groundwork, but it also
creates a new, urgent responsibility for human engineers: providing the high-fidelity
context that these systems require to operate safely.
This chapter explores the practical utility of agents within observability, identifies the
boundaries where they fall short, and argues that the gap between success and failure
continues to come down to a single underlying problem: context.

What Is an AI Agent for Observability?
In Chapter 7, an AI agent was defined as a software harness that combines a genera‐
tive LLM and some subset of tools, or remote procedure calls exposed through an
API. For observability, AI agents use an LLM as their reasoning engine, and the
tools give it the ability to take actions such as querying databases, calling other APIs,
writing and executing code, or triggering automated workflows.
Unlike a chatbot that simply responds to questions in a single turn, an agent can
pursue a goal across multiple steps—deciding what to do next based on what it learns
along the way and adjusting its approach when earlier assumptions turn out to be
wrong. Applying that to an observability workflow means that an agent could follow
a complex decision tree when an alert is triggered, such as shown in Figure 10-1.

important to understand why things get complicated.

The Pitfalls of Querying Without Context
One of the simplest ways to begin integrating an agent into your observability work‐
flows is by asking it to query your telemetry data for analysis; in other words, you’re
delegating data retrieval.
Give an agent access to your telemetry data, and let it answer questions in natural
language: “What’s causing the spike in errors?” or “Which service has the highest
latency over the past hour?” or “Show me all logs from the payments service in
the last 30 minutes.” By granting an LLM access to your telemetry store, you can
bypass the hurdle of complex query languages. For teams that spend hours wrestling
with complex query languages, having a natural language interface is a genuine
improvement.
While this is a natural place to start, the problem with this common agent integration
approach is that querying in isolation produces poor results. The resulting failure
modes are instructive because they reveal the fundamental limitation of agents oper‐
ating without sufficient context:
The agent doesn’t know what it doesn’t know.
    When an experienced engineer investigates an incident, they begin with a
    hypothesis that is shaped by weeks or months of accumulated context. For
    instance, they know the payments service tends to misbehave after deployments
    to a specific downstream dependency. They might know a particular upstream
    API has been flaky since the infrastructure team migrated it last quarter. They
    also know what “normal” looks like on a regular Tuesday afternoon, versus a
    Tuesday afternoon following a long weekend. An agent querying telemetry data
    cold has none of that. It will find what the query returns, which may be accurate
    but is often beside the point.

Naming is chaos in production.
   Services get named by whoever created them, at whatever stage of a company’s
   growth that happened to be, and the names accumulate rather than converge.
   You will have user-service, users-svc, svc-user, and legacy-user-api all
   doing related things in the same system, with varying degrees of documentation.
   An agent without a canonical map of that nomenclature will confidently draw
   conclusions from incomplete or misattributed data, and it will do so without
   flagging that it might be wrong.
The data surface is enormous.
    A moderately complex production system might produce millions of spans per
    minute across dozens of services. Without knowing which signals are meaningful
    in a given context, an agent will either miss the relevant pattern in an ocean
    of noise or surface correlations that are technically present in the data but
    operationally irrelevant. Knowing what to look at is itself a form of expertise, and
    that expertise has to come from somewhere.
Correlation is not causation, and agents often conflate the two.
    Agents (again, limiting to those using LLMs) are good at finding patterns in data,
    but they are not inherently good at distinguishing “these two things happened at
    the same time” from “this thing caused that thing.” In a distributed system where
    dozens of services are constantly interacting and covarying, spurious correlations
    are abundant. Without an explicit topology model and some sense of the causal
    structure of the system, an agent attributing a root cause is doing something
    closer to educated guessing than analysis.
You might get surprisingly good results by piping error logs directly to an LLM in a
small, well-named, well-instrumented system where the services are few enough to
reason about in a single prompt. But the systems that matter most to organizations—
the ones where getting observability right is genuinely critical to the business—are
the ones that are large, messy, and built over years by rotating teams with different
conventions. Those are exactly the environments where naive agent approaches break
down most visibly.

Proven Use Cases for Observability Agents
Agents are not just a future promise projected onto an incomplete present. There are
areas where they are already adding real value in the observability space. Understand‐
ing what those areas have in common is as useful as understanding the individual use
cases.
Let’s look at three proven use cases where agents demonstrate a high degree of value.




                                                  Proven Use Cases for Observability Agents   |   165
Incident Response
When an alert fires, the first 15 minutes of an incident are often consumed by the
same mechanical investigative steps: pulling the relevant dashboards, querying recent
logs, checking what was deployed in the last few hours, and mapping the blast radius
across dependent services.
This work is important but largely procedural, and it is well-suited to an agent. An
agent can execute those steps faster than a human, run several queries in parallel, and
surface a structured summary of the situation before the on-call engineer has even
fully oriented themselves.
The best implementations here are careful about scope. They use agents to augment
the on-call engineer rather than attempting to automate incident resolution end
to end. The agent handles the investigative groundwork; the human interprets the
summary, applies domain judgment, and makes the decision about what to do.
Given where reliability and trust in autonomous agents currently sit, this division of
responsibility is both practical and appropriate.

Explaining Errors and Patterns in Telemetry Data
One of the most underappreciated use cases for agents in observability is translation:
taking raw telemetry signals and producing coherent, plain-language explanations of
what they mean. A stack trace, a slow trace waterfall showing cascading latency, an
anomalous shift in a metric’s distribution—these things require genuine expertise to
interpret, and that expertise is unevenly distributed across engineering teams.
An agent that can look at a trace and explain that “this shows a sequential chain
of database calls where each one waits for the previous to complete, which is a
classic N+1 query pattern introduced by an ORM (object-relational mapping) mis‐
configuration” is compressing hours of learning and debugging into something a
junior engineer can act on immediately. This matters both for incident response,
where time is the constraint, and for building organizational understanding over
time by surfacing patterns that engineers might otherwise not have the bandwidth to
investigate.

Improving Instrumentation Quality
Using agents to help with instrumentation is a use case that often doesn’t get
enough attention in discussions about AI and observability, but it can give you great
long-term leverage (for a deep dive on improving instrumentation with agents, see
Chapter 7).
Getting teams to instrument their code well is genuinely difficult. Developers under‐
stand, in the abstract, that they should add spans with meaningful attributes, emit



166   |   Chapter 10: The Role of AI Agents for Observability
structured wide events with rich context, and propagate distributed tracing head‐
ers across service boundaries. In practice, instrumentation is consistently an after‐
thought. It gets added inconsistently, by whoever last touched a given file, and the
quality degrades over time as the codebase grows and the people who set the original
conventions move on.
Agents are well-positioned to address that gap. Given a function, a service boundary,
or a pull request, an agent can identify what instrumentation is missing, what is
structured poorly, and what does not conform to the team’s conventions, and then
implement improvements correctly and consistently. Best practices like wide events,
proper attribute naming, and appropriate log levels—which take years to develop as
institutional knowledge and spread unevenly across teams—can be encoded into an
agent’s context and applied at the point of code authorship rather than bolted on after
the fact.
Across all three of these use cases, what will make or break your success is how much
context your agent has over your system. With poor context or vague prompting,
agents produce generic output that looks plausible but does not fit the system or
the team’s conventions. An agent instructed to “add observability to this service”
without knowing your instrumentation library, your attribute naming conventions,
your service’s role in the broader architecture, or your logging standards will produce
something that compiles but does not help. With precise, rich context, the same agent
produces output that a senior engineer would approve and merge without significant
changes.
Along these lines, there are a few additional places that agents can be incredibly
useful. Let’s look at those use cases as well.

Observability-Adjacent Use Cases for Agents
Beyond the core observability workflows, there is a whole category of operational
work that teams have historically been poor at, not because the work is technically
difficult, but because it requires sustained attention and is consistently crowded out
by more urgent priorities. Agents are well-suited to a lot of this work.
Alert quality is a perennial disaster in most engineering organizations. Alerts get
added during incidents or out of anxiety, tuned rarely if ever, and accumulate over
time into a wall of noise that engineers learn to ignore. Teams end up with hundreds
of active alerts, many of which fire regularly and are silently acknowledged without
investigation because everyone knows they are not meaningful.
An agent can audit an alert configuration against actual incident history and traffic
patterns, identify which alerts correlate with real user-facing problems, flag which
ones fire constantly without ever leading to meaningful action, and propose specific
threshold adjustments based on observed baselines. Importantly, this can be done


                                                Observability-Adjacent Use Cases for Agents   |   167
continuously rather than as a periodic cleanup exercise that never quite makes it to
the top of the backlog.
Service level objectives suffer from a related problem. Defining good SLOs requires a
real understanding of traffic patterns, historical reliability, the distribution of error
types, and what users actually experience. Most teams either skip SLOs entirely, set
them at whatever feels intuitively reasonable without grounding in data, or copy a
template from a blog post and apply it without adapting it to their system.
An agent with access to historical performance data and a model of the services
can propose SLO definitions that are grounded in observed behavior. It can identify
which SLOs are being approached before they are breached, and can surface trends
that indicate a service is drifting toward unreliability before it becomes an incident.
(For detailed guidance on using agents for SLO creation, see Chapter 11.)
Both of these represent work that teams universally agree is important but struggle
to prioritize because the cost of not doing them is diffuse and deferred. Agents make
this category of work tractable by removing the activation energy required to get
started and by operating continuously rather than in occasional bursts of attention.
While the use cases we’ve covered demonstrate the high leverage that agents can
provide in controlled scenarios, their effectiveness is fundamentally tethered to the
environment they inhabit. But as we increasingly rely on more automation, we also
risk losing the institutional knowledge that makes providing rich context possible.

The Production Problem: The Mental
Model That’s Disappearing
Production environments are messy, and that messiness is a fundamental property
rather than a failure of discipline. Services accumulate over years of changing pri‐
orities, changing teams, and changing infrastructure constraints. Names drift as
organizations restructure. The team that originally built a service has turned over
twice, and the documentation, if it ever existed, was last updated 18 months ago. A
critical business path runs through four different languages, three cloud providers,
and a legacy service that nobody wants to touch but everyone depends on. There are
operational quirks that only two people know about, and one of those people left six
months ago.
When an experienced engineer investigates a production issue, they navigate this
complexity using a mental model that has been assembled over months or years of
working with the system. For example, they know the topology of services: which
things call which other things, where the critical paths are, and which dependencies
are known to be unreliable. They know the nomenclature: that auth-proxy and api-
gateway are the same service, depending on which team you ask, and that orders-v2

is what actually processes checkouts despite the name suggesting otherwise. They
know the history: that the telemetry from the inventory service has been partially
broken since the Q3 migration and its error rates cannot be taken at face value. All
of this knowledge is carried implicitly, and it shapes every query they write and every
hypothesis they form.
For an agent to be effective in production, it also needs access to that mental model,

An agent may need rich context around concepts like service topology, naming
conventions, critical paths, known quirks, or recent incidents. With poor context,
the agent issues generic queries, gets an incomplete picture, and reaches incorrect or
shallow conclusions. With rich context, the agent can run targeted queries, identify
relevant signals, and arrive at an accurate diagnosis. The biggest differentiator in
agent effectiveness is not the model alone, but the completeness and quality of the
operational context it has available.
The context that an observability agent needs to cover may include several distinct
layers of knowledge:
Service topology
    What calls what, what depends on what, where the critical paths run, and which
    services are at the edges versus at the core of the system

Naming conventions
   A canonical mapping of service names, their aliases, their owners, and what they
   actually do
Deployment context
    What has changed recently, which services have active deployments, and where
    the risk surface is elevated
Known issues
   Long-running bugs, degraded dependencies, services that are under maintenance
   or running in a degraded mode
Recent incidents
    What went wrong in the last 30 days, which services were involved, how long
    they were degraded, what the blast radius was, and whether the root cause was
    ever actually confirmed
Business context
    What a given error rate or latency change actually means for the business, which
    endpoints are revenue-critical, and what the acceptable thresholds are from a
    user impact perspective
The uncomfortable reality is that maintaining and providing this context so that an
agent can be successful is getting harder over time, not easier. With the rise of coding
agents that write and deploy code with limited human supervision, the engineers who
would have built up that mental model through the ordinary process of authoring,
debugging, and operating software are increasingly removed from the details. Code
gets written and shipped at a pace that makes it difficult for any individual to main‐
tain a coherent picture of the whole system, and the organizational knowledge that
would have accumulated around a service through the ordinary cycle of incidents
and postmortems forms more slowly. The mental model becomes fragmented and
incomplete.
Agents operating in production need to compensate for this erosion of institutional
knowledge. They need to be given—explicitly and in a maintained form—the context
that engineers previously held in their heads. Building that context layer, keeping it
current as the system evolves, and making it available to agents at the moment it is
needed, is one of the most important and least-solved problems in this space. Teams
that treat it as a first-class engineering concern will get significantly more out of their
agent integrations than teams that treat it as something the agent should figure out on
its own. (For more detail on increasing organizational learning speed, see Part VI of
this book.)

The Need for Context
We are watching the software development lifecycle get reinvented in real time.
Observability is at the center of that shift, because it’s both a tool that agents use and
a domain that agents are beginning to operate in. For decades, the cycle of writing,
deploying, observing, and fixing assumed a human at every step. In the AI era, that
assumption is being eroded simultaneously at multiple points in the cycle.
Coding agents write and modify code. Deployment pipelines automate the path
to production with decreasing human checkpoints. Observability agents monitor
running systems and surface findings. The role of the engineer is shifting from direct
execution toward direction: setting context, reviewing outputs, and making judgment
calls at the boundaries where automation is not yet reliable enough to be trusted
alone.
The pace of change in AI is fast—faster than most organizations’ capacity to adapt
(see Chapter 24). Tools and patterns that were clearly experimental six months ago
are now running in production today. What works is changing quickly, and the space
is full of genuine uncertainty about what the right architectures and workflows look
like.
What won’t change is the underlying principle: just like their human counterparts,
agents are only as good as the context they’re given. That is true for incident response,
for instrumentation quality, for alerting, for SLOs, and for everything else in the
observability space. The teams that will get the most out of AI in their observability
practice are the ones that invest seriously in building and maintaining that context
layer—the machine-readable equivalent of the mental model that senior engineers
carry and that has historically lived only in people’s heads.
The tooling will keep improving. The models will get better. But the context problem
is yours to solve.

Conclusion
Boris provides a salient look at how to navigate the fast-changing world of AI
with regard to observability. Observability has been, and continues to be, highly
dependent on context to reach the correct conclusions. Inevitably, how that context
is gathered and who holds it throughout an investigation will change. But we believe
the principles outlined in this chapter will continue to be true, even as the underlying
technology rapidly evolves.
In the next chapter, we’ll cover how to use SLOs for alerting and to trigger inves‐
tigations. As you’ll learn, SLOs are the best fit for alerting when working with
high-observability systems because they focus on the “who” and “what,” and leave the
observability tooling free to explain the “how” and “why.”
