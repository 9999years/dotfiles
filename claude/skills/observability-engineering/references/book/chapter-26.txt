                  The Business Case for Observability




In the AI era, our primary constraint has shifted from developing code to under‐
standing and validating it. To do this swiftly, easily, and consistently, you need to
connect developers to their code in production through a single, fast, efficient feed‐
back loop.
Production is where developer intent meets reality. Every deploy is a unique conflu‐
ence of infrastructure, software, user traffic, and time, which happens once and can
never be precisely repeated; observability is how we make sense of that intersection.
This chapter takes the next step: how to get funding support and protect the work
that turns feedback loops into sociotechnical change that persists.
Modern observability is rooted in infrastructure, and many leaders are still accus‐
tomed to managing it as a cost center. But observability for developers is one of the
most powerful levers for accelerating learning and converting engineering labor into
product delivery as efficiently as possible. If you’re a leader trying to do just that, this
chapter will help you make the business case.
In this chapter, we’ll talk about how to anchor your business case in the feedback
loop you want to accelerate, how to quantify value for your organization, and how to
frame observability in a way that survives annual planning cycles.

Identifying Your Priorities
Developing the business case for observability shouldn’t start with comparing vendor
feature lists. Instead, leadership alignment begins by identifying which feedback loop
is the primary bottleneck in your organization right now: the operational loop or the
developer learning loop.

By establishing shared intent, you ensure that your observability spend can translate
into measurable improvements that solve real organizational problems. You need a
sustainable business case aligned with your strategic priorities—whether that’s the
stability of your existing infrastructure or the speed of your development teams.
In this section, we’ll look at the complementary roles of the two loops and how to
decide which to prioritize.

Complementary Roles of the Two Feedback Loops
This isn’t a purely binary choice. Most organizations need both loops to function. The
goal is to get clear on which problems you’re trying to solve, because that tells you
where to put your energy.
Operational feedback loops are essential safety mechanisms. They monitor your
infrastructure, track overall system health, and ensure you meet your SLOs.
      Infrastructure (n.): The code you have to run, in order to run the code you want to
      run.
          —Charity Majors

Operational feedback loops are downstream, delayed, and threshold-based, driven by
customer complaints and system failures. By the time your operational loop fires,
users have already been impacted and the threat is sizable.
Operational loops are very important and you definitely need them. But they should
not be the only way (or the primary way!) that developers learn about their code in
production. They are guardians of stability, the last line of defense, protectors of the
last resort.
Developer learning loops let you track developer intent, verify user experience (UX),
and validate that your code is working as intended—and identify problems before
users notice anything is wrong. These loops move learning upstream toward preven‐
tion, iteration, and product quality.
This is the difference between prevention and cure, between learning continuously
and only learning from disasters.

Read the Fire Codes
For many organizations, there’s no clean choice between which loops to prioritize.
The reality is that many teams are fighting fires constantly while also being under
pressure to ship faster. On this point, we’ll borrow wisdom from one of our favorite
SREs at Honeycomb:
      It’s great that you’re so good at firefighting, but maybe it’s time to read the fire codes?
          —Fred Hebert

Many engineering orgs are proud of their firefighters—and they should be. Incident
response requires calm under pressure, sharp instincts, and a willingness to take
ownership when things go wrong. But when an organization’s identity is built around
firefighting, improvement becomes reactive by default. When it takes a crisis to learn
anything, all your capacity gets spent on reaction and recovery rather than progress.
“Reading the fire codes” is a metaphor for strengthening the upstream mechanisms.
The bigger leverage is in prevention: designing systems and practices that stop fires
from starting in the first place. That happens by building feedback loops that reduce
the frequency and severity of incidents, and learning loops that make every change
safer and more informative.

The Business Case for Operational Loops
The business case for operational loops is essentially the same as the traditional
business case for monitoring and logging. The business cares about system health,
and aggregate measures can often satisfy that goal.
The degree of observability in your system matters. Signs of poor or insufficient
observability include: customers finding your bugs before you do, small incidents
snowballing into major outages, and debugging by intuition that burns out your
senior engineers.
Regardless of your current observability level, the business case is the same. ROI is
demonstrated clearly and measurably through:

  • Improved uptime
  • Faster incident response
  • Cost savings from prevented outages
  • Reduced employee burnout

Operational loops are downstream and time-sensitive. They are triggered by alerts
(threshold-based or SLO violations) or by humans noticing problems (customer
reports, helpdesk tickets). Operational loops succeed when teams can quickly answer
three questions: what is happening, who is affected, and what will restore service
fastest?

The Operational Mandate
To understand the value of operational loops, look at how operational instability
affects your business. Signs you may be approaching a breaking point include:

  • Customers discover and report critical production bugs before you do.
  • Minor incidents take so long to detect and fix that they escalate into extended
    outages.
  • Your backlog of investigations keeps growing because new problems pile up
    faster than you can triage them.
  • Your teams spend more time on break/fix work than on building new features.
  • Customer satisfaction is low because of repeated performance issues that the
    support teams can’t verify, reproduce, or fix.
  • New features are delayed by weeks or months because engineers are tangled up in
    figuring out how services are interacting.
  • You hesitate to ship AI-generated diffs to production because nobody wrote them
    or really understands how they work.

This list is entirely about bugs, incidents, and performance issues. If these are your
biggest constraints, your operational feedback loops need work.
The business case for improving operational loops hinges on four things:
Higher incremental revenue
    Better uptime and performance directly translate to more revenue from
    improved code quality.

Cost savings from faster incident response
    This results in faster mean time to detect (MTTD) and mean time to repair
    (MTTR), quicker query response, faster bottleneck identification, reduced on-
    call time, and fewer rollbacks all reduce labor costs.
Cost savings from avoided incidents
    Finding problems before they become critical prevents outages from happening
    in the first place.
Cost savings from lower turnover
    Better tooling reduces burnout, alert fatigue, and on-call misery, which means

Other benefits may apply depending on implementation, but these four should be
universal for any business that adopts the practices in this book. Rather than getting
caught up in feature comparisons between vendors, focus on whether—and how
much—they actually improve business impact in these areas.
Operational feedback loops are a vital safety net for almost every organization. If
improving operational outcomes is your goal, then observability is your primary tool.
But because operational loops are essentially table stakes, let’s turn our attention to
the business case for developer learning loops, which is where the real leverage lies.

The Business Case for Developer Learning Loops
Developer learning loops are upstream from operational loops, and have compound‐
ing effects downstream. If developers only look at production after something breaks,
most of the learning is being lost.
Developer learning loops are about far more than just bugs and incidents. They are
about understanding the intersection of code, infrastructure, and users, and all that
this entails. Production is a very noisy place, and users are agents of chaos, so you
need to be able to ask exploratory questions and follow the trail, wherever it goes.
These investigations often start with open-ended questions, like “Am I building the
right product?”, “How are my users using it?”, and “What happened when I shipped
that change?”
Recall from Chapter 24 that we said observability is the sense-making layer for com‐
plex sociotechnical systems. It’s what closes open loops and turns slow, lossy feedback
into tight, accelerating ones. The questions that matter for reducing uncertainty are
crosscutting by nature: impact by cohort, outcomes by feature state, regressions by
request path, behavior under real load.
Given that, two things should become clear: (1) tooling capabilities matter a lot
here, and (2) this is a different kind of investment. With high-quality, granular

sustained pressure to the single greatest point of leverage in any software engineering

Unified observability unlocks developer learning in ways that the three pillars cannot.

The difference in outcomes is stark. The business case here hinges on how you
quantify the impact of developer learning. Before we get into the mandate, let’s look
at prior research we can build on.

Estimating the Value of Developer Learning
If “developer learning” feels squishy and hard to justify, recall the research of leading
organizations like DORA, SPACE, DX,1 GitHub, and others (see links in Chapters 23
and 24), that have been quantifying the measurable effects of developer productivity
for years.
When thinking about the business case for developer learning, notice how many tools
in your current budget already try to buy it indirectly: CI/CD, test infrastructure,
feature flags, experimentation platforms, progressive delivery, internal developer plat‐
forms. The developer tools category is enormous because the economics are real—
engineering time is expensive.
As the number of developer tools has grown, so has the theoretical ceiling for
engineering velocity. Yet very few organizations have come close to those theoretical
gains; most see only modest improvements. Why?

In our view, this is less mysterious than one might guess. Developer productivity is
a system, and many tools succeed weakly (if at all) because they don’t close the loop.
They improve one part of the system while leaving the feedback step weak. In the
absence of learning, all you have is activity. Some omnipresent examples include:
Chaos engineering
   If you’re injecting failures into your systems but can’t identify precisely where and
   how things broke, chaos engineering is just chaos. The shift to learning requires
   clear attribution: what degraded, where, and why?
Feature flags
    Flipping flags to expose features to subsets of users gives you control, but control
    without validation is a trust-fall activity—the control is illusory.
Progressive delivery
    Rolling out features gradually reduces risk, but only when regressions can be
    precisely identified by cohort and slice. Otherwise, you have a slower deployment
    pipeline and the same uncertainty.
Many modern engineering practices only succeed when they form a closed loop:
change → observe → act. Observability is what closes that loop and turns these
practices into fast, specific, actionable learning.
A credible business case doesn’t need to prove that developer time has value. It needs
to show how observability reduces wasted cycles, accelerates outcomes, and aids in
the value creation loop.

The Developer Mandate Has Two Halves
The business case for investing in observability to accelerate learning from develop‐
ment loops has two halves to measure:
External measures
    Outcomes experienced by customers
Internal measures
     Outcomes experienced by engineers and engineering-adjacent roles
Where you invest internally should reflect your business model, your competitive
position, and where your biggest opportunities lie. Let’s take them one at a time.

External measures: Product differentiators and customer experience
From an external perspective, observability plays a supporting role in every customer
interaction. This includes dependability, but also the quality of the product and the
experience of each user, as well as the ability to understand and respond to real users’

what they’re trying to accomplish, and where reality diverges from your assumptions.
Other terms pleasing to the ear of finance include gems like: “fully-loaded engineer‐

The decisions you make about how to instrument your code, or how to collect and
analyze your telemetry, are not operational decisions. These are product decisions.


                         The Cost of Not Paying Attention
  What gets missed when developers only look at production for bugs and incidents?
  Almost everything.
  Instagram grew out of an app called Burbn, a location-based check-in app. The
  founders observed that users were gravitating toward the photo-sharing feature, so
  they pivoted and doubled down on it.
  YouTube started off as a video dating site, but users seemed more interested in the
  videos than the dating. Twitter (now X) was originally a podcasting platform called
  Odeo. Slack emerged from a failed gaming company’s internal chat tool.
  It’s remarkable how often this pattern pops up. Some of the most interesting features
  and creative uses come out of watching what users do and following their lead. But if
  you can’t or don’t look, you will miss it.


The external half of the dual mandate is about being able to learn from customers and
evolve the product toward better outcomes—before your competitors do, and before
internal narratives harden into the wrong roadmap.

Internal measures: Velocity as a competitive advantage
Internal value is the part that leaders undervalue because it’s harder to put on a slide.
It doesn’t always show up as a clean “+1% revenue” headline. But it’s where the real
leverage lives, because it determines whether your organization can actually execute
and learn without drowning in its own complexity.




                                                The Business Case for Developer Learning Loops   |   473
Instrumentation and enablement around internal developer feedback loops—CI/CD,
deploys, test suites, agentic monitoring, specs, SLOs, developer sandboxes, visual
tests, progressive delivery, feature flags, and so forth—is the difference between ship‐
ping hundreds of times a day and shipping once a week. It’s the difference between
small and nimble teams that can self-serve their needs, and slow and lumbering
process overhead. It’s the difference between engineering labor converting efficiently
into outcomes or evaporating into rework, escalations, and task trackers.
And increasingly, it’s about which teams are going to survive the leap to AI-generated
code. Teams that struggle to complete a migration or understand a user session
in production are not equipped to validate nondeterministic agentic workflows in
production, much less what it will take for their code to cross over (as discussed in
Chapter 2).
The reason is simple: uncertainty is expensive. When engineers can’t see what their
code is doing in production, every change carries more risk. Organizations respond
with more coordination and risk mitigation. Uncertainty creates drag, and so do most
of the ways people respond to it. It’s how capable teams slow down without even
noticing.
You can’t hire your way out of a velocity problem caused by uncertainty. More bodies
don’t help, they just increase the coordination tax.
The way to break a velocity stalemate is by accelerating your ability to validate each
change in production. You need to build confidence and you need to build it fast.
That is the best way to efficiently convert engineering labor into outcomes: with fewer
regressions, less rework, and tighter learning loops.
The business case for developer learning can be assembled using internal velocity
metrics, external experience metrics, or both. The investment returns on external-
facing developer loops come from leaning into your product differentiators to
increase revenue; the investment returns on internal-facing developer loops come
from allowing each developer to accelerate and move with confidence.
In an era where AI is being used to accelerate the development process, your ability to
capture those gains and convert them into business results will depend on your ability
to validate each change and understand the impact it has on your customers.

learning as an investment.

Strategic Investment or Cost Center Optimization?
There’s a reason many organizations habitually manage observability as IT or cost
center technology: that’s how the industry got here. Monitoring and logging were
traditional operations tools, as discussed in Chapter 3, and many of our tools and
practices still reveal signs of their origins.


                         Cost Centers Versus Investments
  A cost center is a department or function within a company that incurs expenses but
  doesn’t directly generate revenue. It’s tracked as a cost to the business rather than a
  profit contributor. Classic examples include IT, human resources, legal, finance, facili‐
  ties, ops, and infrastructure. They’re necessary for the business to operate, but their
  value is indirect. They enable the revenue-generating parts of the org to function.
  The term often carries a slightly loaded connotation: calling something a cost center
  is sometimes a way of signaling that it is lesser-than or shouldn’t exist.
  An investment, on the other hand, is something you spend money on now in order
  to get more value back from later. The key distinction is the expectation of return. An
  investment is supposed to compound.
  The tricky part is that the return on many investments is delayed, indirect, or just
  hard to measure. If you can’t draw a straight line from “we spent X on observability
  tooling” to “we made Y more dollars,” the finance brain defaults to treating it as
  overhead.
  Engineers often find ROI estimates endlessly exasperating, because they seem fuzzy
  and imprecise—they are, but that’s not the point.
  The point is: framing matters. Cost centers get cut; investments get funded. The time
  you spend crafting estimates is time well spent.


“Is observability a cost center or an investment?” is a trick question. It’s actually both,
or it can be, because observability inherits the properties of the code you are observing.
Think about it:

  • The observability you use for infrastructure and operational outcomes is a cost
    center, because infrastructure itself is a cost center. You can’t make more money
    by spinning up extra pools of idle capacity, and you can’t make money by ladling
    on more monitoring checks than you actually need. Infrastructure is about
    operating as efficiently as you can.




                                              Strategic Investment or Cost Center Optimization?   |   475
  • Your revenue-generating application code is an investment; you might add new
    features, improve the recommendation engine, or make it faster and more inter‐
    active in order to boost sales and conversion rates. The instrumentation and
    telemetry you use are also an investment, if you are able to use better observabil‐
    ity tools to improve the product or UX in ways that generate more revenue than
    the marginal costs of those tools.
  • The code you use to deploy, test, validate in production, and so forth, is not
    revenue-generating code. However, engineering salaries are the largest single
    category of expenses for most technical product companies, and internal tooling
    and enablement can be a force multiplier on developer productivity as well as
    overall confidence and execution speed.

Observability and precision data are also dependencies for the coming AI revolution,
but no one knows how to price that yet.
In practice, managing all observability like a cost center doesn’t just reduce tool
spend—it degrades the information channel the organization depends on to detect,
diagnose, and improve its systems. This has many unfortunate consequences
downstream.
This is not to say that there are no effective techniques for constraining costs, or that
you shouldn’t try to limit your spending. (There are, and you should.) But it would be
short-sighted to manage all telemetry spend the same way. If you prevented develop‐
ers from gathering high-cardinality data because it was too expensive, and as a result
they don’t have enough specificity to identify outliers or improve performance, you
are only hurting yourselves.

Observability as Organizational Investment
Observability is the sense-making apparatus for sociotechnical systems with con‐
stant flux, dynamic components, nondeterministic agents, and countless emergent
behaviors.
It is the mechanism that connects cause and effect to create feedback loops. Observa‐
bility is what builds the bridge that connects developer intent to user impact. And
context-rich data and precision tooling are the primary safety mechanism for the
emerging domain of automated coding techniques like harness engineering.
Yeah, it’s kind of a big deal.
If observability has not historically been managed as an investment or a strategic
priority, what might that look like from an organizational perspective? Here is what
needs to change:

Reporting structure
    Infrastructure, operations, and IT excel at managing cost center technologies
    to a strict budget, which is why observability should not report to them. It
    should roll up to engineering leadership—your vice president of engineering,
    CTO, or sometimes chief product officer (CPO). Those roles typically have more
    experience managing technology as an investment, not just as overhead.
Team structure
   Observability teams should be staffed and run like platform engineering teams,
   not like infrastructure/ops. Follow the platform “Prime Directive”: build the
   components that are unique to the company, but shared across teams. Use design
   principles and user research, treating software engineers as their customers.
   Minimize cognitive overhead for developers. Make auto-instrumentation feel like
   magic. Make it easy to do the right thing and hard to do the wrong thing.5
Remember, the worst possible outcome isn’t picking the wrong tool; that’s recovera‐
ble. A worse outcome would be if you start pouring money, time, and engineering
cycles into a solution when you don’t yet have alignment around questions like “What
are we trying to solve?”, “What does success look like?”, and “What feedback loop are
we trying to improve?”

Conclusion
In this chapter, we showed how to build a business case around operational response
(firefighting) or developer learning (reading the fire codes). Each loop has different
success measures, and the business case should be built around those measures
instead of specific tools or features.
We also identified the management shifts required to unlock funding and manage
observability as an investment. Operational outcomes are cost-center optimizations
and belong under IT/ops, but developer learning loops are a strategic investment and
should live within engineering.
When you translate faster learning and validating into conservative estimates of
recovered capacity, reduced waste, and reduced risk, the case becomes fundable and
sustainable.
In the next chapter, we’ll cover some more practical diagnostics, as well as questions
you can use to figure out if your investments are paying off or not.
