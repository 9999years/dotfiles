Remember when we used to be able to predict the upcoming tech trends, the emerg‐
ing architectural evolutions, and what the software world would be like in five years?
I mean, we were never able to actually do so accurately, but it sure felt like we could.
The first edition of this book came out in 2022, which somehow feels like it was only
yesterday, yet it also feels like an entirely separate era of history.
Since the first edition came out, we’ve learned how to apply observability to mobile
and the web, we’ve seen the rise of agentic AI, we’ve dug into vendor relationships,
we’ve explored instrumenting regulated environments, and so much more. Every year
has seen the momentum around observability increase. It’s been so exciting to watch
it happen in real time!
But that momentum isn’t without a cost. The extraordinarily wide range of topics
in observability has made the second edition of this book far longer (and also far
better, in my opinion). It’s almost an entirely new book! From behind the scenes, I
saw all of the hard work that Charity, Liz, George, and Austin put into this book;
their efforts, along with those of numerous other contributors, have shaped this into
a book that could never have existed before now. I hope you appreciate the magic and
sweat and tears. I hope you enjoy reading the results. It turns out that while writing
about observability is already hard enough, knowing when to stop and cut the book
release is an impossible challenge. Especially when AI threatens to make everything
obsolete every two months. Well, not everything.
The hard-won knowledge about how complex sociotechnical systems work? That’s
never going away. The practitioners working to understand their systems? We’re still
here, and we’re not leaving. Software complexity has dramatically increased. So has
the growth of the tech industry. Remember when microservices and Kubernetes were
“unnecessarily complex”? Now that’s a three-pillar-era throwback. Throughout all
this growth, we’ve had to figure out how to build the tools and practices necessary to
keep our systems healthy. It’s hard work! It felt like we had finally figured it out before
AI arrived on the scene. Now, sometimes it feels like we’re starting all over again.


                                                                                       xxiii
Predicting what the world will look like in five years? I feel like I can barely predict
what next week will bring, but I can’t help but speculate. Candidly, here’s what I see
for the next five years in observability: the world is going to get even more intense,
and we’re going to have to keep up. How will we do that? I see a few key evolutions
taking place to enable this: lakehouse data architectures, contextual tooling, and
delivering value outside of engineering. These three things synergize with each other,
and the combination is incredibly powerful.
Observability has traditionally been most limited by the constraints of the data
engine backing the tooling, but lakehouse architectures eliminate most of those
constraints, enabling maximum cardinality, flexibility, compliance, and more. Mean‐
ingfully using telemetry data, and accessing it where you need it, has been the hardest
UX challenge of observability, but advances in contextual tooling are enabling new
possibilities for bringing the right data to the engineers (or agents) who need it, and
when they need it. Lastly, technological investments are increasingly cross-functional,
and so the value that is produced from engineering practices also needs to evolve and
become cross-functional. The days of buying tools for platform operators exclusively
and hiding them away from the rest of the company are coming to an end.
Above all, my prediction for the future is that it’s going to get weird. It’s already weird.
It’s going to get weirder. It’s going to change faster than we ever thought possible.
We’re going to have to rethink our prior assumptions; we’re going to have to break
our old mental models and build new ones. Our beliefs about software engineering
will change, and our techniques are going to stop working. Just like “open the
debugger and set a breakpoint” stopped working, those three pillars of observability
and those shiny, sparkly dashboards are on their deathbeds as we speak.
I find a lot of comfort in that swirling vortex of unknown unknowns, personally. The
reason for that is because it gives me that much more confidence in what will stay
the same: people. Yes, even in a world of AI, people are still here. We’re still going to
work together. We’re going to build new tools, new systems, and new methods. We’re
going to learn together. We’re going to dance and laugh. We’re going to knuckle down
and push ourselves to the next level. We’re going to reach ever upward, to the next
generation.
And while we can’t predict the future, we can prepare for it—starting by reading this
book. So please, find a comfortable spot to relax and enjoy the tools, techniques, and
insights this book offers. Together, let’s learn how to understand and observe our
systems of today and tomorrow.

                                                                          — Hazel Weakly

When we wrote the first edition of this book, the observability landscape was shifting,
and practices were advancing faster than we could collectively write them down. In
retrospect, we were chasing a moving target. It took three and a half years to write
and edit the book that was released in 2022.
In the four years since, the landscape and practices for observability have solidified
a great deal, which makes it easier to write about. However, life is never boring;
ChatGPT and GitHub’s Copilot were also released in 2022, kicking off an explosive
period of innovation that has yet to subside.
As we write this preface, in March of 2026, the entire software industry is going
through yet another unprecedented upheaval, a convulsive period of compressed
change. New AI development workflows are being hammered out in real time, for
durable code as well as disposable code. Harness engineering, agentic orchestration,
Phoenix architectures…every day seems to come with new terms and new concepts.
It feels overwhelming trying to keep up, and the only consolation is that everyone else
feels just as overwhelmed.
We’ve seen AI models leap generations, entirely new categories of tooling appear, and
workflows that felt cutting-edge become table stakes, all within one fiscal quarter.
This is what exponential change feels like from the inside: not a smooth trajectory,
but a series of jarring “wait—already?” moments. The hype trains are roaring, and it
can be difficult to pick trustworthy voices out from the ambulance chasers seeking to
cash in on our anxieties.
And yet, an emerging, ongoing punch line this year has had people dryly comment‐
ing each time we rediscover the principles we’ve known all along:
    “Oh, so we should have fast feedback loops?”
    “Tests are important, you say?”
    “Are you insinuating that we should talk to our own users? To each other?”



                                                                                      xxv
This time around, it has only taken us nine months to write and edit the book,
despite it being twice as long and almost entirely rewritten. Yet as we went into
final edits, reading the book front to back, it was already painful to read some of
the things we had written less than a year ago. A year ago, it was reasonable to be
skeptical about whether or not generated code would ever be as good as the median
developer could write, or how long it would take; now it is not reasonable. A year ago,
it was reasonable to bifurcate the space into disposable versus durable code; now that
division is blurring.
We have done our best to adjust for the shift in aperture, to acknowledge the ways
that our spectrum of possible futures has widened and bent in recent times.
But this is not a book about AI; it is a book about software development. And the
further we get, the more we learn, the clearer it becomes to us that software (and
people) are still the hard parts. The struggle is less about the truly novel aspects of
AI, such as wrangling nondeterminism, and more about the things that have always
been hard: connecting cause and effect, constructing coherent mental models, and
managing user expectations. Only now, the rate of change is revving up to 10 times,
100 times, 1,000 times as fast as ever before.
We take comfort in this. Observability itself has never felt like something flashy or
new to us—it has always felt like an intrinsic property of the software discipline,
derived from first principles, discoverable by anyone. We believe this still. And we
fiercely believe these first principles are more relevant and desperately needed by
practitioners than ever before.
This moment of compressed change will not last forever. Indeed, the one thing we
know about exponential change is that it must end, because all resources have limits.
As uncomfortable as it is, this change is also an unprecedented window of opportu‐
nity. After 20 years of DevOps, the overwhelming majority of software engineers
still don’t include production in their development workflow; at many places, they
aren’t even allowed to. The number of companies that have the right guardrails and
precision tools in place to validate AI-generated code in production is minuscule—in
the single digits.
Why? Because it’s hard. Building out feature flags, progressive deployment, and the
right instrumentation has been time-consuming and labor-intensive, and the old
ways worked well enough to get by, using human intuition as spackle and duct tape.
But AI breaks this stalemate. It enables key technological breakthroughs on the
supply side. And on the demand side, it provides the competitive pressure to change.
Engineers who have long agitated for better platform support and enablement should
receive this opening as the gift that it is.

How to Read This Book
We wrote this book with software engineers and technical decision-makers in mind,
but it should be relevant (and hopefully interesting) to anyone else who builds
software or works with those who do.
Part I is for all readers. It contains foundational concepts that we will draw on for
the rest of the book: what observability is, how to develop good software, and the
user experience you’re aiming for. Readers of the first edition might skip Chapter 3
(arguably, it was the point of the entire first edition). However, this part of the book
has been entirely overhauled to better frame the “observability” conversation in our
modern zeitgeist.
Parts II and III are written primarily for software engineers and observability practi‐
tioners. Every software engineer should understand how to instrument their code
and use the telemetry it emits. The chapters in this section cover the technical basics
that all modern software engineers should be familiar with.
Part IV is for engineers who want to go beyond the surface. You can think of these
as the “show your work” chapters of the book. We dive deep into the internals and
plumbing that enable the instrumentation and analysis demonstrated in Parts II and

suited to working with backend systems. Part V shows how the same principles can
be applied to other domains.
Part VI is for technical decision-makers. Its chapters are aligned with typical org

organization, then this section is for you.

                                                                                 PART I
                       Introduction to Observability




This first segment lays the foundations that the rest of the book builds on.
You’ll learn what observability actually means in the context of software develop‐
ment, how AI changes that picture, and how to gauge how observable your system
really is. You’ll also learn what investigative capabilities a high degree of observability
unlocks, and how to test whether your system actually has them.
Chapter 1 traces where the term “observability” came from, how it was adapted for
software systems, and what it actually means to have an observable system. It also
looks at how to think about observability in the age of AI-assisted development and
agentic applications.
Chapter 2 is about how code crosses over from development into production, and
what it takes to do that well. It describes the full “test in prod” toolkit—instrumenta‐
tion, feature flags, canaries, automated rollbacks—as a flywheel where each practice
enables the next. And it makes the case that all of it depends on one thing first:
observability that is rich and precise enough to actually trust.
Chapter 3 is a retrospective on the evolution of observability, written by coauthor
Charity Majors from her own perspective. It focuses on the lessons that matter
most now that “observability” has become ubiquitous, and closes with the functional
requirements that anchor the concepts introduced throughout this part of the book.
In Part II, we’ll introduce you to instrumentation principles and OpenTelemetry.
