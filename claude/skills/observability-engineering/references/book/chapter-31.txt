Ralph Waldo Emerson once wrote, “language is fossil poetry.”1 Social scientists and
philosophers have long observed that language itself both shapes (and is shaped
by) society. Wherever two or more people gather, a language is sure to be either
developed or refined. Culture itself is the crucible of language—the way we interact
and relate to one another.
An observability engineering team grappling with an instrumentation strategy for
their system faces similar burdens. Code ages like the value of a new car driven off the
lot: it depreciates the moment it’s written. While software (as a whole) may provide
value, code itself contains a burden of maintenance and care that requires careful
planning, strategy, and gardening in order to avoid overwhelming operators as their
system scales. If that describes the kind of problems that keep you up at night, then
you’re in the right place.
Observability teams may take different forms depending on the size, scale, and remit
of an organization. In smaller orgs, “observability team” may be a role that’s foisted
upon a site reliability engineer. Larger organizations may have multiple teams, each
responsible for a certain set of logical systems. Your bosses may be in information
technology, or they might be in software engineering. Your job may look more like
vendor management than anything else. There’s a wide spectrum of practices.
But in general, we believe that an observability team should own instrumentation at
a pretty wide level. That includes instrumentation code and frameworks, telemetry

What Observability Teams Need To Know
As previously mentioned, language is a construct constantly evolving based on social
interactions. Instrumentation is very similar: it’s a construct that is created from the
evolving operation of software. If we had perfect visibility into what our code was
doing without logging, metrics, profiling, or other technology, then we wouldn’t need
to worry about instrumentation.
For many years, we’ve seen various tabulae rasae for the semantics of software appear.
Unfortunately, they often wind up as being overfit for a particular domain. For
example, the .NET and Microsoft Stack have long enjoyed a vertical integration
between runtime and operating system that allowed Microsoft to produce a more or
less consistent dialect of system state metadata, but it generalized poorly (and then
Microsoft decided to embrace Linux and subsequently threw away half of it).
Similarly, Java has its own dialect and semantics that are integrated up and down
the stack—that is, until you add in a React frontend or a mobile app written in
Swift. Then you’re back to square one. *nix systems in general have a somewhat
consistent and self-referential dialect of telemetry, but like most things involving *nix,
the learning curve can be more of a learning cliff.
Ultimately, the north star for an observability team is being able to define and
manage the constructed language for their organization. That requires an ability to
play archeologist with the terms and definitions that have slowly ossified into the
informal language of software engineering at a company, while making a bridge to
the lingua franca of the wider world. Your role as part of an observability team isn’t
just handing out instrumentation and documentation. You must also provide the
framework that individual contributors and leaders across teams, business units, and
companies use to communicate intent and action. That’s a big responsibility and role.

Semantic Conventions
The most valuable part of OpenTelemetry (OTel) for an observability team is in its
semantic conventions. Semconv—as they’re sometimes colloquially referred to—are
a collaborative effort to define standard human definitions for common concepts in
software, hardware, and telemetry itself.
A subtle goal of semconv is to make telemetry a trainable skill, rather than something
that must be learned at the knee of more experienced developers at each new job.
Because semconv are universal and portable across languages, clouds, and runtimes,
they work more as a dialect than as an encyclopedia. You shouldn’t necessarily
need to memorize them, but knowing how the structure works helps you interpret
unfamiliar conventions.
Semantic conventions are a shared vocabulary and grammar for observability meta‐
data. The vocabulary is the set of names for attributes, metrics, spans, events, and so
forth—things like http.request.method, db.system, exception.type, GET <route> .
The grammar is how those names are organized into reusable patterns: dot-separated
namespaces, families like http.request.header.<key>, requirement and maturity
levels, and stability.
That design offers scalability: you can reasonably infer other domain shapes from
existing ones, making discovery and mapping easier. This makes observability a more
teachable practice. Rather than needing to remember the details and minutiae of
each individual service, once an engineer learns the basic namespaces, they’re able to
trace through a service call chain easily. Most of the heavy semantic lifting is covered
by two heuristics: domains are namespaced in order of specificity, left to right, and
actors are defined by client/server or producer/consumer.
The other advantage of semantic conventions is that you don’t have to do all of
the work yourself. The OTel project defines a significant number of domains and pro‐
vides instrumentation for them, along with tooling such as Weaver, which allows you
to create and define your own domains. That domain definition and promulgation is
one of the most important aspects of instrumentation design for observability teams,
so let’s focus on that next.

Telemetry Schemas in Practice
The benefits of API contracts and versioning are well-known: they improve the safety
of deployments, provide stable integration points for consumers, and allow teams to
more easily scale. So, why not treat telemetry the same way? Telemetry schemas are a
concept that brings the best practices behind modern API design to the observability
space by providing a textual representation of the telemetry data a service emits and
providing tooling for the discovery, linting, creation, and code generation of those
schemas.


                                                  What Observability Teams Need To Know   |   559
As of this writing, introducing a telemetry schema is not a turnkey process. But, the
building blocks exist for you to adapt and implement. In lieu of a step-by-step user
guide, let’s discuss the benefits of schemas and how to write new ones.
Schemas provide a single source of truth for the telemetry emitted by an application
or service. This is beneficial for developers and SREs alike. Instead of a potpourri of
fields and subtly duplicated data, users can refer to a single document that lives near
the code. That discoverability has myriad applications as well: tools can leverage these
schemas for easier access to end-user discoverability, auditing becomes easier, and AI
assistants can ingest these documents into their context for safer editing or agentic
SRE practices.
In addition, these schemas allow for both compile time and runtime verification
of telemetry. Builds can be failed or flagged when attributes or signals that aren’t
specified appear in the code, and pipelines can be created that drop mystery attributes
from generated data.
Finally, schemas allow for safe evolution of data. Pipeline processors can use schemas
to update or modify telemetry data in flight, normalizing data before it’s ingested and
exposed to alerting rules or dashboards.
Writing new schemas is an ideal way to move toward your north star of normalizing
the constructed language of observability in your organization. Here are a few tips:
Start from the “why.”
    Base your schema on the questions that users are likely to ask, while also sup‐
    porting the requisite variety of fields and attributes to answer emergent questions
    about system behavior. Make the common questions cheap, reliable, and efficient
    to answer.
Define your namespace.
    Identify the shared business attributes that are truly consistent across your orga‐
    nization and place them at the right level of a namespace.
      For instance, if a transaction ID is truly shared across the organization (in
      multiple systems—line-of-business applications, billing systems of record, etc.),
      then this should be expressed at a higher level than a system-specific or service-
      specific ID (acme.transaction.id versus acme.serviceName.transaction.id).
      The reason is that you don’t want to make your grammar confusing to users who
      are working outside of their normal domain. A business analyst may want to
      answer questions like “Based on annual revenue, which customers that have filed
      support tickets in the last quarter have had the worst API performance?” If you
      intermix identifiers, then it’s probable that some data will be incorrectly included
      or excluded. This is more important than ever with the rise of AI assistants
      throughout organizations; AI will pattern-match very strongly, and may not have


560   |   Chapter 31: Instrumentation for Observability Teams
    the sophistication required to realize why these values are different (or that they
    are at all).
Abstract at the right level: actors and roles over protocol details.
    While you should expose protocol information to your telemetry, the relation‐
    ship between services should remain decoupled. This is the rationale behind the
    client/server and consumer/producer delineation in OTel, and you should mirror
    that. Transports can change, after all.
DRY.
   Shared attribute keys should be shared across signal types. If the same attribute
   appears on different signals, then the values for that attribute should also be con‐
   sistent. A good example is our aforementioned transaction.id. That attribute
   contains extremely high-cardinality values, and it’s not recommended to have
   high-cardinality fields on time series metrics.
    Instead, you can layer attributes between signals (see Chapter 7) that improve
    your ability to navigate from one to another while also maintaining limits on
    cardinality. In this case, you may wish to add a customer.region (or tier, etc.)
    that has lower cardinality and enables you to preserve some semantic customer
    metadata on time series, and reserve higher-cardinality attributes on signals that
    are better-suited for them.


                         Identifiers and Security Teams
 In regulated environments, there are a variety of identifiers that raise the compliance
 level of data by their existence (e.g., internal customer identifiers). That presents
 a challenge to the observability team: security thrives off of isolation, compliance
 thrives off of evidence, but observability thrives off of correlation.
 In cases where useful identifiers would trigger compliance or security guidelines, you
 should consider one-way hashes of sensitive data. For example, an internal customer
 identifier can be anonymized in such a way that it can’t be reversed into its original
 format. As long as the hash method is consistent across your organization, you can
 still gain the benefits of correlation without incurring the oversight of security and
 compliance.


One critique of telemetry schemas is that they appear to be a very heavyweight
solution, especially for smaller organizations. That’s a reasonable critique, but it
speaks more to the frustration and conflict present in the misaligned incentives for
observability teams and development teams in organizations, rather than an inherent
flaw.

Dev teams often don’t see the same constraints as observability teams because their
scope is more limited. They may only have to care about a single service, or a small
group of them, whereas observability teams are exposed to the entire firehose of
data (and are often scored on keeping the costs of that firehose controlled). You
can’t necessarily browbeat other teams to take those concerns seriously, either. Direct
accounting of costs is a strategy, but it’s one that usually engenders resentment and
poor observability outcomes. So then, how should an observability team create the
right incentives to encourage desired behaviors?

Tooling, Migrations, and Paved Paths
Aligning development, operations, business, and observability teams on telemetry
data isn’t just publishing a wiki page about “best practices” and calling it a day. Nor is
it dropping in some sort of monitoring agent and giving people a bunch of prebuilt
dashboards.
We’ve seen many well-meaning DevOps teams try to “bring in observability” by
spending months carefully analyzing each infrastructure and service dependency,
rolling out agent configurations and dashboards for those services, presenting them
to development teams as a fait accompli, and then having them collect dust because
the data being collected isn’t relevant to the day-to-day work of any of the aforemen‐
tioned groups.
Recall that your north star is to build a shared language for the organization and
to adopt the techniques that society uses to promulgate language: education, diction‐
aries, rites, and repetition (namely, culture). In a more practical sense, you need to
build tools and frameworks that make it easy for users to adopt the language that
you’re building, create repeatable and accessible training opportunities for users as
they adopt it, embed touchpoints for your instrumentation practice and strategy into
your SDLC, and ingrain these into your processes.
Let’s look at each of these.

Tools and Frameworks
OTel is designed to be extensible and interoperable with existing telemetry systems,
cloud environments, and SDLC tooling. When considering how to build your own
tools, keep in mind two basic things:

 1. Don’t wrap the API, but you can extend it or build indirection around it. OTel
    is intended to be a universal API, with universal idioms. Wrapping the API and
    changing the names of method calls for consistency with existing telemetry suites
    or preferences is a distinct antipattern. You can extend the API—e.g., writing




562   |   Chapter 31: Instrumentation for Observability Teams
    decorators that bundle multiple calls into one (think starting a span and updating
    a bundle of metrics at the same time)—but avoid changing it entirely.
    You can also choose to bundle and reexport methods from the upstream library.
    For example, you can create a single indirect package that bundles together OTel
    along with dozens of other existing instrumentation APIs. Doing that enables
    you to immediately migrate many services without breaking callers while rewir‐
    ing them behind the scenes.
 2. Hide the right complexity from users. The OTel SDK can be highly verbose, with
    what feels like a mountain of boilerplate and customization options. Most of this
    complexity can be distilled into a one-liner that you add to a service framework.
    Current work in the space is aiming to make this even more straightforward,
    with a configuration file–based approach.

The single thing that trips up most development teams is context propagation
between services. Solving this problem is a perfect opportunity to start as you
plan to keep going, by embedding OTel into your remote procedure call or service
framework with a basic telemetry schema, and ensuring that this framework handles
service-to-service context propagation. If adopting OTel is as simple as picking up a
new version of an internal library, teams will be far more likely to adopt it.
On the backside of telemetry generation, you’ll want to focus on managing and
understanding data volume and data quality. The OpenTelemetry Collector and
tooling like Weaver are crucial here. The Collector is wildly configurable, but also
highly customizable. Rather than trying to handle everything in config, we suggest
writing custom extensions (it’s just a little Go) and building your own collector
distribution using the OpenTelemetry Collector Builder (OCB). That choice gives
you significantly more flexibility in handling unique filtering, sampling, or transfor‐
mation scenarios, as well as reduced resource usage and reduced security attack
surface. Weaver can be integrated into your build or deployment pipelines to live
check emitted telemetry data against registered schemas, or even added as a hook for
local build/test scenarios.
AI can also be an ally here. Explore AI-assisted remediation for existing services by
capturing telemetry from preproduction environments, feeding it through Weaver,
and then handing the code and Weaver outputs to an AI agent to suggest fixes for. If
you’re feeling adventurous (and have great tests), you can even have the agent go off
and make the PRs for you.
For the enterprise users, Weaver offers additional benefits. While it’s good hygiene to
ensure that your telemetry matches a schema, regulated industries will often need a
compliance review of code or outputs in order to verify that they’re not leaking sensi‐
tive data. Gathering evidence for the compliance team and auditors using Weaver in
CI can automate a tedious process.

Paved Paths and Special Hats
Too often, we see OTel (and observability) initiatives in general meet a staggering
shrug of indifference from users. You can lead a developer to water, but you can’t
make them drink. That shouldn’t be terribly surprising. Observability can feel like
a chore, even at the best of times, and it’s little wonder why the industry seems to
gravitate toward “easy buttons” and magic agents that promise to do all the work for
you (or, these days, magic AI agents that will understand and solve your problems for
you).
A possibly persuasive story comes from the early days of distributed tracing at
Google. One of the engineers responsible for Dapper (Google’s internal tracing tool)
was wondering how to get more users. Dapper had been rolled out and was collect‐
ing traces from a variety of services (search, shopping, etc.). A guerrilla marketing
approach of “telling people to use it” had stalled.
The engineer’s mentor suggested figuring out where people already were: what tools,
screens, and services were they using to understand performance? Figure that out,
then the path should become clear.
The Dapper team interviewed and analyzed engineer behavior, and ultimately added
a single link to the monitoring graphs that teams would regularly consult when they
were paged that would lead into Dapper. Usage doubled and then tripled, almost
overnight. Engineers who discovered Dapper would tell their colleagues on other
teams about it. Before long, distributed tracing had become a core tool in production
monitoring and observability at Google.
Your engineering teams probably have similar places they congregate: existing dash‐
boards, alerts, wiki pages, and so on. Identify those, understand their pain points,
pave a path to your observability data and OTel solutions, and let the magic happen.
You’re not going to win over everyone immediately, but you have to start somewhere.
Make adoption as easy as possible by building tools, as previously mentioned. A little
upfront work will pay dividends over time, especially with recalcitrant teams. The
“right way” and the “fastest way” should be the same way. One tactic we’ve seen in
successful organizational adoption is compiling and promulgating OTel wins—keep a
brag book of where your observability efforts are helping and make sure management
sees them. Make stickers, t-shirts, or little charms to incentivize adoption and usage.
Get creative!
Finally, make adoption safe. Leverage Collector pipelines to dual-send data to cheap
blob storage as well as telemetry databases—especially when doing migrations or
changing sampling rules—to compare old versus new, and have confidence that
you’re not dropping anything. Add rules (either through the transform processor,
schemas, or other mechanisms) to avoid cardinality explosions by dropping high-
cardinality fields on specific signal types like metrics. Ship default pipelines for local


564   |   Chapter 31: Instrumentation for Observability Teams
development to make it easy for devs to understand what telemetry is being emitted,
and centralize the management of pipeline configurations so it’s easy to tell why some
data is being dropped.
The last tip for adoption is especially important for sampling. One of the biggest
frustration moments for developers is when they go looking for data that they think
should exist, but doesn’t. Dual-send can help this by providing a fallback when data
is sampled out. But you can also help by doing more education about sampling,
its benefits, and its drawbacks. Make sure sampling rates are easy to understand
and avoid overly complex sampling strategies at first. It’s generally better to get “too
much” data when starting out and then walk it back as you build confidence in your
tools.
To wrap up, it can be helpful to think of yourself as an observability guide when roll‐
ing out instrumentation and OTel as an observability team. You’re not necessarily the
one who’s going to stand at the summit and get your picture taken, but you’re going
to try really hard to make sure nobody who’s coming with you gets themselves killed.
OTel gives you the framework to create safe, reliable, and repeatable instrumentation
paths—and as it matures, it’ll become easier to do so. It gives you the grammar and
vocabulary that you and your organization need as a floor to scaffold your own
concepts on top of. It provides tools to help manage and constrain the volume of
telemetry generated by modern, cloud native software, and it can help you avoid
common pitfalls if you use the features it offers. Learn it, leverage it, maybe even
love it, but don’t dismiss it—it’s a wave that you can surf to accelerate observability
adoption at your organization.

Instrumentation in Highly Secure Environments
In highly secure environments, two of the chief concerns about instrumentation
are compliance and security. While both are immensely complex topics, from the
perspective of observability, we’re going to think of compliance as being largely about
the evidence one provides, and security as being largely about the isolation one
guarantees.
Unfortunately, that means we quickly run into a tricky situation: observability’s
superpower is data correlation. Done naively, that very superpower can easily com‐
promise security isolation guarantees and stymie compliance evidence collection.
However, if we approach observability as a shared language between the entire enter‐
prise, it becomes possible to facilitate compliance without compromising security.
Given how these two concerns make implementing observability much more dif‐
ficult, it might be surprising to know that it’s still very similar to implementing
observability in any enterprise. One main difference comes from how high the bar is
for implementing security capabilities; many “best practices” become requirements.


                                              Instrumentation in Highly Secure Environments   |   565
Another significant difference is how heavy the burden of proof is. Do you have end-
to-end encryption? Do you secure customer data properly? Is your OTel Collector
using FIPS 140-2 encryption? Lovely! You’ll need evidence for all of that. In addition,
much of the success here relies on properly communicating with and designing
incentives for various teams: leadership, engineering, product, architecture, and every
other function need to be on the same page.
If you thought it was hard to convince stakeholders to work with you to implement
observability, convincing them when they’re in an organization that has 10 times the
compliance-related busywork on their plate is another story entirely.
Many organizations in this situation fall into the trap of only being able to make pro‐
gress on “mandatory” items; unfortunately, observability is almost impossible to make
mandatory. Success here often requires designing incentives in a way that everyone
involved looks good when they help you; they’ll often want the observability, but they
need to justify it against a massive pile of mandatory work. Help them do that.
This is one reason why it makes sense to talk about facilitating compliance evidence
collection with observability.2

Use Compliance Tiers to Your Advantage
Luckily, there’s another component to highly secure environments that can make
observability easier to implement: very rarely does every environment need every
compliance control.
Nobody wants to add unnecessary work to their plate, so it’s common to encounter
multiple tiers of compliance, multiple tiers of regulation, and a graduated approach
to environments. Use this to your advantage and provide seamless ways to “graduate”
services from one level of compliance to another. While startups might recoil at the
thought of a 12-month process to get an application “in a production environment,”
this is the norm for regulated industries.
That said, just because a service is not “in production” doesn’t mean they’re not
deploying code. Often, services are built out in local environments, and then in
development, testing, staging, and finally production. Importantly, every level has
more stringent requirements. A common mistake might be to shift implementation
burden to the development teams by providing a “production ready” observability
framework.
Rather than requiring development teams to do months of busywork in order to
“just get some data” locally, enable them to experiment as rapidly as possible given

A Minimal Set of Capabilities for Observability Teams
If we’re facilitating compliance and security concerns, the following are a good start‐
ing point of capabilities to think about:
Vendor management
   You’ll have many requirements from your regulators. They’ll want to know that
   your vendors are capable, and you’ll need some strong business agreements and
   financial incentives to ensure your vendor relationships are mutually beneficial.
   Be prepared to pay for what you’re asking for.
Encryption (end to end, at rest, and more)
    End to end as a requirement is more thorough than you might expect. You need
    mTLS between every component, even services in the same trusted boundary. If
    it talks over the network, it needs mTLS, period. This is a strong reason to prefer
    sockets for intraservice communication when possible.
    While mTLS takes care of most end-to-end encryption, encrypting the data at
    rest is also important. Depending on your requirements, encrypted hard drives
    may or may not be sufficient.
Multilayer data analysis
   This is the hardest component. You have to prove you’re satisfying all your
   regulatory requirements with every byte of data you send over the network to
   anywhere. Merely satisfying it isn’t enough; you have to show the work too.
    For example, many data regulations require that only minimally relevant data
    is sent to locations that customers explicitly authorize. You will need to be
    very careful to not accidentally send identifying data in telemetry. The superpow‐
    ers of observability will be working against you here: correlation is a strength
    of observability, but it means that it becomes extremely easy to accidentally
    send telemetry that is “safe” locally, but globally can be constructed into de-
    anonymized and identifiable data.
You’ll notice a pattern: all of the aforementioned capabilities are invisible. No
customer-facing roadmap is going to prioritize developing these capabilities, and
even observability teams will struggle to demonstrate value when implementing
them. Consequently, it becomes critical that they can be implemented as quickly and
efficiently as possible.

Valuable Capabilities in a Regulated Environment
Because of this, the following capabilities become quite valuable for operating in
a regulated environment that it’s almost infeasible to attempt observability without
them:
Chargeback
   This becomes very useful for ensuring that the observability team is suitably
   staffed and that the value of it can be shown in the company. Without that,
   observability efforts might die out from lack of buy-in.
      This can also be viewed as building cost and operational concerns into the
      “language” of the business. Business-intelligence shaped concerns are often hand-
      in-hand with regulatory requirements, especially regulations that have language
      around undue burden.
Schema control
    You can do multilayer data analysis without this, but it’s vastly easier to be able to
    have multiple levels of confidential data when you can check things automatically
    against a schema. Schemas also make it easier to prevent global correlation from
    compromising regulated anonymity of client data. In other words, judicious use
    of the OTel Weaver project can be the difference between needing to roll out an
    observability deployment per application versus one per environment. It can make
    or break a team’s ability to be allowed debugging information at all.
Data transport through proxies you control
   You don’t strictly need this, but runtime scanning of data for compliance and
   security reasons is required in almost every regulated environment. Doing it
   without proxies is possible, but needlessly painful.
      Sufficiently powerful proxies are key for enabling policy agents to become an
      effective tool for enforcing company-wide data controls. They’re exceptionally
      useful for providing evidence on otherwise hard-to-prove requirements such as
      what data is allowed to be sent to a third-party vendor.
Tokenization and data anonymization
    Tokenization enables you to do something very powerful: it can help prevent
    locally safe data from being globally correlated. If the data can’t be anonymized
    because it’s encrypted, you don’t have to worry about identification in the same
    way.
      Tokenization has another strong benefit: it allows you to do side channel correla‐
      tion outside of your tooling. What might otherwise be too sensitive or verbose to
      put in observability data—such as AI chat logs or database queries—can be made
      useful when combined with a technique such as tokenization.

    As mentioned earlier, there are benefits to correlation of anonymized identifiers.
    Using safe, one-way, hash functions that are consistent across the organization
    provides a level of functionality while minimizing data exposure risk.
Authorization and authentication
   Your data handling needs to have certain guarantees around it being handled
   by agents that are allowed to do so. This is significantly easier to achieve if
   your mTLS utilizes short-term credentials, attested identities, and granular per‐
   missions.
In addition, many regulatory requirements deal with “nontechnical” aspects of run‐
ning a technical organization. You will encounter a need for documenting and exe‐
cuting long-term support plans. You will also need to develop operating models
for migrations. Best practice guidelines evolve into blueprints and frameworks.
Documentation graduates into requirements around teaching courses and internal
support SLAs. Code scanning and schema validation become something that needs to
happen at development, in CI, during deployment, and throughout the lifecycle of an
application operating at runtime.

This Is Hard Work, But Work Worth Doing
As an observability team, it can feel overwhelming to think about all of these
regulatory requirements, and it can also feel like you’re doing all the security and
compliance work “in addition to” or “instead of ” the value-delivering work of observ‐
ability. However, partnering proactively with security and compliance teams will pay
dividends down the road in establishing trust and unlocking the roadblocks for
engineering teams.
After all, if engineering teams aren’t allowed to instrument their code effectively,
nothing else about observability will end up mattering.
Just like spoken human languages, the power of observability really shines through
when everyone is on the same page and communicating effectively with each other.
Giving them that language and the tools with which to express it safely, securely,
and compliantly, is the secret to delivering modern engineering capabilities in highly
secure environments.

Conclusion
The role of an observability team is ultimately not to claim the success of an
observability program, but to enable that success for the entire organization. That
makes defining success harder; data volume and dashboard count are a lot easier to
measure.

bridges shrink to days. Teams communicate expectations in SLOs, and they’re echoed

OTel provides a level foundation for you to build rich, business-specific context

that your engineers already are. At the end of the day you’ll know you’re succeeding
when the language of observability becomes part of the engineering and business
