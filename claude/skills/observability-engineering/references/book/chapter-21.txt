     Observability for Large Language Models




Artificial intelligence has been a revolution over the past two years. AI and machine
learning have been powering both transformative consumer digital experiences (such
as recommendation engines, search engines, and spam protection) as well as business
workflows (such as translation and logistics) for decades. However, the introduction
of LLMs has ushered in a new era of AI-powered applications and tools. Organiza‐
tions have eagerly embraced the potential of LLMs for programming assistance,
natural language interfaces to data, new categories of search products, support bots,
and more.
While LLM applications often impress through a small up-front investment to prove
a concept, the real work happens after the demo. LLMs are unlike most traditional
software workloads, and the unpredictability inherent in their design and use leads
to a fundamental challenge: users and engineers alike expect predictability in the
outputs of a software system, but LLMs are fundamentally nondeterministic. How
can we square this circle?
In this chapter, we’ll dive into the realm of observability for this new generation
of LLM-powered applications. You’ll learn about how to continuously improve the
performance and user experience (UX) of these applications through evaluations.
Beyond the theory, we’ll use case studies from two different LLM-powered Honey‐
comb products—Query Assistant and Canvas—to demonstrate practical applications
of what we’re discussing. We’ll introduce each of these products as we go through the
chapter.

Why Observability Matters for LLMs
LLMs represent a step change in the capability and accessibility of machine learning
models for both organizations and developers. Traditionally, AI has been used to
solve various problems for users where there was no one “right answer”—like recom‐
mending a movie based on your taste, or determining if an email is legitimate—but
usually at a great cost. LLMs have changed this calculus dramatically by lowering the
barrier of entry to “anyone with a credit card” simply by issuing prompts to a model
via an API.
Traditional observability and monitoring approaches are not suitable for understand‐
ing these types of applications, though. While helpful at establishing a high-level
signal or monitoring cost, traditional approaches don’t give any insight into behavior
that leads to an undesirable outcome. The primary reason? Opacity. You can’t connect
a debugger to an LLM to understand why it gave a specific output for a given
input; you can’t even guarantee that it will produce that output again, irrespective
of how many times you give it the same input. While there are parameters—such
as temperature and top_p—that control the distribution of outputs, they do not yet
guarantee repeatability. Confounding our attempts at sense-making, the spectrum of
natural language inputs is an impossibly broad space. Your users will invariably find
the most interesting ways imaginable to ask for the same thing—this assumes, of
course, that your users are also not trying their darndest to make your LLM say or do
something funny.
In addition to all of this, LLMs are advancing at a breakneck pace. In 2025 alone,
models leapfrogged their capabilities within a year thanks to improved tooling, har‐
nesses, and advances in techniques like tool calling. Traditional methods of iterative
design and development can’t keep up with this manic pace. Even in a future world
where the rate of progress slows, it’s still beneficial to iterate rapidly when working
with LLM applications. Adjusting model behavior is often a question of adjusting
prompts (natural language instructions given to an LLM) rather than pushing deter‐
ministic code changes. Achieving greater accuracy, reliability, or task completion rates
often involves both.
This model stands in stark contrast to the conventional wisdom for understanding
the UX of web applications. Popular models of quantifying this—such as Apdex or
other derived measures—primarily use API or endpoint speed as a proxy metric. In
2006, Greg Linden presented that Amazon’s latency experiments found substantial
revenue drops for every 100 milliseconds of added page delay. Similarly, in the same
year, Google found that a half second of latency resulted in a 20% drop in traffic for
search!
This does not hold true for AI applications—while measurements such as “time to
first token” can be valuable, fast (but incorrect) responses may not drive satisfaction.

In addition, in more complex agentic applications, such as research assistants or code
reviewers, failure may not be a valuable signal in and of itself. An agent may fail to
fetch a web page for many reasons, or pass incorrect parameters to a remote API. In
these cases, it’s less important that the discrete operation failed and more important
to understand if the overall task completed. In addition to these, AI applications
introduce a very discrete cost component. GPU time is valuable, and is priced accord‐
ingly. Understanding the trade-offs between token usage and user satisfaction is of
paramount importance to not just the business, but also engineers.
Opacity of system internals, rapid technological advancement, and poor proxies for
user experience in telemetry may not be completely unique to LLM applications,
admittedly. It’s ironic in some ways that these three points mirror the basic tenets of
observability, as applied to the emergent behavior of a distributed system. We expect
that our systems will fail, we expect that other services are untrustworthy, and we
know that everything has a price. The answer in both cases is the same: we need
instrumentation and telemetry that describe the system behavior. This is what makes
modern systems interpretable, knowable, and improvable at scale. But what should
we test? We need some sort of yardstick to measure our system performance against.
For this, we turn to evaluations.

Using Evaluations for LLM Reliability
Evaluations (evals for short) are a foundational component of building reliable LLM
applications. While they bear some resemblance to unit or integration tests—in that
they have an expected output for a given input—they introduce a level of indetermi‐
nate expectation that sets them apart. In short, an eval can have three results: pass,
fail, and maybe.
The power of evaluations is that you can blend them together and build them into
your observability program in order to achieve reliable, helpful, and useful LLM
applications. For example, at Honeycomb, we use a set of evals as a key input to the
development of our Canvas product. Canvas is an AI chat experience, where users
can ask open-ended questions that the model answers by calling tools allowing it to
query telemetry data. Let’s walk through these evals in order to explain how they
work.
We have two sets of evaluations: question evals and task evals. The first are a series
of questions that involve extracting factual items from a set of golden data. Golden
data is hand-crafted (or otherwise representative) input data for a task. In the case of
Canvas, it’s a variety of query results from Honeycomb. We know exactly what is in
this data, and we evaluate the model’s ability to extract specific facts, such as “at what
time did the most requests occur” or “which category of errors was most prevalent.”
These questions are more precisely known as the task function of an evaluation—it’s
what generates the output that would be presented to a user or passed back into

an agent loop. Finally, we have a scoring function that is used to grade the model’s
response. For our purposes, we use a simple string-matching heuristic—we know the
actual values present in the golden data, and we simply verify if the model presented
this known answer.
These question evals are paired with another set that we call “task evals” (see Fig‐
ure 21-1). These differ in that they do not have a simple scoring function; they are
fuzzy, subjective tasks based on our internal production monitoring. Rather than
asking for something with a single answer, we ask the models to perform investi‐
gations, determine the root cause of a performance regression, or other similarly
judgement-based workflows. There is often no single “right” answer here, so what we
score them on are the actual steps taken by the model. In order to do this, we must
have telemetry for the execution of a workflow—and this forms a positive feedback
loop, where we can use our telemetry to identify scenarios of user unhappiness or
where the model performance is lacking, and then turn those into evals that we can
iterate toward improving.

It’s this loop that is at the heart of driving continuous improvement for LLM applica‐
tions. Having a reliable set of evaluations offers you the opportunity to experiment.
When a new model version is released, you can run your evaluations against the new
model and measure the change. You can replay existing sessions against changes in
prompts or tool harnesses and track the differences in behavior.

While a full discussion of eval design is outside the scope of this book,1 it’s important
to remember that your evals are similar to unit tests. Ultimately, they only test a small
portion of the overall function of an LLM application—the part where the application
interacts with the language model. You can have great evals and a terrible UX; this is
where observability fills the gap. Achieving observability for these applications starts
with telemetry.

Designing Telemetry for LLM Applications
Instrumentation and telemetry for LLM applications is conceptually similar to instru‐
mentation for any other application in that you care about inputs and outputs,
requests and responses, the duration of API calls, and so on. Helpfully, quite a bit
of this instrumentation can be generated automatically via built-in OpenTelemetry
instrumentation in LLM client libraries. Modeling for most LLM interactions can be
found in the OTel generative AI semantic conventions, and libraries that interact with
LLM providers should follow these.
If you look at the conventions, you’ll see that common values such as input and
output token count, model name, sampling parameters, and system prompts are
recorded on client spans. If you are using your own LLM client framework, you’ll
want to follow the conventions in order to create maximally portable telemetry data.
There are a few unique considerations and questions that you’ll need to answer when
designing telemetry for your application, though:
How much visibility should you have for inputs and outputs?
   User prompts to an LLM are impossibly varied, and can often be incredibly large.
   This is discussed at length in the conventions, so we’ll avoid rehashing all of it
   here, but it’s worth considering. There are a few different ways to deal with this,
   but one to consider is that you’re possibly recording the inputs and outputs of
   a model independently from your telemetry—rather than writing the inputs and
   outputs to an attribute value, add a link to the system of record as the attribute
   value. This has downsides (namely, you’ll need to follow those links to actually
   see what was in a session), but it’s better than not having any visibility at all.
   You can choose to elide only certain inputs or outputs this way (e.g., binary files
   like images or PDFs). You should also consider user privacy expectations and
   regulations when designing telemetry. Users can—and will—use poor discretion
   with LLM systems, and you should be considerate to yourself when thinking
   about what you want or need to save.

How are you integrating your eval loop with your telemetry?
   Ideally, you should be able to “promote” certain traces of user interactions to
   evals easily. This goes in tandem with the prior consideration of inputs and
   outputs. Helpfully, many off-the-shelf evaluation frameworks also support OTel,
   enabling you to make an apples-to-apples comparison between a user session
   across two different models or prompt variations.
How should you approach cost tracking?
   As LLM usage (and spend) continues to rise, organizations are demanding a
   greater ROI—or at least the ability to attribute value to their monthly AI bill. You
   may note that the semantic conventions don’t include a standard attribute key for
   cost data. You may elect to create your own, or you can derive this cost at query
   time based on the model and amount of tokens used. We recommend the latter
   option, as it allows you to easily create eyeball cost differences between different
   prompt versions or model updates.
How are you integrating user feedback tracking into your telemetry?
   Traditional observability approaches can use proxies for UX, such as errors or
   API response time, as a primary signal of UX. This isn’t necessarily the case with
   LLM applications. Fast but incorrect responses are unlikely to spark joy in your
   users. Adding UX attributes such as feedback signals (thumbs up/down, user
   retry, etc.) to your telemetry is a powerful technique to better understand how
   your system is performing in production as well as to identify new evals.
LLM applications and features may exist as islands of functionality in your system,
but most of the time they need to interact with other services in your backend for
everything from authorization and authentication to databases. As of this writing, an
emerging pattern for AI applications is the agent pattern: an LLM calling tool in a
loop, with the ability to modify its environment. Agents require access to internal
and external APIs for all manner of functions and functionality—everything from
contextual data about the user that’s interacting with them, to historical records of
prior conversations, to embedded or third-party knowledge bases and remote APIs.
While they’re often being developed as greenfield applications, agents interact and
touch almost every aspect of an existing system. The key insight is that telemetry
design for LLMs isn’t just about seeing what the LLM is doing or how users are
interacting with it, but about the connections between the AI part of the application
and the rest of your system.

Analyzing Telemetry for AI Applications
As mentioned, natural language inputs to an AI application are impossibly varied,
complex, and nondeterministic. While you may have telemetry data recording every
aspect of an interaction with the AI, looking at that data along a single axis often


396   |   Chapter 21: Observability for Large Language Models
results in unsatisfying results. Looking at just errors may give you a priority list of
bugs to fix, but this may not move the needle on the overall user experience of your
product.
Broadly, it’s useful to think of your analysis workflows in terms of two different
outcomes:

 1. Ensuring that the deterministic aspects of your AI application are functioning
    properly
 2. Building a continuous improvement loop for the nondeterministic aspects of
    your application

The former outcome has been written about at length in this book, and much of the
same advice applies. Multidimensional analysis of attributes enables you to find bugs,
spot performance regressions, and improve overall performance. A common example
of this, as of writing, is using observability to understand upstream LLM providers as
a resource. Tracking rate limiting, retries, and response time from a remote provider
is a key detail in both improving UX as well as managing your cost profile.
A specific example of this is using telemetry analysis to determine cache behavior.
LLM providers heavily encourage callers to leverage token caching, and incentivize
this through significant cost reductions. Telemetry can help you identify opportuni‐
ties for caching, or to discover where you’re incorrectly invalidating your cache.
Providers will usually provide this information in their API response, so be sure to
include it on your spans!
Additionally, due to a variety of factors, upstream model providers will often time
out or fail to service otherwise-valid requests. Being able to track and monitor
these through service level objectives (SLOs) is a valuable use of your time. Often,
engineers will reach for threshold-based alerting to notify them about problems
such as this, but these alerts are fundamentally unactionable and should be avoided
(unless, of course, the reader happens to be part of an LLM inference provider, in
which case you probably should have threshold alerting for things like this), as there’s
little you can do about it. SLOs are a better option here because they smooth out
these intermittent spikes and help your team focus on meaningful degradation to UX.
The Honeycomb Model Context Protocol (MCP) server is an excellent example of
this flywheel in action. When developing the MCP server, we only had a limited
dataset to pull from in developing evals and gathering feedback about the real-world
performance of our tools. Compounding this, the instrumentation and telemetry
available to Honeycomb engineers is rather idiosyncratic compared to both the aver‐
age Honeycomb customer and the average organization. Honeycomb, after all, is an
observability tool—our engineering team is accustomed to having a tool available
that supports high-cardinality data. In order to understand how customers were


                                                    Analyzing Telemetry for AI Applications   |   397
using the MCP server, we needed to instrument the tooling in such a way that we
could not only see what tools customers were using, but also where those tools were
failing. We paid special attention to errors in this process in order to determine where
to invest our engineering resources. An MCP server is an interesting case, in that
you cannot see the caller’s logic or rationale behind why it’s using a particular tool or
passing in specific parameters; you can only see what it’s actually doing. We decided
to set an SLO of 90% success rate for queries and waited to see what happened.
In doing so, we discovered several interesting facts. First, calling models tended to
jump to conclusions about field names, even when prompted to first check available
fields. Second, we noticed that models had significant issues with interpreting and
modifying time ranges. This feedback informed our evaluations, giving us entire
classes of errors and issues that we could build test cases for and start to iterate on
prompting, tool descriptions, and more.
This basic flywheel is uniquely suited to the development of AI applications, irrespec‐
tive of their type. Telemetry data becomes the golden standard for understanding
how users are interacting and engaging with your application, which provides the fuel
of continuous improvement for your development process.

Feeding Observability Data Into
AI Application Development
There are two primary ways that you’ll want to integrate observability data from AI
applications into your development cycle. The first, as mentioned, is as a source for
evaluations. The second is as feedback for your software harness. What does this
mean? Broadly, the harness is the deterministic code that you’re building around the
AI in your application. This harness is responsible for everything from providing
tools to the AI (methods and functions it can call to perform work), to correcting
errors in inputs or outputs, to how the AI communicates with end users. It’s the
plumbing, so to speak, between your application and its various services and the
language model itself.
While it’s important to do this for any application, it’s more urgent for AI applications
precisely because they’re unlikely to behave the same way across environments or
users. In a traditional application, you can build voluminous amounts of test cases
that cover practically every interaction a user may have, and then run those tests
across a homogenous array of environments. This is not the case for AI applications.
There is no amount of testing that will possibly capture every interaction, and the
pace of innovation in AI is so high at this point that there’s little guarantee that the
decisions you make today will have a shelf life of over six months. You must test in
prod.

It can be appealing to try and address all challenges through improved prompt
engineering. But while this is an important aspect of building AI applications, often a
little code will go a long way. An example of this is the Honeycomb Query Assistant
feature. When released, it produced an error of some kind about 25% of the time.
This high rate of errors is quite common for LLM-powered features at the outset.
When Honeycomb developers looked at some of the most common errors, they
found that many were parsing or validation errors—the response that OpenAI gave
back was a JSON object whose structure couldn’t pass validation checks. For example,
consider the following JSON object:
    {
        "calculations":[
           {"op":"COUNT", "column":"exception.message"}
        ],
        "filters":[
           {"column":"exception.message","op":"exists"},
           {"column":"parent_name","op":"exists"}
        ],
        "breakdowns":[
           "exception.message","parent_name"
        ],
        "orders":[
           {"op":"COUNT","order":"descending"}
        ]
    }


In the Query Assistant feature, the object attempts to represent a query for “exception
count by caller”—a common scenario that users use Honeycomb for. However, it’s
subtly incorrect. The structure that represents a COUNT operator is wrong, because
COUNT doesn’t take a column as input. A correct response, like this one, would not
have included the column:
    {
        "calculations":[
           {"op":"COUNT"}
        ],
        "filters":[
           {"column":"exception.message","op":"exists"},
           {"column":"parent_name","op":"exists"}
        ],
        "breakdowns":[
           "exception.message","parent_name"
        ],
        "orders":[
           {"op":"COUNT","order":"descending"}
        ]
    }

In this example, the first object is mostly correct. In fact, programmatically removing
the column field from the COUNT calculation object not only makes the query object
fully valid, but the resulting response from this post-processing is an ideal query to
generate given the input. And so, instead of failing validation and creating no result
for the user, Honeycomb decided to intervene on this kind of error by correcting the
structure programmatically so it passes the validation check.
As a result of analyzing outputs like this and issuing only these kinds of manual
corrections, the error rate in Query Assistant dropped from 25% to about 14%. Later,
improvements to a prompt brought the error rate to less than 1% (excluding timeouts
or other uncontrollable errors).
Finally, an interesting sidebar to this story is that the broader engineering team
realized these AI-generated queries were actually representative of an unmet need
with our users. People actually wanted to be able to COUNT with a column, and we
shipped a change allowing that query syntax. This, helpfully, meant we could reduce
some of our error correction code and improve the performance of Query Assistant
as well!

Using Evaluations and Observability Together
If you’re already collecting inputs, outputs, and errors through production traces of
your AI application, then you’re 90% of the way toward an effective eval flywheel.
You have real data about how users are actually using your feature: the sort of things
they ask for, the data sources and tools that the LLM uses, and the responses. You can
export this data—or, in a purpose-built tool, generate new evals from it—and begin to
update your evals based on what’s actually happening.
The basic loop here is as follows:

 1. Are the new inputs something we want to evaluate against? Generally, the answer
    here is yes. Then:
      a. For these inputs, is there an ideal output? Was the existing output the ideal
         output?
      b. If not, update the output to what it should be based on the inputs.
 2. Repeat step 1 until you’ve fleshed out your golden dataset with real user behav‐
    iors.
 3. Remove items from the golden dataset that are not representative of real user
    behaviors.

Rerun your evaluations and compare with historical trends. If this is a new applica‐
tion or workflow, you’ll most likely see a dramatic drop in eval performance—this

is to be expected, as real-world use cases often intersect with things that you didn’t
expect.
A practical example of this is how we update our task evals for Canvas. As mentioned
earlier, the task evals are a very general goal with a known answer. We care about how
many steps it takes for a model to arrive at this answer, how many tokens it uses, and
if the answer contains a few specific conclusions. We use LLM-as-a-Judge to score the
response into a simple pass/fail based on some criteria, then select a sample of these
to review by hand on a regular cadence.
This manual review is one of the most important parts of effective observability
(and evals), because it surfaces insights that the LLM might miss. We noticed that a
particular task had a persistent failure due to the model misinterpreting the meaning
of a particular column in the result data. This column was named is_slow and
was set to true when a request took over 500 milliseconds to complete. The model
was unable to see that this column was a calculated field (a Honeycomb query-time
construct that is able to create a new value based on other attribute values in an
event), and it assumed that the column was actually a feature flag. Because it couldn’t
find any other reason, the model’s logic was that the is_slow attribute meant there
was a load test (or some other external event) happening that made the request slow,
and incorporated it into its result. This misinterpretation caused the model to ignore
the underlying factors contributing to latency—it effectively went off on a wild goose
chase.
Our solution? We updated the column to include a description of what it actually
was, and suddenly the model started ignoring it! It then was able to successfully
investigate and identify the underlying cause. In practice, this is an incredibly com‐
mon failure mode for AI agents and AI applications: the model infers or imputes
meaning incorrectly, leading to incorrect assumptions and conclusions. Good observ‐
ability and good evals enable you to catch these sorts of things in production and
update your software harness to correct them, or update your prompts to better steer
model behavior.
A final note here on public benchmarks and generic metrics: many organizations
have published and promulgated various measurements for things like hallucinations,
toxicity, tone, truthfulness, bias, etc. Frontier labs will evaluate their models against
these published benchmarks and produce very pretty graphs demonstrating how they
stack up. These benchmarks and measurements are not bad, per se, but they can
be highly misleading. You may find that your AI application scores quite well on
these benchmarks while utterly failing to deliver value to end users. Put in the extra
effort to integrate telemetry between your AI system and the rest of your application,
and create the learning flywheel of evals with observability, and you’ll gain a better
understanding of how well these features are working for your end users and how
you can improve them toward those ends.

Conclusion
At its core, observability for AI applications looks a lot like observability for any other
application. The nondeterministic nature of an LLM presents unique challenges, but
teams already practicing observability will find their existing tools and frameworks
transfer readily.
The key insight from this chapter is the learning flywheel: production telemetry feeds
evaluations, evaluations inform harness improvements, and those improvements
show up in telemetry. This loop is how you move from a 25% error rate to less
than 1%, how you catch a model misinterpreting a column name and fix it with
a description, how you discover that models are guessing at fields and fix their
behavior.
In the age of AI, it’s tempting to reach for AI-native solutions to every problem.
But the fundamentals haven’t changed: instrument well, analyze with intent, monitor
what matters to your users and your business, and create feedback loops that com‐
pound. The barrier to building AI applications has dropped dramatically. But the bar
of success for operating them well is still having empathy for your customers, your
business, and your team.
