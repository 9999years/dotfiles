An Introduction to Telemetry Pipelines
If you’ve worked in observability, you’re familiar with the challenge: modern systems
generate an overwhelming amount of telemetry. Every signal is critical (or is it?).
Every new cluster, new service, new application, and even the expansion of legacy
systems compounds the challenge. Observability, security, compliance, and AI are
hungry for data, and even if you know what can be trimmed, doing that at enterprise
scale quickly overwhelms even the best-prepared teams.
This is the reality that many organizations find themselves in as their observability
practice matures. When we started Bindplane, this challenge didn’t have a name, let
alone a clear solution. OTel was in its infancy, and the term “telemetry pipeline” didn’t
exist. Observability teams beginning to hit this limit were searching for quick fixes,
or began designing their own systems with mixed results. It was a finger-in-the-dam
approach of temporary patches and improvised pipelines meant to hold back the
flood.
In our early days, we built what we called “monitoring integration as a service.” In
hindsight, the description simply reflected how early the space was. At the time,
however, we lacked a vocabulary to discuss the challenge and solution set. More
importantly, it was missing the point: customers and platforms reported that to “get
the data in” was simply too difficult. How do you collect every signal from every
device, and do this in a platform designed for simplicity and scale? That’s the problem
we initially focused on solving, but in doing so, it magnified a parallel challenge we
hadn’t yet recognized. You can instrument and collect every trace, log, event, and
metric, and now your team is happy, and your observability platform has everything
it needs. But the relief is short-lived, and quickly turns into difficult questions from
the CFO, along with a growing infrastructure team that’s scrambling to manage the
new scale issues created by all that data.
It’s a classic scaling problem. You make it scale, and then reveal a whole range of
new challenges. In observability, the explosion of data volume and the emergence of
new use cases for this data have made old approaches unsustainable. The cost and
complexity of managing telemetry at scale demand a new solution. If you’re feeling
that pressure, you’re not alone. And this is exactly where telemetry pipelines come in.

The Telemetry Pipeline Solution
The solution starts with recognizing the problem. It’s not just that there’s too much
data; it’s that the data flow itself was never treated as its own concern. Instead of
every application determining where telemetry should go, a telemetry pipeline should
become the backbone by collecting, normalizing, processing, and routing signals
everywhere they’re needed.



294   |   Chapter 16: Telemetry Management with Pipelines
Take, for example, a power grid. Wiring every appliance directly to a power plant
would be fragile and inefficient. This is solved by utilizing substations, routing, and
redundancy. All that’s required for the consumer is a plug and an outlet, and the
remaining delivery is handled. Telemetry pipelines work the same way.
All modern telemetry pipelines share several core functions:

  • Collect signals from any source emitting telemetry.
  • Normalize and secure data into a standard model while enforcing compliance.
  • Enrich telemetry with context to make it actionable.
  • Reduce volume with filtering, aggregation, and sampling.
  • Route the right telemetry to the right tool: observability, security, analytics,
    and/or long-term storage.

These functions ensure that telemetry pipelines manage end-to-end delivery for logs,
metrics, traces, or any other signal.

Why This Matters Now
Pipeline adoption is growing exponentially because of two basic principles: cost and
standardization. Sending everything everywhere is no longer sustainable, and OTel
has become the vendor-neutral standard. With its Collector and libraries, teams can
build flexible pipelines supported by every major observability and security vendor.
Cost and standardization have made telemetry pipelines a foundational piece of
modern infrastructure. With a pipeline in place, you can:

  • Add a new observability tool without touching application code.
  • Enforce compliance rules company-wide, from a central location.
  • Reuse the same telemetry through multiple domains (e.g., observability, security,
    AI and machine learning, or BI).

Taken together, a well-architected pipeline transforms telemetry from a scaling issue
into an asset.

Core Functions of a Modern Telemetry Pipeline
In the previous section, I outlined the core functions of a telemetry pipeline. Now,
let’s go a layer deeper. Rather than a patchwork of components, you can think of a
telemetry pipeline as a sequence of stages: collect → normalize → enrich → reduce →
route. All of these stages are supported by two critical requirements: data resilience
and pipeline control and observability, as shown in Figure 16-1.

In this section, I’ll explain each stage, why it matters, and how OTel brings it to life.

Collect
Telemetry collection is the first and most important step for most observability
practitioners. Applications, infrastructure, and devices all emit logs, metrics, traces,
and events. A telemetry pipeline must collect every signal that matters, from legacy
systems and cloud providers to modern microservices and greenfield deployments.
Without a reliable collection, decisions are made on incomplete telemetry. Telemetry
collection ensures the full context required to troubleshoot, secure, and optimize
your systems.
OTel has become the standard for collection. It’s open source and vendor neutral
with industry-wide support, and able to gather logs, metrics, and traces through a
single collector. It includes broad support for both new and legacy technologies, with
over 100 receivers in the OTel project, including generic options like OpenTelemetry
Protocol (OTLP), Prometheus, StatsD, Java Management Extensions (JMX), and host
metrics. The depth of support and flexibility makes OTel an ideal building block for
your telemetry pipeline.
Moving away from proprietary agents and SDKs is critical. As long as collection
is tied directly to a single destination, you will never achieve the reusability and
standardization that a pipeline enables.

Normalize and Secure
Telemetry normalization and security is the next step. Raw telemetry is messy, and
this stage makes it structured, consistent, and compliant. This is accomplished with:
Parsing and coercion
    Convert unstructured logs into structured events, fix timestamps, and enforce
    schemas.



296   |   Chapter 16: Telemetry Management with Pipelines
Normalization
   Transform mixed formats (for example, JSON logs or Zipkin traces) into a com‐
   mon model such as OTLP. This prepares signals for downstream processing.
Security and governance
    Redact personally identifiable information (PII) and apply data access, retention,
    and residency controls to manage compliance requirements—e.g., General Data
    Protection Regulation (GDPR), Federal Risk and Authorization Management
    Program (FedRAMP).
Normalizing early ensures that all downstream data is clean, usable, and compliant,
reducing both rework and risk later in the pipeline.

Enrich
Telemetry enrichment adds the context that makes telemetry meaningful. Raw signals
are enhanced with metadata, business context, and external lookups. Enrichment
includes:
Infrastructure context
     Attach details like Kubernetes pod, node, region, or availability zone.
Business context
    Add business-specific details such as customer ID, environment (prod, staging),
    or service tier.
External lookups
    Resolve IPs to locations or map DNS to hostnames.
Early enrichment makes telemetry immediately actionable and shortens time to
detection. (For more examples on telemetry enrichment, see Chapter 6.)

Reduce
Telemetry reduction, or reducing overall data volume, is one of the biggest reasons
teams adopt pipelines. It keeps costs under control and helps maintain a high signal-
to-noise ratio. Modern systems generate more data than most teams can reasonably
handle, and low-value telemetry can easily bury the data that matters most.
The overall volume of telemetry can be reduced with:
Filtering
     Drop low-value logs, redundant metrics, or trivial traces.
Deduplication
   Consolidate repeats without losing visibility.



                                               Core Functions of a Modern Telemetry Pipeline   |   297
Aggregation
    Roll up metrics or events to higher-level views.
Sampling
   Apply head, tail, or adaptive sampling to noisy sources.
Centralized reduction preserves visibility while removing waste and noise. This
makes it possible to adapt your reduction strategy in real time as needs change.

Route
Routing telemetry to multiple destinations is where the value of a pipeline becomes
obvious. Once signals are standardized, enriched, and reduced, they can be used for
multiple purposes. There are two main use cases for routing:
Fan-out
    Send logs to a security information and event management (SIEM), metrics to
    APM, and events to analytics, and store high-fidelity copies in cloud storage for
    compliance, as shown in Figure 16-2.




      Figure 16-2. Telemetry routing to multiple destinations—observability platforms,
      security analytics, log analytics, and archive.

Flexibility
    Add or test new tools without touching application code or reinstrumenting your
    systems.
Routing turns a single clean data stream into fuel for observability, security, compli‐
ance, and AI, without duplication.

Data Resilience
Data resilience ensures telemetry keeps flowing when systems fail. Backends go down,
networks spike, and data volumes burst. A resilient pipeline absorbs those shocks and

298   |   Chapter 16: Telemetry Management with Pipelines
ensures telemetry is delivered reliably even when parts of the system are degraded.
That resiliency can be achieved with:
Buffering and persistence
    Temporary, adjustable buffers at each stage of the pipeline with optional disk
    persistence to prevent data loss
Retries and flow control
    Retry logic control with exponential backoff and backpressure to avoid the
    “thundering herd” effect or cascading failures impacting downstream consumers
High availability and failover
    Redundant agents, gateways, and control planes to provide continuity when
    nodes or regions fail
For example, in Bindplane, resilience is critical at scale. Our collectors and gateways
buffer telemetry locally, queue it for delivery, and support failover paths when a
backend is unavailable. This prevents gaps in observability data during outages and
helps teams maintain trust in their telemetry.

Pipeline Control and Observability
Even the best pipeline fails without management and visibility. Pipelines are not “set
it and forget it.” They need to be monitored, tuned, and governed. A control plane
makes this possible, providing the capabilities that keep pipelines operationally viable
at scale. All telemetry pipelines should include:
Real-time visibility
    Dashboards and metrics show throughput, error rates, and latency across the
    pipeline.
Policy enforcement
     Centralized controls apply compliance rules, retention settings, and data-
     handling policies consistently.
Configuration management
   Pipelines must be adjustable at runtime to roll out changes quickly, update
   destinations, or alter reduction strategies.
Incident response
    Telemetry control planes make it possible to rehydrate historical data, reroute
    flows, or isolate faulty components during incidents.
Fleet management
     Standard protocols such as Open Agent Management Protocol, or OpAMP,
     define how collectors report health, fetch configurations, and receive updates.
     Tools like Bindplane build on this to add enterprise-grade features such as

      torical data.
This layer of control is what makes pipelines valuable. It lets you manage fleets,
preview changes, and keep environments consistent so you can truly simplify and

What You Should Remember About Core Functions
A modern telemetry pipeline is built from a sequence of core functions. They work
together to transform raw signals into a governed, reusable stream of data. Each stage
builds on the last. Collecting, normalizing, enriching, reducing, routing, and ensuring
resilience and control form the backbone of a modern telemetry pipeline.
When they operate in harmony, telemetry shifts from an unruly firehose into a
strategic asset that powers observability, security, compliance, and innovation. This
foundation sets the stage for the architecture and adoption strategies that I’ll walk
you through next.

Pipeline Architecture in Practice
So far, you’ve learned about the core functions of a telemetry pipeline. Architecture
is where they come together in practice. How you deploy the components determines
scalability, resilience, and manageability. Get the architecture right, and you can start
small and scale to thousands of collectors without losing control.

Core Components
At the heart of any telemetry pipeline are three categories of components:
Agents
   These are lightweight OTel Collectors deployed on hosts, containers, or as side‐
   cars. They capture telemetry as close to the source as possible with minimal
   overhead.
Gateways
    These are centralized, scalable nodes that aggregate data from agents, apply pro‐
    cessing and compliance rules, reduce volume, and route signals to destinations.
    They are typically deployed in clusters for resilience and throughput.
Control plane
   This is the management layer that makes pipelines operationally viable at scale.
   In Bindplane, this is where you configure, visualize, and update collectors and
   gateways across fleets.
These categories are shown in Figure 16-3.

Figure 16-3. Core pipeline components—agents, gateways, and a centralized control
plane providing orchestration and configuration.

Another essential core component in Bindplane is the OTel Collector. A telemetry
collector is the foundation of most modern pipelines. The OTel Collector has a
pluggable architecture that includes:
Receivers
    For ingest (HTTP, gRPC, Kafka, Syslog, etc.)
Processors
    For transformations like normalization, enrichment, sampling, and batching
Exporters
   For sending data to backends (Prometheus, Elasticsearch, Datadog)
Extensions
    For cross-cutting functions like zPages, health checks, and authentication
A single binary can run in multiple roles, which is where the flexibility shines:
Agent
   Runs close to workloads, performs minimal processing, and forwards telemetry
   to gateways. This keeps the edge footprint light and reduces impact on local
   systems.
Gateway
    Runs as resilient, load-balanced clusters. Gateways handle the heavy lifting like
    reduction, compliance, routing, buffering, and queuing on dedicated nodes that
    won’t interfere with workloads.

Deployment Patterns

The flexibility of these components means they can be composed in various ways to
suit different organizational use cases. The most common configurations include:
Agent-only
   This is the simplest approach. Each agent sends telemetry directly to one or more
   backends. This works in small environments, but it doesn’t scale and it creates
   tight coupling between sources and destinations.
Agent + gateway
   This is the most common production pattern. Agents forward telemetry to
   gateways, which centralize processing, reduction, and routing. This decouples
   collection from delivery and establishes compliance and control in one place.
Multi-tier topologies
   This pattern supports the needs of large, distributed organizations. Local gate‐
   ways handle local collection, reduction, and compliance before forwarding
   optimized signals to regional gateways. This enables high availability, disaster
   recovery, and cost efficiency at scale.

Scaling and Performance in Practice
At Bindplane, we work with customers processing petabytes of telemetry daily across
hundreds of thousands of OTel collectors running as both agents and gateways.
Performance and resilience at that scale require careful planning, but with the right
patterns, you can avoid common pitfalls, such as:

Agents
   Agents should stay lightweight. Apply minimal processing, set clear memory and
   CPU limits, and use small local buffers only to handle short bursts of data. Their
   primary role is reliable forwarding to the gateway layer without impacting local
   workloads.
Gateways
    Gateways carry the heavy load. Scale them horizontally, add redundancy and load
    balancing to eliminate single points of failure, and use buffering and queuing to
    absorb bursts. Health checks should track queue depth, latency, and error rates to
    catch issues.
Applied consistently, these practices enable pipelines to handle massive throughput
without data loss or degraded performance.

Adoption and Migration
Few companies start thinking about pipeline architecture, where it fits, and how best
to use it from a clean greenfield slate. More commonly, pipelines are introduced in
brownfield telemetry architectures. By the time you’re considering adding a telemetry
pipeline, you already have hundreds of streams running on proprietary agents. The
challenge is adapting it to the ideal pipeline architecture.
In this section, we’ll look at a phased strategy for introducing telemetry pipelines into
preexisting application architectures.
It’s rare that you’ll need to reinstrument your entire environment to gain the benefits
of a telemetry pipeline.

A Phased Path to Adoption
At Bindplane, we guide customers through a staged approach to a telemetry pipeline:

 1. Start with a greenfield app.
 2. Establish best practices and future-proofing with OTel standards.
 3. Incrementally redirect legacy flows to the new telemetry pipeline.

A phased approach delivers early wins, builds familiarity, and reduces risk by avoid‐
ing disruptive cutovers. The process starts small and then incrementally incorporates
more systems until the majority of your telemetry is standardized and centrally
managed.

Phase 1: Greenfield
Start with a simple pipeline connected to a single application or service. This can be
applied regardless of the source type. The goal is to validate the pipeline end to end
and provide a low-risk way to build confidence in new tooling and practices. This is

Business Case
Throughout this chapter, I’ve focused primarily on the technical advantages of tele‐
metry pipelines over existing solutions. While critical, making the case for pipelines
also means articulating their impact to those who control the budget. In this section,
we’ll examine telemetry pipelines through a business-value lens.

Cost Control
Telemetry pipelines deliver direct financial impact by addressing one of the biggest
pain points in observability: ingest volume. Most vendors charge based on the
amount of data sent, meaning you pay for everything, regardless of whether it’s
valuable or not. In fact, we’ve often worked with teams that discovered that 20–40%
of their SIEM data was never actually queried. A telemetry pipeline solves this by
filtering, deduplication, and sampling. Better yet, all of this happens before the data
ever reaches expensive telemetry storage.
An even bigger win is cost predictability. Instead of explaining why observability
spend spiked 30% last quarter, pipelines provide real-time visibility into data flows
and the cost drivers behind them. This turns observability from an unpredictable
cost to a manageable and forecastable monthly invoice. CFOs stop asking why costs
are exploding, enabling teams to focus on improving insights rather than justifying
budgets.

Vendor Neutrality
Pipelines built on OTel remove the lock-in of proprietary agents and plumbing.
Switching tools becomes a configuration change, not a multiyear migration project.
This flexibility creates negotiating power with vendors and gives organizations the
confidence to adopt new tools without the fear of disruption.

Telemetry as a Strategic Asset
At the same time, pipelines elevate telemetry from a cost liability to a strategic asset.
Think of telemetry as raw material:

  • Observability teams use it for uptime and performance.
  • Security teams rely on it for detection and response.
  • Compliance teams need it to enforce security policies and regulations.

Without a pipeline, each team duplicates effort, reinstruments systems, and pays
vendors to store overlapping telemetry. A pipeline unlocks a unified, clean, enriched
stream of telemetry, fanned out to every team and tool that needs it.
Pipelines also reduce compliance and security risk. Sensitive fields can be redacted
before they leave your environment, with GDPR, FedRAMP, or the Health Insur‐
ance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA) controls enforced centrally
instead of scattered across dozens of tools. This reduces breach exposure, simplifies
audits, and ensures consistent governance without relying on downstream vendors.
The economics are straightforward:

  • Save 50% or more on vendor ingest costs.
  • Reinvest ~10% into pipeline infrastructure.
  • Net out at ~40% savings, while gaining more control and vendor neutrality.

As telemetry grows with your business, telemetry pipelines ensure that observability
costs don’t outpace revenue. The result is sustainable scaling, improved visibility and
security, predictable costs, and telemetry evolving from a headache into a competitive
advantage.

The Role of Telemetry Pipelines
Telemetry pipelines are now a core part of observability engineering. They solve the
scaling trap that every growing system encounters: too much data, too many tools,
and not enough control. By collecting, normalizing, enriching, reducing, and routing
with resilience and governance, pipelines turn raw telemetry into something you can
rely on.
Today, telemetry pipelines are no longer optional. They are the backbone that makes
observability, security, and compliance sustainable at scale. When you invest in a
telemetry pipeline, you can expect lower costs, predictable spend, vendor freedom,
and stronger governance. Your telemetry does indeed become a strategic asset.
