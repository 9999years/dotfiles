Welcome to the end of the second edition. You made it.
We have covered observability from many different angles in this book: its origins in
control theory, how software engineers should instrument and understand the code
they write, deep dives and use cases, and how technical decision-makers should think
about observability for their orgs.
Our goal in this edition was to update the technical content, but also to underscore
how observability is derived from first principles and reasoning that endures, so you
can guide your own teams and organizations through turbulent waters.
Our industry is undergoing a convulsive period of compressed change. Roles and
identities are being stressed in new ways. Anxiety is endemic because the things we
long took for granted are no longer safe bets.
In this chapter, we’ll look at where the industry is headed over the next few years, and
what engineers and technical decision-makers can do to prepare.

Developers Must Return to Production
Before we look at what’s next, we must address the “wish” we made at the close of the
first edition (paraphrased slightly):
    The space we will be watching closely is that of developer workflows…Developers need
    fast feedback loops and the ability to understand how each code change affects users in
    production. Incredibly few developers have the ability to do that.
    We believe the industry needs fundamentally better developer workflows in produc‐
    tion, and we predict observability will be the path for many to get there.




                                                                                              571
Developers still don’t have the ability to validate a diff they just shipped, or identify
what effect it had on any user or subset of users. Developers still live in a world where
testing means “correct in theory,” not correct in reality.
Consider this our one prediction for the second edition: the dev/ops dividing line
must be destroyed. Developers are no longer crafters of code; they are builders and
architects of systems. That system is production. Test in prod, or live a lie.
By this, we do not mean larding on yet another mandate onto overflowing plates. We
mean that core development workflows must include instrumentation and produc‐
tion telemetry, validating user expectations and commitments as they actually exist.
We can no longer afford to stub reality out of the picture. Software engineers must
become as fluent in the messy world of production as they are in the clean, structured
world of staging environments, CI/CD pipelines, and tests. Only one of those worlds
holds real value. Only one is production.
This has been forecasted before. This time is different. The reason why is AI.

The Changing Cost Model of Software
The dev/ops divide has endured long past its sell-by date because it encodes the ten‐
sion between change and stability in a way that is legible to org charts, budgets, and
career seekers. Over time, it has calcified into policy, friction, tools, and expectations
that combine to make production inaccessible and inhospitable to developers.
AI attacks this consensus on many simultaneous fronts: as a technological enabler,
it brings practices that have required exceptional engineering discipline and skill
within reach of the median engineering org. It provides the competitive pressure
to accelerate, and introduces nondeterminism as a feature—the only way to validate
agentic workflows is by instrumenting your code and observing production, then
porting what you learn back into evals.
But the most powerful and intriguing assault on the status quo comes from the
changing economics of code production.
For as long as we’ve had software, we have treasured the code artifact. We have
obsessed over its elegance and readability, and developed elaborate rituals around
changing it safely. As one of your authors declared earlier this year:
      There is no test suite on earth that could make me trust a new chunk of code more
      than I trust the code that’s been running in production for two years. That’s how trust
      works; it takes time.
         —Charity Majors

This is a truism where durable code is concerned. The trust lives in the code,
implicitly.
All of this makes sense—indeed, it is the only sensible approach—in a world where
producing code is time-consuming and hard, and good code is scarce. The true cost
of software has always been defined by the cost of maintaining it. Our focus on ele‐
gant code made sense because readability was a reasonable proxy for maintainability.
We couldn’t predict the future, but we knew our ability to read and understand the
code would gate any change.
But that gate has been necessary for the same reason that the only way to safely
change the code has been with a series of tiny incremental diffs: because most
behaviors have always been implicit and poorly understood, discovered only when we
break them. The most important parts of our systems and software—the expectations
of our users, the commitments we have made to uphold stability and continuity—
exist nowhere at all. Not in the code, and not in the system.

The Most Important Parts of Our System
Have Never Been Specified
The code is not the system. The system is the unique and irreplicable intersection
of code, infrastructure, users, builders, data, and time. Artifacts accrue meaning
as users develop expectations around existing behaviors. These layers of acquired,
nonarticulated meaning are what make rewrites, migrations, and replacements so
infamously perilous and particular.
Code that makes it into production becomes more than just code. It becomes the
fossilized record—the only record that exists—of developer intent, business needs,
and user expectations and dependencies that have built up over time.
The durable model of software delivery is agonizingly expensive. The cost of software
has always been defined by its maintenance costs as a direct consequence of those
sedimentary layers of accrued expectations and forgotten intent. If nobody under‐
stands them or knows what they are, we certainly can’t test for them.
If we are ever going to bring the cost of software development materially down, we
are going to have to unbundle the expectations from the artifact and make them
explicit—and live elsewhere.

The Great Unbundling
Few reach their second decade of software development without acquiring a robust
fear of large diffs. Indeed, we ourselves have argued that an intuitive sense for how
much change is too much change, along with a pessimistic stance to production
systems in general, is part of what defines maturity in a senior developer.
Chad Fowler, engineer and CTO at BlueYard, wrote a post called “The Deletion Test”,
in which he proposed a thought experiment: what do we lose when we delete all the
source code? Why is it so scary?
      When people say, “We can’t just throw the code away,” what they usually mean is
      something more precise:
          • We don’t know exactly what behavior is required.
          • We don’t know which failures are unacceptable.
          • We don’t know what invariants must always hold.
          • We don’t know how to tell if a new version is correct.
          • We don’t know which bugs are intentional fixes for forgotten edge cases.

      Those are not code problems. They are evaluation problems.
      Code becomes precious when it is the only place knowledge lives.

Fowler observes that the bottleneck has shifted from generating code to validating
code, as we have argued in this book. He goes on to reframe the question, from “How
do we write safer code?” to “What must exist so that code can be replaced safely?”:
      That question is harder. It requires deciding what truly matters and making it explicit,
      testable, and durable. It requires relocating rigor out of the implementation and into
      the system around it.

If you can delete and regenerate the code while upholding all of the commitments
you have made to users, the business, and other parts of the system, then code is
no longer the sedimentary record of long-forgotten developer intent or the residue
of past promises, only a materialized view of current understanding. Code becomes
cache. Maintainability becomes a property of the system, not the code.

The Human Cost of Compressed Change
Generative AI has moved so quickly from novelty to utility that many are still
processing the shock. We don’t wish to catastrophize or equate the dislocation of
technological advancement with far greater traumas. But the change has come fast,
which makes it hard.

Many seem to be exhibiting mild symptoms of disorientation, disconnection, or
depression. We have lost track of how many engineers have privately confided their
plans to retire instead of processing the abrupt obsolescence of the skills they had
cultivated for decades, building careers and communities and identities around mas‐
tery of practice. Others show traces of mania, obsession, or magical thinking, issuing
overheated exhortations around how “this changes everything.”
Silicon Valley, of course, prefers—some might say actively cultivates—the latter.

The Claim: “AI Is Unprecedented”
It has grown popular in these parts to speak of AI as sui generis, the trigger mecha‐
nism that will unleash unbounded exponential growth by achieving artificial general
intelligence (AGI). We will build robots that do our jobs better than we can, that will
build robots better than we could, and those robots will build other robots beyond
the capability of our understanding, and thus inaugurate a new era of boundless
exponential growth for our species, where we are no longer in control of our destiny.
Climate change? Solvable and solved, thanks to more powerful models. Poverty?
Solved by UBI (universal basic income) via some vague, undescribed AGI-related
mechanism that is definitely not taxing the rich.
This strikes us as a fantasy. At best, it’s a thought experiment that remains unproven.
“Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence,” as Carl Sagan said. If AI can
usher in exponential forever growth, that violates the one thing we know for certain
about exponential growth: it will end, either by flattening out into an S-curve or by
consuming all resources and crashing.
Some of the pressure people are experiencing is due to real structural change and
uncertainty, but much of it is speculation or marketing. To pick on one representative
post that blanketed the airwaves in early 2026:
    The AI labs made a deliberate choice. They focused on making AI great at writing
    code first...because building AI requires a lot of code. If AI can write that code, it
    can help build the next version of itself. A smarter version, which writes better code,
    which builds an even smarter version. Making AI great at coding was the strategy that
    unlocks everything else. That’s why they did it first. My job started changing before
    yours not because they were targeting software engineers...it was just a side effect of
    where they chose to aim first.
    They’ve now done it. And they’re moving on to everything else.

We must resist being credulous. Until proven otherwise, we still live in history, in a
world governed by finite resources and human constraints. To claim that we are on
the verge of exponential liftoff, where robots build robots beyond our understanding,
is quite extraordinary.

Reality is enough to deal with, we feel, without borrowing hypotheticals from the
future to stress about.

This Moment Has Many Precedents
A more practical reason to resist the framing of AI as sui generis is that it robs us of
our history. And history, it turns out, has many instructive precedents for our current
upheaval: 1

  • The craft of weaving, replaced by the powered loom
  • The craft of scribing, replaced by the printing press, the typewriter, the word
    processor, and Google Docs
  • The shift from handcrafted server pets to disposable, immutable infrastructure
    cattle in the early years of our own careers

The history of technology tells us that the most dramatic gains are made in the
earliest years of any technological breakthrough, as the easiest problems get solved
first. Once the low-hanging fruit has been harvested, it gets harder to come up with
new ideas, and progress slows and becomes ever more incremental. As Adam Becker
said, in Chapter 2 of More Everything Forever (Basic Books): “It is around 18 times
harder today to generate the exponential growth behind Moore’s Law than it was in
1971. Ideas, and the exponential growth they imply, are getting harder to find.”
To us, it seems unlikely that the AI labs chose software opportunistically to accelerate
their exponential takeover of humanity. Software is built out of language and logic.
Software, in other words, is the low-hanging fruit.

The Sysadmins Went Through This
Ever since the first reprogrammable computer was built—the ENIAC, 80 years ago—
the code artifact has been our most holy and sacred of relics. Lines of code built
the world’s most valuable companies and many of the nouveau-iest fortunes. The
loveliness of code has built reputations, landed jobs, and justified exorbitant salaries
compared to other roles in a post-post-industrialized world economy.
Of course it’s disorienting to live through the rapid dethroning of code as singular
repository of meaning, value, craft, contracts, and ledger of history.
But if you, like us, are old enough to have worked in tech in the 2000s, there is also
something familiar about this feeling.

Do you remember the sysadmins?
We do. We remember their pride and dedication to craft; encyclopedic knowledge
about kernel modules and filesystems, how to calculate a netmask or configure a
routing table. The long running in-joke of obfuscated Perl code awards. We also
remember the panic and paranoia and mounting embitterment that surged in some
corners of the community, once the value of those skills began to crater, replaced by
automation and regeneration.
The emotional reaction is normal. The weavers felt it, the sysadmins felt it. It hap‐
pens, it’s hard, you move through it. Most sysadmins ultimately adapted. They picked
up a programming language, started writing more code, and automated themselves
out of a job. They built the systems that replaced the work they used to do, and then
they worked on those systems.

From Software Code to Software Systems
The shift from handcrafted servers to immutable infrastructure taught us that muta‐
bility is the sworn enemy of understanding. Any artifact that is edited in place creates
drift. Drift is what makes systems impossible to maintain.
Our ability to kill and regenerate infrastructure components is the reason we trust
it. At Honeycomb, we kill the oldest Kafka node off via cron every Tuesday. That’s
why we are confident in our bootstrapping and balancing processes: everything is
repeatable, the data can be regenerated, the commitments live elsewhere.
The fact that we cannot regenerate our code in the same way is a sign that we don’t
understand it. We don’t know which commitments we have made. We don’t know
which dependencies will break. We find them by breaking them, mostly.
In another post, Fowler said:
    The discomfort with “fresh code” is understandable, but misplaced.
    What people actually fear is undetected behavior change, performance regressions,
    security regressions, and silent drift. Those failures are caused by unobserved change,
    not by newness.
    A system with stable contracts, strong evaluations, continuous monitoring, and clear
    rollback paths can safely tolerate very fresh code. A system without those things is
    dangerous even if the code is ten years old.

Throughout computing history, working code has been expensive and hard to pro‐
duce, so we have conserved it and changed it parsimoniously.
Every change was gated on human understanding, and we had little cognitive
capacity left over. As a result, everything outside the code got neglected. Architecture.
Coherence. User experience. We have treated these like afterthoughts, emergent prop‐
erties and effluvia of our precious code, but actually they are what matters.

                                                      From Software Code to Software Systems   |   577
Now that working code is abundant and cheap, we can reclaim that cognitive capacity
and use it to build the parts that matter.

Value Must Be Backed by Durability
Disposable software is great for iterating fast and trying lots of experiments cheaply.
But when that software meets reality, it needs to slow down. Durability is not what
creates value, but value usually needs to be backed by durability.
People prefer a consistent experience and a coherent mental model. Behaviors create
expectations that we need to honor. Businesses run on structure and continuity. No
one wants to open Slack every morning and find all their buttons rearranged.
Code in production accumulates dependencies. This is a good thing. It means people
use it. It means it has value.
Dependencies require dependability, whether those dependencies are users, busi‐
nesses, or other components. The cost of maintaining software is driven by the
challenges of stably replacing its components.
In Chapter 2, we talked about the messy, gritty business of code crossing over—that
liminal state between states while fulfilling commitments to both.
Rewriting a system in flight is far more difficult than simply writing the original
legacy code or the new replacement; the challenge lies in crossing over. Dropping
the cost of code to zero doesn’t substantially alter the cost structure defined by
maintenance. Your migration path still consists of inching along, instrumenting and
observing, discovering commitments and entanglements as you go, mostly by break‐
ing them.
Moving to a world of specification, evaluation, and testable commitments—where
any component can be replaced behind its interface—changes the cost structure
entirely.

The Tools to Build Durable Systems Will Come
We feel lost and disoriented because our eyes have not yet adjusted to our new reality.
It is easier to see the practices we have lost than the tools, practices and ideas we will
gain.
The tools to build durable systems—to extract commitments and make them explicit,
to preserve intent at the right level of granularity, to make architecture the versiona‐
ble, reviewable artifact from which code is generated—do not exist yet. They will
come. The economics of scarcity dominated our past, but the economics of abun‐
dance will govern our future.

The problems of abundance are different problems. Our ability to generate code
has skyrocketed, but our ability to understand it has not kept pace; if anything,
understanding gets harder as volume and throughput pick up. All of this pushes
toward unbundling the code. If the interfaces and expectations are extracted from
code, if we can validate each commitment, then we don’t have to understand the
implementation.
The tools may not exist, but many of the ideas do exist. Most come from operations
and QA, two domains that software engineering has historically been rather snobbish
about. These tests and techniques are not about testing for correctness or what ought
to be happening. Rather, they are about observing and encoding what is happening:
behavioral tests, characterization tests, capture/replay, traffic splitters.
But you don’t need to wait for better tools to come. You can start today. Use instru‐
mentation to capture your intent, and then follow it out into production. Can you
validate it there? Can your coworker? Can an agent?
Arm yourself with observability tools that give you precision data—context and car‐
dinality—and a scalpel-like ability to identify the consequences of every change you
ship. Build the right seams to make your code composable, observable, upgradable,
maintainable. Become as fluent in the real world of production as you are in the
abstract world of stubbed-out tests.

Conclusion
The transition from treating code as a sacred relic to code as cache will be unavoid‐
ably messy. As we unbundle our expectations from the artifact and move toward
explicit specification, evaluation, and commitments, the traditional dev/ops divide
simply ceases to make sense.
Nostalgia aside, nobody misses the bad old days of handcrafted servers, Perl scripts,
and Snowflake cron jobs. Sedimentary layers of untangleable purpose and long-lost
intent create all the most frustrating parts about working on software.
No one is going to miss the bad old days of handcrafted code, capture/replay, and
strangler figs either. One day, we will reminisce about the worst software rewrites we
have been part of, and the kids will listen in dismay.
The tools for this future don’t yet fully exist, but the principles of observability are
already in your hands. It starts with understanding. It starts with observability in
production.
